Celtic Cross Tarot Spread: All 10 Positions Explained

Ten cards, one centre axis and a staff of four. The Celtic Cross is the most-used deep reading in tarot, and it is also the most frequently misremembered. Here is every position, what it actually answers, and how to read the pairs.

In short

  • The Celtic Cross is ten cards in a fixed shape: one significator, a staff of four, and a crossing card.
  • Positions one to four are the situation, five to eight are the deeper layers, nine is the final outcome.
  • Position six, the heart of the matter, is the card most readers over-look.

The ten positions

Shuffle, cut, and lay the cards in this order. Positions 1 to 3 form the horizontal arm, positions 1 to 6 the vertical staff.

  1. Present. The heart of the matter, the core of the situation right now. If one card could answer the question, this is it.
  2. Crossing. What cuts across the present: the obstacle, the tension, the thing working against you. Often internal.
  3. Foundation. The root, below the conscious surface. What is actually generating the situation.
  4. Recent past. The energy that is leaving, usually fading within days or weeks.
  5. Crown. The conscious goal, the conscious intention, what you are aiming at on purpose.
  6. Near future. The next development, arriving soon.
  7. Self. Your own attitude and role in the situation, as you are currently holding it.
  8. Environment. Other people, external influences, what everyone else brings to it.
  9. Hopes and fears. Often the same card, or a pair that contradicts itself. This position is where you are most likely to be honest with yourself.
  10. Outcome. Where this is heading if nothing changes. Conditional, not fixed.

How to read the pairs, and why it matters

Most bad Celtic Cross readings read ten cards in isolation. The spread is designed to be read in pairs, and that is where the structure lives.

  • 1 and 2 (Present and Crossing) is the central conflict. Present against obstacle. The tension of the situation in one line.
  • 3 and 4 (Foundation and Past) is the cause. What is buried under what just happened. Read 3 first, then 4 as its consequence.
  • 5 and 6 (Crown and Future) is the trajectory. Conscious intention meeting probable arrival.
  • 1 and 10 (Present and Outcome) is the whole reading in two cards: where you are versus where it ends. If these two are the same card, the situation is already resolved, in one direction or the other.
  • 7, 8 and 9 are the moving part: your attitude, the outside influence, and the hidden motive. Read as a conversation between three people.

The staff of four on the right is the staff in many traditional cartomancy layouts. It is read from the bottom up, 4 to 1, which is why it is sometimes called the ascending staff.

Before you start: the four questions that ruin the reading

  1. Asking a yes/no question and then reading all ten cards. A yes/no reading is one card. If you want depth, ask an open question.
  2. Laying it out with no question. The cards answer something. If you do not know what, the reading will feel unfocused and you will invent meaning to compensate.
  3. Reversals without a decision. Decide whether you are reading reversals before you shuffle, not while reading card seven.
  4. Interpreting position 10 as fate. It is a conditional outcome, not a sentence. It describes the path that is open while the present holds.

What each of the ten positions actually answers

  • The Celtic Cross is not ten separate cards read one after another. It is a diagram with three blocks, and the order of reading matters because each block answers a different level of the question.
  • The first block is the crossing: card one, the heart of the matter, crossed by card two, what crosses or challenges it. Read as a pair, these two alone usually tell you what is going on.
  • The staff is four cards going down. Card three sits above the crossing and describes the conscious basis, what you know and are working with. Card four goes below and describes the unconscious, the foundation under everything else. Together they are the vertical axis of the situation.
  • The staff of three is the horizontal axis. Card five is the conscious goal, what you are aiming at. Card six, to the right of the crossing, is the heart of the matter in its nearest form, the thing under your nose. Card seven is yourself, your attitude and your role. Card eight is the environment, what surrounds you.
  • Card nine, above the staff, is the final outcome. Card ten, to the right of the staff, is the culmination. Both describe where this ends up, and they sometimes disagree, which is itself information.

The five pairs that carry most of the reading

  • Reading ten cards in sequence tends to blur them. Reading five pairs keeps the structure, and each pair answers one specific question.
  • One and six together give the tension at the centre: what is happening, and what is closest to you. Seven and eight show the exchange between your attitude and your environment, which is where most of a reading actually resolves.
  • Two and seven, the crossing card against yourself, show the challenge in terms of your own position rather than the outside force. Three and eight, conscious basis against environment, show whether your understanding matches your circumstances. Five and ten, goal against culmination, show whether what you are aiming at is what you get.
  • Four and nine are the pair most often misread. The unconscious foundation compared with the final outcome tells you whether the change is going in the direction of something you have not acknowledged yet, or against it.

Getting a clean Celtic Cross reading

  • Deal freehand rather than from a machine shuffle if you can, but be consistent: if you always shuffle the same way, the reading stays honest. The important thing is not the method, it is that the method does not change between readings.
  • Reversals are optional and you should pick a convention and keep it. Traditional practice often reads the Celtic Cross with upright cards only, because ten reversed cards become unreadable. If you use reversals, record the convention in your journal so you read old spreads consistently.
  • Number the positions before you start, and note the card as you deal it rather than trying to remember. Ten cards is the point where memory starts failing, and a mislabelled position silently corrupts the whole spread.
  • Write one sentence per position as you go, not a full interpretation. Full interpretation belongs after the layout is complete and you can see the whole shape, which is when cross-position meaning becomes visible.

Celtic Cross questions

What does the Celtic Cross spread have 10 positions for?

Ten cards answer more than one question: the present situation, its obstacle, its root, its direction, your own role, outside forces, hidden motives and the likely outcome, all in one layout.

Which cards are read first in a Celtic Cross?

Read the pairs, not the cards in isolation. Positions one and two for the central conflict, three and four for the cause, five and six for the trajectory, and one against ten for the whole arc.

Do I need a physical deck to do a Celtic Cross?

No. The free spread builder on this site sets out the layout and keeps your notes next to each position, and there are free card reference tools for meanings.

What are the three cards in the middle of the Celtic Cross?

Position seven is the Self, position eight is the Environment and position nine is Hopes and Fears. Read them as a conversation between three parties.

Should I read reversed cards in a Celtic Cross?

Either way, but decide before you shuffle. Reversals add a layer of tension and are more useful for question-shaped readings than for yes/no ones.

Is the staff on the right read separately?

Yes. It is the staff of four from traditional cartomancy and is read from the bottom up, position four to position one.

Do I need an app to do a Celtic Cross reading?

No, but frequent readers usually want offline shuffling and a saved history. The Rider Waite Tarot app is $9.99 once and includes the Celtic Cross among six spreads.

How long should a Celtic Cross reading take?

Twenty to thirty minutes unhurried is enough for the pairs. The mistake is reading all ten cards in isolation, which doubles the length and loses the structure.

How long should a Celtic Cross reading take?

Long enough to note one sentence per position before interpreting any of them. That takes twenty minutes for a first spread and five once the positions are familiar. Reading ten cards in the moment is where layouts get corrupted.

Do the cards need to be reversed in a Celtic Cross?

Not necessarily. Many traditional readers use upright cards only for this spread, because ten reversed cards are hard to hold in mind. Either convention works as long as you record it and read your old spreads the same way.

Which position matters most?

Position six, the heart of the matter in its nearest form. It sits beside the crossing and is where most readers under-look, but it is usually the closest actionable description of the situation.

Can I use the Celtic Cross every day?

For deep questions, yes, but not for the small ones. This is a ten-card layout and it uses up attention; if you run it daily it stops being special and you start filling positions with what you expect instead of what is there.

What if the reading contradicts itself?

Then the contradiction is the reading. Cards nine and ten disagreeing, or the conscious basis failing to match the environment, tells you the situation has not settled yet. Write down both halves instead of picking the one you prefer.
