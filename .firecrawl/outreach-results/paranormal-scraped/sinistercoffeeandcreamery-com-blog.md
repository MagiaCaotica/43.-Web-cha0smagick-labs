4\. Visualize. Picture your intention as though it’s already yours.

## **Phantoms unpacked**

**_The word_** _phantom_ **_often illicits a wide range of emotions and conjecture. Is it a shadow that moves when there is no one there, the cold shiver that hits you in an empty hallway, or the quiet nagging suspicion that something you can not see is watching. Across centuries, people have described phantoms as experiences that exist just beyond the explainable. There is a fantastical element to them as how one percieves them can be fleeting, intangible, and yet undeniable in the moment._**

**_Phantoms can be used to describe more than just haunting dark spaces. In medical terms, phantom pain is the very real sensation felt in a lost limb—burning, aching, or tingling in the absence of something that was once a part of you. The phenomenon was named in the 1800s by Civil War surgeon Silas Weir Mitchell, who documented the lingering experience of pain in wounded soldiers._**

## _Origins of Phantom_

**_Phantom comes through Latin (phantasma) from the Greek phantasma (φάντασμα), meaning “an apparition, a vision, an image that appears without substance.” Both draw from the root verb phainein (φαίνειν): “to bring to light, to make visible.”_**

**_In Greek and Roman ideology, phantasma was something that deceived the senses: shadows, dreams, or illusions. In mythology, phantasmata were divine visions or apparitions sent by gods. One might say the name Phantom was created as an attempt to connect non-visible and visible experiences._**

**_Phantom, gives meaning to the unseeable yet deeply felt. It describes the space where spirit, sensation, and the supernatural converge._**

# **What Is a Ghost Light?**

If you’ve ever stepped foot inside a theatre after hours, your eyes may have been drawn to single, bare lightbulb standing alone in the center of the darkened stage. This is what’s called a **ghost light**, referring to a long-standing theater tradition that serves both practical and supernatural purposes.

# **The Practical Purpose: Safety First**

On a basic level, the ghost light prevents accidents. Theaters are full of hidden obstacles as they are constantly in a state of recreation. Depending on the play being recounted there could be open trapdoors, loose cables, furniture and lighting set onstage and of course uneven flooring. Leaving a light on ensures that anyone entering after hours won’t trip in the darkness.

# **The Superstition: A Light for the Spirits**

![](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/5c76de607fdcb8facd765433/7dfc168a-6022-4191-b4b6-5e9bd6afe4a5/94DFA851-4140-4626-AB1D-316687452B9A.png)

Beyond the scope of safety, the ghost light is at the heart of theatrical superstition. Many actors, theatre staff, and theatre enthusiasts believe that **every** theater has a ghost or two. The idea of who they may have been; former actors, stagehands, or patrons who never left, brings out as lively a conversation as one might imagine with a theatre as its backdrop. Some say the ghost light is intended keep spirits happy, giving them time to perform unseen. Others claim it wards off mischievous spirits who might otherwise cause havoc.

# **The Ghost Light in Folklore**

![](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/5c76de607fdcb8facd765433/3afa4493-b3a5-4366-8de2-55052785eb19/D386A2FF-9E75-4D2D-B4FA-69B45794C446.png)

Different theaters have their own versions of ghostly encounters. Some report flickering lights, strange sounds, or even full [apparitions](https://www.sinistercoffeeandcreamery.com/blog/apparitions). Nicknamed “the Bishop of Broadway”, David Belasco was well known and loved in the theater world. Shortly after his death, a spirit figure or apparition, was spotted in the theater and his spirit is felt there to this day. Whether these stories are real or the product of being alone in a space typically occupied by vibrant energy, they add to the mystique surrounding the ghost light’s purpose.

The ghost light is more than just a bulb on a stand—it’s a symbol of theater itself. Even during times of closure, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, theaters kept their ghost lights burning as a promise that the show would go on. ### The History of Scrying: From Ancient Divination to Paranormal Investigations

Scrying traces back thousands of years, with cultures across the globe incorporating some aspects of its value. Ancient Egyptians used bowls of water to communicate with the deities. In Greek tradition, peering into polished dark obsidian allowed one to receive messages from the Oracle. During the period of Spiritualism in England, a “magic mirror" was used almost like a portal, to converse with angels by a close advisor to Queen Elisabeth I. Although many mystics and spiritualists claims have been debunked over the years, the possiblility of insight, protection and guidance keeps people coming to the mirror for answers so to speak.

### **Pareidolia in Ghost Hunting: Fact or Fallacy?**

### **Faces in Clouds**

Where things aren’t always what they seem, kinda like the movie The Labyrinth-a cult classic for a reason! Is what we see, just the best approximation of what our brains can process? When confronted with ambiguous or unknown stimuli, our minds conjure the familiar. Faces in trees or the clouds are good examples of our mind putting together patterns in elements that either change or have structures already similar to things or people we’ve seen before.

### **Shroud of Turin image**

![](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/5c76de607fdcb8facd765433/f0a49c45-49ce-4b06-b8db-274baeb3f1c9/Screenshot+2024-05-15+at+9.44.44+AM.jpg)

One of the most well documented human relics which many experience pareidolia regarding is The Shroud of Turin. The Shroud of Turin, dates back to the 1300’s and is a linen cloth bearing an image or outline of a man. The authenticity of the cloth has sparked intense debate since its acquisition. While some believe it to be the burial shroud of Jesus Christ and the imprint of his body is revealed on the cloth, others argue that the image is a result of pareidolia, our mind perceiving facial features and details in the irregularities of the fabric's weave. ### **The Science of Pareidolia**

![](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/5c76de607fdcb8facd765433/31214bc8-eb43-45c8-aca9-7e9b9d8574e5/IMG_5144+%282%29.jpg)

credit VikiWi - Pixabay

According to a [Psychology Today article](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-gravity-of-weight/202306/on-the-face-of-it-pareidolia), the human brain is set up to recognize facial features because of the importance expressions have for human interaction. For the human brain to perceive and interpret facial features in any human or non-human form, several different parts of the brain are triggered and work together. Some may even say, as humans we are hard wired to put together noticeable patterns in seemingly unrelated things to try to make snap decisions and process complex data.

### **Pareidolia in ghost hunting**

![](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/5c76de607fdcb8facd765433/ed943c1a-03f3-4382-b1ad-bec1c1341f54/nature-1902404_1280.jpg)

credit Tombud - Pixabay

When it comes to paranormal encounters, it is important to consider pareidolia and its influence on how we experience our world. If it is normal for us to try to create easily identifiable patterns out of what we see, then one could assume pretty quickly how that may influence a paranormal investigation or even an experience it feels like we are having. In the dark of night, where our vision is far less precise, our eyes may perceive danger where there is none relying on a fight or flight response that is an important part of human biology. Proper debunking, and sharing of data across the paranormal community is a necessity for legit ghost hunters many perhaps because of pareidolia and how it may manifest in images, or across our visual spectrum during investigations of haunted locations. This blog focused solely on visual pareidolia, however auditory pareidolia is also an interesting topic worthy of conversation on a different day.
