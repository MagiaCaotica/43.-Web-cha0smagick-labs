What is a sigil?

A sigil is a symbol created from a written intention. It compresses a desire into a visual form that bypasses the conscious mind and speaks to the subconscious. The technique was formalized by Austin Osman Spare in the early 1900s, but the principle — symbol → subconscious → manifestation — appears in every magical tradition.

Sigils are the most accessible form of magic: no tools, no rituals, no pantheon. Just intention, a symbol, and a moment of focused will.

How to create a sigil (step by step)

  1. Write your intention as a single sentence in the present tense: "I AM CONFIDENT IN SOCIAL SITUATIONS."
  2. Remove duplicate letters — keep only the first occurrence of each letter.
  3. Arrange the remaining letters into a unique, abstract symbol. There are no rules — let your hand move freely.
  4. Simplify — remove unnecessary lines, merge shapes, make it clean.
  5. Charge it (see below).
  6. Forget it. The sigil works while you are not thinking about it.

How to charge a sigil (gnosis)

Charging means projecting your intention into the sigil with enough emotional energy that it bypasses the conscious filter. Two methods:

  • Inhibitory gnosis: deep meditation, sensory deprivation, exhaustion. The mind goes blank; you project the sigil into the void.
  • Excitatory gnosis: intense emotion, physical exertion, sensory overload. The mind is flooded; you project the sigil through the noise.

Practical charging methods: orgasm, exhaustion, breathwork, cold exposure, dancing, drumming. Choose what fits your temperament. The key is reaching a state where the conscious mind is temporarily disabled.

The 3 rules of sigil magic

  1. One intention per sigil. "I want money AND love AND health" produces a confused symbol. Pick one, sharpen it, charge it.
  2. Forget it after charging. The sigil works while you are not thinking about it. Obsessing over the outcome is the #1 reason sigils fail.
  3. Charge with genuine emotion. A sigil charged while bored does nothing. The energy must come from real desire.

Common mistakes

  • Too many sigils at once: charging 5 sigils in one session dilutes the energy. One at a time.
  • Not forgetting: checking "did it work?" every day kills the sigil. Put it away and move on.
  • Vague intentions: "I want to be happy" is too broad. "I feel confident presenting to my team on Friday" is specific and actionable.
  • No feedback loop: record results. Use the sigil intention encoder to track outcomes.

Sigil magic for money, love, protection and more

Sigils can be used for any intention: wealth, love, health, protection, creativity, academic success, career advancement. The method is the same; only the intention changes. The sigil intention encoder helps you formulate precise intentions for any goal.

For money sigils specifically: be specific about the amount and the source. "I receive $500 from an unexpected source" works better than "I am rich."

Where sigils come from: seals, Spare and the alphabet of desire

The word comes from the Latin sigillum, a small seal. In the medieval grimoiric tradition a seal was not decoration: it was the signature of a spirit, a compressed name that gave the operator a handle on a force. The Picatrix (the 11th-century Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm, translated by Greer and Warnock in 2011) and Agrippa's Three Books of Occult Philosophy (1531–1533) both treat names, letters and figures as interchangeable: compress the name correctly and you hold the thing.

The modern sigil is a deliberate break with that tradition. In The Book of Pleasure (Self-Love): The Psychology of Ecstasy (1913), Austin Osman Spare kept the compression and threw away the ceremony. No circle, no dagger: write the desire, remove the repeated letters, and draw what is left into a single glyph. Spare called the result a sigil and the alphabet he built from it an alphabet of desire. His cosmology, the Zos Kia Cultus, treated the body (zos) and the absolute (kia) as the only real materials — everything else, including the gods, as furniture of the mind.

Spare died in 1956 with a small cult following. The technique survived because it was cheap, private and testable. In the late 1970s Ray Sherwin published The Book of Results (1978) and Peter J. Carroll published Liber Null (1978); between them they turned a painter's private method into a reproducible practice and, with the Pact of the Illuminates of Thanateros, into an order. Carroll's Psychonaut (1987) and Liber Kaos (1992) added the vocabulary — gnosis, paradigm shifting, belief as a tool — that most guides still use. Phil Hine's Condensed Chaos (1995) and Jan Fries's Visual Magick (1998) made the practice accessible to people with no order and no teacher, and Grant Morrison's essay "Pop Magick!" (1991) carried it into comics and pop culture.

Why the reduction matters

The letter-reduction step works as an amnesia device. A sentence you can read keeps the desire in the same part of the mind that keeps the lack. A glyph you cannot read does not. That is why the classical instructions insist on removing repeated letters, mixing what remains, and drawing the result until it stops looking like writing. Some schools drop vowels, some keep them; some rotate the glyph 90 degrees to break the reading habit. The variants matter less than the effect: the finished sigil should be unrecognisable to the part of you that worries.

There are four common families. Mantrical sigils reduce the sentence to a syllable string you chant until it loses meaning. Pictorial sigils drop letters entirely and draw the desire as an image. Robotic sigils are written with the hand while the attention is elsewhere, so the conscious mind never reads them. Combined sigils stack two or three of these. All four end in the same place: a form with no verbal handle.

Gnosis: the state, not the drama

Carroll's useful contribution was to describe gnosis as a physiological state rather than a mystical one: a narrow band of attention where the internal monologue goes quiet. It can be reached from below (inhibitory) or above (excitatory). Inhibitory methods are stillness, breath counting, sensory deprivation, the death posture Spare borrowed from yoga. Excitatory methods are drumming, dancing, hyperventilation, pain, sexual climax. The sigil is charged at the peak — the moment the state is about to break — and then deliberately abandoned.

The abandonment is the part beginners skip. Staring at the sigil, checking whether it "worked", and recharging it every night is the opposite of the instruction: it reinstates the conscious monitoring the glyph was built to bypass. The traditional formula is fire and forget. In practice that means: charge once, destroy or file the sigil, and do not think about it again until the result arrives or the experiment ends.

What can honestly be claimed

No controlled study shows that sigils change external events. The evidence that does exist is about attention, ritual and behaviour: a private ritual can focus intention, reduce rumination, and make a course of action feel permitted — and those effects are real even when the metaphysical claim is not. The honest test is a personal log: date, intention, method, and what happened in the following 90 days, written before you know the outcome. That is also the only method that protects you from remembering the hits and forgetting the misses.

The 1985 Carlson double-blind test published in Nature did not test sigils, but it is the reference point for why claims about astrological or magical causation need controls. A sigil practice that refuses to keep records is not more spiritual than one that does; it is just unfalsifiable.

Ethics and the failure modes

Three failure modes account for most bad experiences. Targeting: a sigil aimed at another person's will turns a private practice into coercion, and the tradition's own literature treats it as both unethical and unreliable. Rumination: charging a sigil for a lack you think about all day is not magic, it is rehearsal of the lack. Substitution: using sigils instead of the practical action — the application, the call, the saving — that the desire actually requires. Spare's own writing is blunt that the sigil is a tool for the mind, not a replacement for the world.

A 30-day practice that produces data

  1. Days 1–7: one sigil per day, same method, written and drawn by hand. Keep them in one notebook. No results expected yet.
  2. Days 8–14: charge one sigil every other day, using a single gnosis method. Record the method and the time.
  3. Days 15–21: stop charging. This is the forgetting period. Note anything that happens without interpreting it.
  4. Days 22–30: review the notebook against what actually happened. Count the misses, not only the hits. Then change one variable — method, timing, phrasing — and run it again.
