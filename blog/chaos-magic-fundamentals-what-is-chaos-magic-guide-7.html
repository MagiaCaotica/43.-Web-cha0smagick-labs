Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Almost every account of starting chaos magic gets the first month wrong in the same way. It presents the material as a body of knowledge to be acquired before action is possible, so the reader spends weeks reading about sigilisation, then weeks reading about intention, and arrives at the first working with a pile of concepts and no practice behind them. This article takes the opposite route and describes the month as a sequence of days rather than a body of theory, on the assumption that you can do the first thing badly today and improve it by the fourth week. The movement emerged in the early 1970s, with the 1976 founding of Ordo Templi Ouroborus as the conventional starting point, and its founding texts insisted on experiment over doctrine. Day one, throw away the reading list

The most useful thing a beginner can do is postpone reading. Not permanently. For two weeks, so the practice can exist before the theory does, because the theory will be absorbed differently once you have something of your own to attach it to. Everything you read in the meantime will be evaluated against a real experience rather than against your imagination of one.

The founding texts are short and cheap and one of them is genuinely an entry point, but they read very differently in week four than in week one. Peter Carroll's Liber Null and psi and John Crowley's Liber Kaos are the two most widely used, and both of them assume you are already going to experiment. Reading them first is like reading a manual on a bicycle.

Day two, write one sentence

Not a goal. A sentence. A specific thing you want, phrased as something you will do rather than something you will have. The distinction sounds pedantic and it is the whole technique, because the reduction step only works on a statement and a statement needs a subject and a verb.

If you cannot write it, that is useful information rather than a setback, and the usual reason is that the target is a state rather than a task. Feeling more confident is not a sentence. Having sent the invoice is a sentence. The sentence takes about four attempts for most people, and the fifth one is usually the first thing that is actually about you rather than about a version of you that might come into being.

Day three, reduce it by hand

Take the sentence, drop the vowels, and collapse what is left into a shape. It will look bad. That is genuinely fine and it is the most common reason people abandon the method in the first week, having concluded that they are not artistic enough.

The reduction method is traceable to Austin Osman Spare, writing around 1904, and it spread through what followed him rather than through any formal teaching, which means there is no correct version to get wrong. The circle for unity, the line for intent, the cross at the centre are conventional, and the meanings attributed to them vary by source. A rough glyph made from your own sentence on day three is worth more than a beautiful one generated by an app you have not understood yet.

Day four, charge it badly

A charge is a short deliberate act of attention on the glyph. Traditionally it involves forgetting the shape at the moment of dismissing it, and this stage is the one everyone skips because forgetting feels like a failure rather than a step.

Practise it once with something disposable. Light a match, hold it near the drawing, let the attention settle for a few seconds, blow it out, and then genuinely stop thinking about the shape for the rest of the day. You will fail at this. Failing at it is the normal first result, and the reason the practice asks for it is that sustaining the shape after the working is what most people do instead of the working itself.

Day five, write down one prediction

Choose something the sentence will actually affect, name a date, and write it down. This is the step that separates practice from habit, and it is the step nobody does on day five because it is the only step that involves the possibility of being wrong.

The reason for doing it this early rather than later is that the movement's most durable contribution is a method rather than a metaphysics. It made it acceptable to test claims rather than inherit them, and that contribution is unavailable to you unless you register a prediction in the first week. A month of workings with no registered outcome is a month of anecdotes, and anecdotes will be reinterpreted for as long as you have them.

Week two, notice what is actually flexible

The first week assumes the method is a set of rules. It is not. Almost every element is a convention, and the conventions were invented by a small number of people in the 1970s who were making a point about permissiveness as much as about technique. The statement of intent matters more than the sigil, and the sigil matters more than the charging.

This realisation is liberating and it is also where people go wrong by concluding that nothing matters. There is a difference between conventions that are load-bearing and conventions that are inherited. A private hour and a specific sentence are load-bearing. Whether the glyph is drawn in a particular style is inherited, and you will not get better results by getting it more correct.

  1. Load-bearing: a specific statement, a defined action, a date, a record.
  2. Load-bearing: attention during the charge, and a real dismissal afterwards.
  3. Inherited: the drawing style, the colour, the day of the week, the planetary timing.
  4. Inherited: almost everything sold as a requirement by a course.

Week three, add exactly one thing

The temptation at this point is to buy something. A correspondence book, a deck, an app, a course. Resist it for one more week and instead add one element that costs nothing and that you can evaluate: a second working on a different target, using the same method.

This is how you find out whether the method is doing anything or whether the first working was a coincidence you have already built a story around. Two data points is not a study. It is enough to notice that the second one felt different, and the difference is usually information about the method rather than about the world.

Week four, review before you add anything else

Read your own entries. Ask what actually happened, what you supplied, and whether the entry would exist if the week had gone badly. Most beginners find one or two entries that were written in a way that only makes sense now that the outcome is known, and noticing your own handwriting from the inside is a genuinely disorienting experience.

Then decide what to do in month two. The two defensible options are to keep the method exactly as it is and run it on a third and fourth target, or to change one element and see what happens. Adding three elements at once is the standard mistake, and it is fatal, because you will have changed the practice and learned nothing at all about which change mattered.

What belief is or is not required for

None of the above requires you to believe in magic, and this is not a rhetorical point. The methods were designed to be run by a sceptic, precisely so that the results would be harder to dismiss. A practitioner who is willing to keep a record that might disconfirm them has produced better evidence than one who is certain and never checked.

The framework does not ask you to hold a belief about mechanism. The name plays on chaos magic and on the Chaos enthroned motif of The Book of the Law, taken as a deliberate affiliation, and the affiliation is the one genuinely optional part. You can run the whole sequence, keep the results, and conclude that the sigil did nothing. Nothing in the procedure breaks.

The three risks that are real

Fixation is the first. A practice that produces ambiguous results and rewards attention can quietly become the centre of a life, and the people most at risk are the ones whose sigil worked once. Isolation is the second, because the material is a strong identity and identity is easiest to build alone. Sleep disruption is the third, and it is the one that most often gets misread as progress.

The mitigation for all three is the same, and it is boring: short daily practice rather than escalating intensity, keep other commitments, and treat any sign that the practice is interfering with sleep, work, or relationships as a reason to reduce rather than a reason to push harder. Nothing in the tradition requires the intensity, and the practice that survives a year is the one that never escalated.

  • Fixation, which looks like dedication and behaves like a compulsion.
  • Isolation, which is easy to build and hard to notice while it builds.
  • Sleep disruption, which most often gets misread as significance.
  • Reduce intensity rather than escalating, and keep your other commitments.

What to do in month two, and what to read

Run the same method on a third target, in the same shape, and keep the records. Then read one primary text and one history. The history matters more than the technique manual, because the technique is already in your hands and the history is what tells you which parts of the tradition are load-bearing.

If you want an entry point, the short 1977 text is still the most widely used for the reason that it is short, cheap, and written by someone who expected you to disagree with him. Read it after the month of practice, not before. Everything else can wait, and a great deal of what is sold at this stage is better left unbought until you know what you are actually missing.

The first month's mistakes, named in advance

There are four, and they are predictable enough to write down now so that you can recognise them. The first is buying equipment instead of writing sentences. The second is reading instead of working. The third is changing the method mid-month, usually because a working produced nothing, which guarantees you learn nothing from either version.

The fourth is the most expensive and the least visible. It is deciding in advance that the first month will confirm something, and then arranging the record to do that. Nobody consciously does this and everybody does it, because the alternative is finding out that a method you have spent four weeks on may be doing nothing. Write the prediction on day five, before you have invested anything, and then treat whatever arrives as the result.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do I actually need to start doing this?

A statement of intent in one imperative sentence, a reduction of it to a glyph, and a private place and half hour to charge it. That is the entire toolkit, and it has been sufficient since the 1970s. Everything else people buy in their first month is optional, and a few of the most expensive options are sold to people who have not yet written their first sentence.

Is chaos magic dangerous to practise?

Not inherently. The documented risks are fixation, isolation and sleep disruption, and all three come from how intensely you run it rather than from the practice itself. Short daily work, keeping your other commitments, and reducing intensity the moment it starts interfering with sleep, work or relationships covers essentially all of it.

How much of this depends on what I actually think is true?

No, and the method was built so that you would not. The procedure requires a specific statement, a defined set of actions, and a record, and none of those depend on your view about reality. If you keep a prediction with a date and it fails, the method has still worked as a method, and you have learned more than most people who are certain.

Is it worth paying for a course or an app in the first month?

Not in the first month, and probably not in the second. Buy a notebook if you do not have one and run a month of practice so you know what you are missing. After that, a one-time purchase of a sigil generator or a reference book is reasonable, and a subscription is a poor fit for a method whose value lives in a record you own and a sigil you made yourself.

What if nothing happens in the first month?

Then you have run an experiment properly and got a null result, which is a real outcome and worth writing down as one. The most likely explanation is that the sentence was about a state rather than a task, which is worth testing directly by running a third working on something with an obvious action attached. A month of no result is not a reason to buy a more powerful method.
