Supernatural Powers: What the List Is Actually Made Of

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 11 min read

Every list of powers you can obtain with chaos magic sorts into four groups, and they are not equally real. Some entries are capacities you already have half of and can train the rest of. Some are ordinary effects that work because people respond to what you do. Some would be measurable and have never been measured. And some cannot be falsified, which means a successful outcome confirms them and an unsuccessful one is explained away, so they carry no information at all. The whole genre is easier to read once you know which group an entry belongs to, and the disappointing part is that the group people most want is the one that is empty.

Power is the wrong word, and that is why the lists read as nonsense

A power is something you can do. Telepathy is a power, in the sense that either you can read an unspoken thought or you cannot. Walking is a power. A capacity, a trained skill, something that is either available to you or not.

An effect is something that happens to another person or to a situation. Making someone love you, winning the lottery, a stranger helping you, a door opening. These are outcomes, and they depend on other people, on chance, and on the world, none of which you control.

The popular lists mix the two freely and then promise them as a package. That is not only a marketing problem, it is a reasoning problem. A person who has spent six months training a genuine capacity reads a list where it sits next to an outcome and concludes that six months of real work should have produced the outcome too, and that something is wrong with them when it has not.

Sorting the two apart is the whole of what follows, and once it is done the list stops sounding like a promise and starts sounding like a curriculum.

Group one: capacities, which are real, trainable, and yours

These have a clean property: you can tell whether you have them, and you can usually tell whether they improved.

Holding a narrow attention for a defined interval. This is the foundational one and the least glamorous. It is a trainable capacity with an obvious baseline, which is why almost every serious tradition in any field begins here and why almost none of the popular material mentions it.

Noticing a pattern before it becomes obvious. Not clairvoyance in any interesting sense. The trained version is holding a wider field of view while making a narrow judgement, and it improves with a scored instrument you cannot influence.

Recovering from a state you would rather not be in. Emotional regulation, attention recovery, the ability to come back from distraction. Measurable by how long it takes, which is a real number that changes.

Writing an intention in a form that either happened or did not. This is a writing capacity, and it is the single item on the list that changes outcomes more than anything else, and nothing supernatural is required to get it.

Keeping a record you do not edit afterwards. Almost nobody can do this and it is trainable in about three weeks.

Notice that nothing in this group is for sale. Every capacity on it can be developed with a notebook, ten minutes a day, and a source of genuine randomness you cannot predict. The absence of a product here is not an oversight by the market.

Group two: ordinary effects that work for dull reasons

These are reliable and the explanation is not magical. They are on the list because they work, and the genre is under no obligation to be precise about why.

People respond to what you do. If you are reliably more present, more specific, or more reliable, people will act differently toward you. This accounts for a large share of the testimonials in this field and it will account for most of yours.

A dated commitment changes behaviour. Writing a review date in advance and deciding what would count as failure does not cause anything, and it reliably produces follow-through you would not otherwise manage. The deadline is doing the work, and the ritual around it is a container.

Writing the decision down sharpens the decision. Compression helps. The same intention stated in a sentence you would be embarrassed to show a stranger is usually a different intention from the one you had been carrying.

Attention spent on a problem improves work on the problem. This is not mysticism, it is what attention is for, and the genre dresses it up because the plain version does not sell anything.

None of these are fake. They are just not powers. They are what happens when a person takes an ordinary capacity seriously and files the result under a heading that flatters it.

Group three: measurable claims that have not been measured

This group is small and it is not necessarily false. It is untested, and the distinction matters because a lot of the field is quietly moved between group three and group four depending on the person doing the moving.

Telepathy is the canonical case. It has a defined test, the random is genuinely random, the scoring is straightforward, and results are mixed with an effect size near zero under controlled conditions. The genre rarely discusses the controlled conditions.

Precognition would be the strongest member of this group if anyone ran a proper version. It has not been, and the reason is structural rather than cowardice: a precognition test that scores a hit after the fact is a memory test, and separating the two requires a protocol nobody in this genre has an interest in designing.

Remote viewing is complicated by the fact that the target sites are heavily public, so a remote viewer can reconstruct a known image from the description. This does not make the effect impossible, it makes the popular evidence worthless, and the fix is boring and available to anyone willing to do it.

The thing to notice about this group is that a member moves out of it the moment somebody runs a proper test with pre-registered expectations. That has happened several times in adjacent fields. It has not happened in this one, and the absence of anyone trying is itself information about the claims.

Group four: statements that cannot be wrong

These are the entries the genre is built on, and they are recognisable by shape rather than by content.

A claim is unfalsifiable when a good outcome confirms it and no outcome disconfirms it. The classic form: if it works you will know it worked, and if it does not work you will know you were not in the right state. Every possible outcome is assigned in advance, so the claim has no failure condition.

The tell is the state clause. Any claim that can be rescued by asserting you were not focused enough, or not committed enough, or not on the right day, has been built to be unrescuable, because a statement about outcomes that always includes an escape hatch is not a statement about outcomes.

The damage is specific. A practitioner who works only with unfalsifiable claims cannot tell a working method from a non-working one, so their practice cannot improve, and the belief that it is improving is protected by the same clause that prevents it from being checked.

This is also why the honest entries on the list are the unpopular ones. A capacity has a baseline, a method, and a rate. An outcome does not. The genre leads with the outcomes because outcomes are what people want, not because they are what is on offer.

The four questions, applied to any listed power

Does it require an agent besides me? If something else has to decide to cooperate, that is an effect and not a power, no matter how it is described. Most of the impressive entries on these lists fail this question immediately.

Can somebody who is not me observe whether it happened? If the only possible witness is you, the item is in group four regardless of how carefully it is phrased.

Does it survive if I stop believing in it? Practically: write the expectation down, set the review date, and put the sigil away for the whole interval. An item that only works while you are holding it in mind was never an item, it was an attention span with a name.

Is the belief doing the work or is the method doing the work? The honest way to find out is to run the ordinary version with no ritual at all, on something small, with the review date written in advance. If that produces a result, you have learned which part of the practice was load-bearing, which is the single most useful thing in this entire field and costs nothing.

If a listed power fails the first question and passes the others, it is not a power, it is a technique, and it is still worth learning. The reclassification is not a demotion.

What is actually available, without the padding

A sharper attention with a number attached to it. Start from a baseline you can measure this week, train against a source of randomness you cannot predict, and write the score down.

A written decision with a date on it. The single highest-return item on this list, and it takes about four minutes.

A method you can repeat. Not a technique that works, a procedure that can be run again with the same steps, which is a different and much more valuable thing.

A defensible position on a claim anyone can make. Being able to say what would show you were wrong, and what you would do, is a capacity and almost nobody teaches it.

A body of dated cases. Six months of a small function, a review date, and a written count. This is the thing that distinguishes a practice from a mood, and it is the reason the log exists in a tradition with no initiation.

That is the honest list. Every item is free, every item takes weeks rather than days, and none of them is what the videos lead with.

The failure that looks like progress

Collecting methods without running any. It has a recognisable shape: the person reads about a technique, understands it well enough to describe it accurately, feels something on first contact, and moves on. The understanding is genuine and it gets filed as the outcome.

The reason it feels like progress is that it uses the same faculty as the thing being sought. Studying concentration feels like concentrating. Understanding an outcome in detail feels like having caused it. This is why the reading-then-doing gap is the dominant failure in every practical tradition rather than a special problem of this one.

The diagnostic is cheap. Count the things you have actually run, with a date and an outcome, in the last ninety days. Most people who have been reading seriously for a year can produce a very small number, and the number is the real measure of the practice. A log of successes is a mood; a log with a fixed review date is a record.

The fix is also cheap and it is the same as for every other technique in the field: one small working, a written observation, a fixed review date, and one acceptable conclusion written before you start.

Five ways people read this correctly and then get it wrong anyway

Sorting the list correctly and still wanting the effects. Knowing an item is an effect and continuing to work on it, because the wanting does not switch off when the classification does. This is the normal outcome and the honest response is to run the capacity work anyway, since it is the part that pays.

Treating the deflationary reading as permission to do nothing. The sort is descriptive, not a licence. Every item in the first group is available today and requires no belief at all.

Confusing a small reliable capacity with an unimportant one. The whole list in group one is what every serious discipline in any field is built from, and none of it is flashy.

Using a belief to avoid a measurement. A framework that cannot be counted gets defended rather than tested, and the defence is usually the state clause.

Buying tools instead of running the four questions. The questions are free and take a minute. The most common real expenditure in this area is a subscription that organises work you were not doing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you really obtain supernatural powers with chaos magic?

You can obtain a specific and countable set of things, and the honest list is not the one that gets shared. A sharper attention with a measurable baseline, a written decision with a date attached, a method you can repeat, a defensible position on any claim, and a body of dated cases. Those are all real and all trainable. Whether anything supernatural is involved is a separate question, and the two lists are usually conflated. The one thing that changes outcomes most reliably is the written decision, which requires no belief whatsoever.

Does sigil magic actually work?

The sigil is a specification. It compresses a decision into a handle and it makes the intention hard to renegotiate later, both of which are real and both of which survive a deflationary account of the practice completely. What it does not do is add force the decision did not have. A sigil for an intention you have not actually settled will encode the unsettled version, and the popular material is notably quiet about that. The test is whether you wrote the aim as something that either happened or did not, before you drew anything.

What is the first thing a beginner should develop?

The ability to hold a narrow attention for a defined interval, and the ability to keep a record you do not edit afterwards. Neither is dramatic, both are trainable, and everything else on the list is easier once these two exist. A scored deck run properly gives you a baseline in a week, and a five-column log gives you the record. The temptation is to start with the outcome you want, and that is the one thing that reliably makes the practice worse rather than better.

How do I know if I am fooling myself?

Run the ordinary version with no ritual at all, on something small and unimportant, with the review date and the acceptable conclusion written in advance. If the plain version produces a result, you have learned which part of the practice was doing the work, which is the most useful single experiment available in this field. Then apply the four questions to any claim you find yourself drawn to: does it need another agent, can anyone else observe it, does it survive putting the sigil away, and is the belief or the method carrying it.

Is it worth paying for a sigil generator or a chaos magic app?

The mechanics are free and the tools on this site already cover them, so a paid app is buying a schedule, a log, and a library rather than a method. The most common real failure in this area is the subscription pattern, where a monthly bill converts a practice into a recurring decision you keep re-making, which is the opposite of what a commitment device is for. If you want a sigil tool, a scoring practice, and a log in one place with no recurring cost, a one-time purchase fits this work better than anything that bills monthly.
