Horror Fiction and Weird Cosmology

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 13 min read

H. P. Lovecraft built a fictional cosmology that other writers kept extending, and it now circulates through occult practice as if it were a body of doctrine somebody inherited. It is not. The mythos is a literary invention with a documented genealogy, a handful of authors, and a specific set of preoccupations that have very little to do with anything you would find in a grimoire. What makes it useful is narrower and stranger than the fantasy: it supplies a vocabulary for the feeling that the world is small, late and indifferent, and that vocabulary is genuinely hard to get anywhere else. This is written for the practitioner who has met the imagery in a song title or an album cover and wants to know what, if anything, is being quoted. The short answer is that almost nothing transfers except the imagery, and the imagery is worth having on its own terms. Read it critically, borrow the atmosphere, and leave the cosmology on the shelf where a novelist left it.

What the weird cosmology is actually made of

The mythos is not a system of theology. Lovecraft wrote horror stories, and the shared elements across them amount to a recurring set of narrative furniture: indifferent non-human intelligences, a cosmic indifference that makes human concern laughable, forbidden books that destroy the reader, and small towns whose geography hides something. Later writers added to it. Clark Ashton Smith, Robert E. Howard and August Derleth each extended it, and Derleth in particular popularised the framing that turned a set of stories into a mythos proper. The furniture is excellent material for atmosphere. It is not a ritual system.

It is worth being precise about that, because the online material about this is mostly wrong in a specific way. It presents the mythos as an ancient tradition that Lovecraft recovered. He did not recover anything. He assembled it from a wide reading of gothic literature, colonial anxiety, contemporary physics and a genuinely unusual amount of racial and political fear. The genealogy is literary and modern. Treating it as ancient is the single most common error in the material.

Why the 1928 essay matters more than the stories

Supernatural Horror in Literature is short, dated 1928, and it is Lovecraft's own attempt to explain what the weird tale is for. He argues that the atmosphere is the point: that the reader is meant to feel something described with precision, and that the terror comes from the description rather than from what it supposes is out there. The essay contains almost nothing about what he calls the mythos. That is useful, because it means the essay can be read without adopting the politics.

Read it as a piece of craft advice rather than as a grimoire chapter. It will tell you how to write dread, which is a real skill and one you can use to describe your own unease far more precisely than the usual vocabulary allows. It will not tell you what to do on a Tuesday afternoon. Nothing in the essay prescribes a method, a date, or a correspondence, and the absence is not an oversight in the sources that fill it in later.

Reading him without inheriting his politics

His racial views are explicit and repeated in his private correspondence, and they are ugly in the ordinary, specific way rather than the exotic way. A practitioner can read him, enjoy him, and take nothing from that dimension at all. The mechanism is simple: notice the views, decide they are not part of the work, and do not launder them by pretending the fictional universe implies otherwise. The fiction is a separate artefact from the man, and criticism has spent a century making that separation precise.

The temptation to skip this is how people end up defending something they would not defend elsewhere. If you find yourself explaining away a paragraph because you like the atmosphere, that is the paragraph to stop explaining away. The atmosphere survives perfectly well without it. None of this requires you to discard the work, and the people who tell you that enjoying Lovecraft commits you to something are selling a position rather than making an argument.

Where the vocabulary leaked into practice

Almost entirely through music, art and the internet rather than through grimoires. Album covers, band names, tattoo work, game art, and a long-running internet subculture have taken the specific words and the specific shapes and moved them into a register where they read as occult rather than literary. The transferred items are almost all visual or verbal: particular glyphs, particular names, particular feelings of scale. The non-human intelligences have not transferred as anything you can work with, because the texts never gave them a domain, a rank, a seal or a set of powers.

This is worth naming because the absence is informative. Every working entity in the Western grimoire tradition has a jurisdiction. Lovecraft's have atmospheres. You cannot build a sigil for an atmosphere. If you find occult material that claims to give you a Lovecraftian entity with a rank and a domain, that is a modern construction sitting on top of a novelist, and the source will not survive being checked. The good news is that the atmosphere is the part that does the work in practice anyway.

A first contact that costs nothing

If you want to work with this material, the first step is reading and nothing else. Pick three stories, spread across the period rather than clustered in his peak, and read them without a screen. Then read the 1928 essay, which is short enough that finishing it is a reasonable commitment. After that, write down in one sentence what the atmosphere made you feel, in concrete physical terms rather than in cosmic ones. A sentence about the smallness of a person against a structure they cannot perceive is a working sentence. A sentence about feeling insignificant is not, because it says nothing you could act on.

You now have the only piece of the mythos that transfers, and you do not need anything further to work with it. If you want to make it concrete, write that sentence out longhand, which turns an atmosphere into a physical object you can return to. If you want a second layer, the deliberate use of difficulty is the part most worth stealing: reading something that resists comprehension is a reliable way to model the feeling of working on something larger than your instruments. That is a real technique and it has nothing to do with Cthulhu.

The four things that transfer and the three that do not

The transferable set is small and specific: the vocabulary of scale, the technique of describing dread physically, the aesthetic of forbidden information, and the habit of taking a local thing seriously by grounding it in a much larger one. Each of those is usable inside a practice without any supernatural commitment. The non-transferable set is equally specific: named entities with jurisdictions, any claim about a real cosmos behind the fiction, and any procedure for contacting what the stories describe. None of those exist in the source, and the sources that appear to supply them are writing from a different tradition entirely.

This distinction is the most useful single thing to take from the material, and it generalises well beyond it. Most folk magic and modern occult writing follows the same pattern: a cultural artefact with real psychological content and no operative content, wrapped in later material that supplies the operative content and inherits none of the authority. Lovecraft is an unusually clear case because his influence is so recent that the seam is easy to see. In older material the seam is hidden by time, which is not the same as absent.

Using the imagery without feeding the fear

There is a specific risk here that is easy to state. Cosmic indifference is a feeling, and feelings can be rehearsed. Someone who spends time with material built entirely on smallness and insignificance is rehearsing smallness and insignificance, and the rehearsal does not stop at the level of imagination. This is why the recommendation in most of the practical literature is to take the aesthetics and drop the cosmology, and it is worth adding a second recommendation: take them in daylight, in company, and in bounded sessions.

A short deliberate exposure is a technique. An open-ended evening with the lights off is a different activity with different consequences, and the two are constantly confused. If you find that engaging with this material makes you check on things, that you are reading it to be frightened rather than to be precise, or that the reading has migrated from the page into the night, stop and note the date. The material is not going anywhere. The effect you are having on yourself is entirely yours and can be managed like any other.

  • Read in daylight, in a lit room, with other people in the house
  • Keep the sessions bounded, and write the end time in advance
  • Treat the atmosphere as a writing problem, not a contact problem
  • Stop the material the moment it starts managing your expectations

What to do with the dream content

Plenty of this material is a dream report rather than a text, and dream reports need a different handling. The tradition is right about the mechanics and vague about the interpretation. Recalling a dream immediately on waking preserves it far better than reconstructing it later, and nothing else in the sequence matters as much. Write what you recall while it is still present, in words, before you interpret anything. The interpretation can come an hour later and will be better for the delay.

What you do with the content is where the risks accumulate. A large dark entity in a dream is a common experience and a common image, and it usually means nothing beyond the fact that large dark entities are a common image. Treating it as a message requiring action is how a person ends up spending a month responding to a dream. Keep a dream log, keep it boring, and look at three months of entries before drawing any conclusion from a single night. If the logged material is a source of persistent dread rather than material, that is a different situation and it needs a different response than interpretation.

The images are not the cosmology

The most common practical error in this area is treating a picture of something immense as a claim about something immense. The image does what images do. It organises feeling, and it does that well, and that is the whole of what it is doing. Once you can say that without feeling that you have explained the image away, the material becomes usable. A sigil built to hold a feeling of scale has as much practical content as a sigil built to hold a request for money, and arguably more, because the request for money arrives pre-loaded with expectations you did not choose.

The practical consequence is a shift in what you charge the image with. Do not charge it with fear, which is what the cosmology wants. Charge it with precision: the specific sensation of being one small thing inside a large structure, stated as a physical experience in your own body. That sensation is genuinely useful. It is the felt form of humility, and humility is a real correction to the failure mode where a practice becomes a way of feeling special. Lovecraft accidentally described a technique for that, wrapped in eleven layers of something else.

Building a personal canon, deliberately

The material is large and the quality is uneven, which is a normal condition for a literary tradition rather than a problem unique to it. The way through is to build a canon with a stated rule. Mine is: a text earns a place if I would still read it having removed the politics, and if it adds something structurally rather than atmospherically. That rule keeps the good work and discards most of the pastiche, which is well-intentioned, competently written, and adds nothing to either. The rule also makes the corpus finite, which matters more than it sounds.

A finite canon is the difference between a reading habit and an infinite scroll. Almost everyone who gets into trouble with this material has a reading list with two hundred entries and no organising principle, and the list becomes the practice by default. Write the rule down. Note which three texts made the largest contribution to your own vocabulary. Then notice that you could reproduce most of the effect from those three alone, which is true, and which frees you to stop looking for the one that completes the set. There is no such text, and hunting for it is the most common way this hobby eats a decade.

When the material starts costing you sleep

The honest risk profile of this material is specific and it is not supernatural. It runs through sleep, through fear, and through identity. A person who spends nights with material about entities older than humanity, alone, in the dark, is running a mild version of an experiment that reliably produces sleep disruption and elevated anxiety in anyone, for entirely mundane reasons. Those are the numbers, and they are well established for the general population and require no belief of any kind to apply.

The thresholds worth watching are practical rather than dramatic. Trouble falling asleep that began when the practice intensified. Checking on things in the house that you did not used to check. A sense that the material is producing instructions rather than describing options. Any of those, and the intervention is reduction rather than analysis: shorter sessions, daylight, company, and fewer of the worst sources. The tradition's own advice is short daily practice rather than escalating practice, and in this case that advice is not a mystical preference. It is the correct clinical response to a known risk.

A thirty-day sequence for working with this

Here is a bounded version, in case you want one. The first week is reading only: three stories and the essay, all in daylight, with a note on what each one made you feel in physical terms. The second week is writing, producing a single page of your own vocabulary, which will be a list of sensations rather than a list of entities. The third week is the first working, and it should be small: one sentence about scale, reduced to a glyph, charged in daylight, and logged the same day. The fourth week is review, and the only question is whether the practice changed anything you can point at.

The design of that sequence is deliberate. Reading comes before writing because writing first produces your own clichés. Writing comes before working because working first produces an atmosphere you cannot examine. And review comes last because a practice you never reviewed is a practice that can only be described in the vocabulary of enthusiasm. If the fourth week produces a null result, that is a successful run and it is worth recording, because it tells you the material was atmosphere and the atmosphere was the point. If it produces a real effect, you now have one data point and exactly one, which is worth having and not worth generalising from.

What to read next, and what to skip

After Lovecraft himself, the highest-value next step is not more Lovecraft. It is one solid history of the tradition you are actually going to practise, read properly, because it will tell you which parts have a lineage and which parts are twentieth-century reconstruction. That single book does more for a practitioner than any amount of additional atmosphere. After the history, read one methodological text, short, from someone who thought about why the method works. After those two, go back to whatever you like, with the criterion you set in the fourth week.

The things worth skipping are the ones that offer access. Any text presenting itself as revealing hidden information, initiating you into a lineage that is not documented, or handing you a method whose effect is guaranteed before you begin. That pattern is common across the entire field and it is not specific to this material, but it is at its most persuasive here, because the subject is supposed to be forbidden. Somebody who knows real secrets would say so, and that is a statement about how secrecy actually works. The people with real secrets are not on the internet selling access to them. Neither am I.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where does the Lovecraft imagery actually enter modern practice?

Through music, art, tattoos, game art and internet subculture rather than through any grimoire. What transfers is vocabulary and atmosphere: words, shapes, and the sensation of scale. What does not transfer is any entity structure, because the fiction never gave one. That is the honest shape of the influence.

Is the mythos a real tradition with hidden origins?

No. It is a literary invention with a documented set of authors and dates, and it reaches the twentieth century through a small number of specific writers. Presenting it as ancient is the most common error in the surrounding material, and any source that claims otherwise will not survive being checked against the publication dates.

Do I have to leave the stories behind entirely?

No. The stories are good and the 1928 essay is genuinely useful craft advice. Leave the cosmology on the shelf, take the atmosphere, and do not explain away the racial views when you read. Those are separable, and the material works fine without them.

Is it dangerous to engage with this at night?

It carries an ordinary and measurable risk, which is why the sensible version is daylight, company and bounded sessions. Cosmic indifference is a feeling and feelings rehearse. Persistent sleep disruption or new checking habits are the signals to stop and reduce, not to analyse.

Is Eerie Roads worth buying for working with this kind of imagery?

Only for the dream half of it. It earns its place if you want a place to log dream material properly, which is the one part of this work that produces a real record. For the Lovecraft half it does nothing, because there is no working half. The method is free and the book is optional; buy the tool, skip the atmosphere.
