Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Ask what the theory of a practice consists of and the honest answer for this one is that it does not consist of propositions. There is no cosmology in it. There is no claim about what reality is made of, no doctrine of correspondence, and no hierarchy of powers to memorise. What there is, in two short pamphlets written in the nineteen-seventies and a name borrowed on purpose, is an order of operations. Write the statement. Reduce it. Charge it. Do the follow-through. Keep the record. Every other thing people call the theory is a later addition, and the additions are where the confusion comes from. Read as a sequence, the material is unusually coherent: it appeared in the early seventies, its founding texts insisted on experiment rather than inherited doctrine, the name announced an affiliation with a much older text, and its one durable contribution was methodological, because it made it socially acceptable to test a claim instead of accepting it from a predecessor. Those four statements are not a worldview. They are the instructions, and they are the reason the sequence works. What follows is that sequence, in order, with the places where practitioners skip a step and the step turns out to have been carrying the load.

A theory that is actually an order of operations

This is the first thing to establish, because everything else depends on it. If you are looking for a body of claims you can accept or reject, you will not find one, and the absence tends to be read as vagueness rather than as design. The material was written to be done, not believed. Its authors were explicit that experiment came before doctrine, which is a way of saying that the procedure was the content and the interpretation was optional.

The consequence is unusual and slightly disorienting. A person can perform the whole sequence perfectly and hold no opinion at all about what any of it means, and that person will have got everything the material offers. Almost every dispute in the genre comes from people who want the sequence to imply more than it says, and then resent the genre for not confirming the extra.

What the founding documents asked you to do first

Two pamphlets, both short, both from the same decade. One is a beginner's manual, ideological by accident. Written by the person whose name the movement still carries. The other is an argument that happens to contain instructions, written by a different man with the same surname and a strained relationship to the first. The first is easier to start with and the second is easier to finish with.

Neither of them is a text to believe. They are texts to work through, and the honest way to read either is as a set of procedures with a stated purpose. If you read them as a creed you will find a great deal to disagree with. If you read them as a manual you will find a first month of work, which is what they were written to be.

The name, and the affiliation it declares

The name is a pun and it is also a claim. It takes a well-established English word for disorder and pairs it with a word for practice, and underneath the joke there is a deliberate reference to an older text whose central image is a throne in the place of order. The founders took that reference knowingly and said so. The genre has been arguing about whether that was sincere ever since.

The affiliation matters practically in one narrow way and not in any of the ways the argument suggests. It means the material is not a system that begins from nothing. There is a prior text, and anyone who wants to know what the founders thought they were continuing can go and read it. What it does not mean is that the prior text's conclusions are inherited. The whole point of the reference was to claim a lineage of method, not of doctrine.

Step one, and the sentence that goes on paper

Write one sentence, in the imperative, that names something you could check. This is the step everybody overlooked, and it is the step that makes the other four possible. An aim in the indicative mood cannot be evaluated. An aim in the imperative can be marked done or not done on a particular evening, and a sentence that can be marked is a sentence the record can hold.

The test for the sentence is narrow. It should name an action rather than a condition, a count rather than a quality, and a time rather than a mood. I write a large number of aims that fail this test and they all fail in the same direction, toward describing the kind of person I would like to be rather than the thing I intend to do on Thursday. The sentence is the working. Everything after this point is bookkeeping on a sentence you have already committed to.

Step two, and the glyph that has to lose its letters

Reduce the sentence to a glyph. The technique was formalised in the early twentieth century by a London artist who had noticed that dropping the letters that repeat and cancel leaves a small set of strokes that nobody but you can read. The reduction is the method, and the reduction is the part that takes twenty minutes, and the twenty minutes is not decoration.

The reason the reduction matters is not that the glyph carries meaning for anything outside your head. It is that the act of reducing changes the sentence. After twenty minutes of subtracting letters, you notice that your sentence was three sentences wearing one coat, and the glyph is whichever one you could not drop. That discovery is the actual output of the step, and it is why the step cannot be delegated to a generator without losing the thing you were getting.

Step three, and the hour after the drawing

Charge it, which in practice means sitting with the glyph and the sentence for ten or twenty minutes with nothing else in the room. The hour afterwards matters more than the sitting. A charged working followed by an immediate return to a screen has been charged into a life that does not want it, and the next morning will tell you so.

I have failed this step more often than any other, and the failure always looks the same: the charge is good, the evening is good, and I open the inbox. What has actually happened in that case is that the sequence is complete up to step three and the working has never been given a chance to attach to anything. Keep the hour. If you cannot keep the hour, you have bought a drawing.

Step four, and the fortnight that is not optional

The follow-through is two weeks and it is the only step that touches the world. The charge changes nothing. The fortnight is where a working either becomes part of a routine or joins a drawer, and the difference is made by whether you did the thing on the days you did not feel like doing it.

Two weeks is short enough that nobody can call it devotion and long enough that the effect of a single evening is washed out. That ratio is the point of the number. A week is too short to distinguish a habit from a burst of motivation. A month is long enough to accumulate the changes you were hoping to cause, so you can no longer tell whether the working did anything. The fortnight is the only window in which the arithmetic of your own behaviour is still legible.

Step five, and the record somebody else could read

Close with a page that another person could read and reach a conclusion from. Four columns is enough. What was asked, in the imperative. What happened, as a number or a yes. What the fortnight was like, in one line. And what you would have done anyway, which is the column that makes the whole record defensible.

That fourth column is where the method earns its reputation. The genre's most durable contribution was not a belief but a permission: testing a claim rather than inheriting one became acceptable, and a permission is only real if you are willing to run the test that might come out badly. A record with that column filled in can come out badly. A record without it can only ever come out well, and a record that can only come out well is not a record.

Where the sequence contradicts the pamphlets

The pamphlets are shorter and more permissive than the procedure most people actually use. They contain very little about what to do after the charge, which is where nearly all the difficulty lives, and their tone suggests that a great deal is left to the operator. The modern sequence with its fortnight and its record page is an addition, made by practitioners who found that the permissive version was not sufficient to produce anything they could see.

That is a criticism of the source material, and it is not a criticism of the method. A founding document is allowed to be thin. What is worth noticing is that the missing parts are all in the operational half, not the rhetorical half, and that the rhetorical half is the half everybody quotes.

The first failure, and which step produced it

The common first failure is a flat fortnight followed by a conclusion that the working failed, and the conclusion is often correct about the fortnight and wrong about the working. Three steps are usually to blame, and they are diagnosable. If the sentence was not checkable, nothing downstream could be read. If the hour after the charge was lost, the working never attached. If the fourth column of the record was filled in generously, you will not be able to tell which evenings were the practice working.

Diagnose in that order, because it is the order the sequence runs. Almost nobody gets to step four with a broken step one and finds the cause in step one. I have. The fix is always to go back and rewrite the sentence, which feels like a demotion and is actually the only repair available.

Repeating without the working going stale

A second working on the same aim is not a repeat. It is a new working with the same target, and it needs a new sentence, a new charge, and its own fortnight. Reusing the glyph from the first one is the common shortcut, and it produces something that has stopped referring to anything in particular because the sentence it referred to has been revised twice without the glyph being redrawn.

The practical version is simple. Keep the old glyph in the notebook with its date and its result beside it, and draw a new one for the new sentence. Two years of a practice kept this way is a body of work you can read, which is a different thing from a drawer of drawings, and it is the only durable artefact the sequence actually produces.

Where the practice goes after the sequence

After a year of this you will have a notebook, a vocabulary, and a set of habits that produce small measurable changes in a part of your own life. That is a complete result and it is the one the material was designed to produce. What it is not is a body of knowledge about the world, and the gap between those two things is where the genre's worst writing lives.

If you want to go further, the further step is narrower than it looks. Add structure to the aims you can already check, one at a time, and let the record accumulate. Do not add a cosmology until the notebook is thick enough that a cosmology would have something to attach to. A practitioner with four years of records and no position on anything is in a stronger position than one with a position and no records, and the genre rarely says so out loud.

Frequently Asked Questions

If there is no doctrine, what exactly is the theory of the practice?

An order of operations. The founding texts were written to be performed rather than believed, and their structure is a procedure: write a checkable sentence, reduce it to a glyph, charge it, do the fortnight, keep a readable record. A practitioner can carry out the whole sequence and hold no opinion about any of it, and that practitioner has taken everything the material offers. Most of what gets called the theory is later interpretation rather than instruction.

Is the name's reference to the older text a claim that its conclusions are inherited?

It is a claim about method, not doctrine. The founders took the reference deliberately and announced it, and the point of it was to place the new work inside a longer line of practice rather than to adopt that line's conclusions. The practical consequence is narrow: there is a prior text you can go and read if you want to know what they thought they were continuing. It does not oblige you to agree with it.

Why is a fortnight the standard follow-through?

Because it is the shortest span long enough to wash out a single motivated evening and the longest span short enough that changes you cause yourself are still separable from the working. A week is indistinguishable from a burst of enthusiasm. A month lets ordinary life accumulate enough to hide the signal. Two weeks keeps the arithmetic of your own behaviour legible, which is the only thing the record is for.

Can a generator replace the letter-reduction step?

It can produce a glyph and it will lose the part that matters. Twenty minutes of removing repeated and cancelling letters forces you to notice that your sentence contained several claims and that you could not drop most of them. The glyph is a by-product of that discovery. Hand the reduction to software and you keep the drawing and lose the twenty minutes, which is where the thinking was.

What should I do if the first fortnight is flat?

Diagnose in sequence order, starting at the beginning. A sentence that was not checkable makes everything downstream unreadable. A charge that was followed immediately by a screen was never attached to anything. A record whose final column was filled in generously will not tell you which evenings were real. Go back to the earliest step you cannot vouch for and rewrite it, accepting that this means a new glyph and a new fortnight.
