ELHOS

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Banishing is the reflex of this whole field, and the reflex is worth examining rather than obeying. The rite is short, old, widely available, and cheap, which is exactly why it has accumulated a large amount of unexamined belief around something that functions as a piece of phrasing. This is an account of where it comes from, what it actually does when it works, the three verbs people confuse, and the point at which a sensible practice turns into a superstition that gets in the way. None of that requires hostility to the tradition. It requires knowing which part is load-bearing, because the load-bearing part is the sentence, and the sentence survives being used without the rest.

The reflex, and what it is actually for

Nearly everyone in this field learns a banishing early, usually without being told what it is for, and then uses it for a wide range of problems that it was not designed to address. In its original setting it is a statement of a boundary, delivered in fixed words, at the end of a formal sequence. Its function is to make the position of the practitioner unambiguous, so that whatever else is happening in the room is not going to become permanent.

That is a narrower function than most use suggests. It does not remove whatever disturbed you, and it does not make the space safe. It establishes a line and says the line out loud. Most of the disappointing results attributed to the rite come from asking it to do the other two jobs that people have bundled into it, and the first step towards getting value from it is separating those jobs again.

Where the default rite came from

The form almost everyone uses descends from initiatory material associated with the Golden Dawn at the end of the nineteenth century, and by the middle of the next one it had become the default closing for Western ceremonial practice. It is a short piece of highly patterned text, built for repetition, and it was written for a room with a defined shape and a defined exit.

Transplanted into ordinary flats, cars, and difficult conversations, it keeps the words and loses the room. That is not a reason to abandon it. It is a reason to understand that its original context gave it structure which most modern use does not supply, and that any substitute you write can supply the same structure more honestly than a text written for a temple can supply it in a kitchen.

Why declaring beats fighting

The grammar of the rite is declarative. It says what is and is not permitted, in words that assume an intelligence capable of understanding them, and it closes the ground rather than attacking anything. Almost every traditional formulation shares that structure, which is a strong hint that the structure is the part that matters rather than the particular wording.

This is also why a talismanic object is a poor substitute. A talisman asserts that a thing has been made to act, which is a claim about power, and it has to be believed to be charged. A declaration asserts a boundary, which has to be understood to be effective, and understanding can be arranged. A boundary you can say out loud in your own words, in a voice that is yours, will outperform an object you acquired in a context you know nothing about.

The rising, and the part that works

Many traditions attach a physical movement to the closing: crossing the space towards the door while delivering the words. People tend to drop it, and the dropping is the most consequential of the omissions, because the movement is where the boundary stops being an idea and becomes an event you have committed to.

The physical component is not a formality. Moving towards an exit while declaring a limit combines two independent signals, one verbal and one behavioural, and it does something that neither does alone: it makes the statement cost you nothing to keep and everything to ignore. That is the mechanism most likely to be doing the work, and it is available without any of the apparatus, in any room, at any hour, which is also why it does not need the ritual to be interesting.

Three verbs people run together

Three distinct operations get collapsed into the word banishing, and the collapse is the source of most confusion. Cleansing is about purification: clearing accumulated residue from a space or a person. Grounding is about your own footing: coming back into your body and your sense of scale after something destabilised you. Banishing is about removal: declaring that something does not belong here.

The three answer different questions and are needed in different orders. Someone who has been frightened needs grounding before they can decide whether anything is present. Someone who has finished working needs banishing to close the boundary. Someone who has moved into a new place and feels stale needs cleansing. Running the wrong one is not harmful, but it is unsatisfying, because the person ends up performing a correct operation that addresses a problem they do not have.

The failure nobody warns you about

The unadvertised failure is that the rite can begin to generate the state it is supposed to resolve. Saying out loud that nothing unwelcome is present, in a room you believe might be disturbed, is a way of putting the possibility in front of yourself repeatedly. People who use it compulsively report more intrusive imagery, not less, and the mechanism is obvious once stated: attention is a spotlight, and a spotlight pointed at a wall shows you the wall.

The corrective is not to stop, but to change the target. Ground first, genuinely, and then declare something specific and true rather than something you are afraid is untrue. A boundary stated about your own evening is a sentence you can stand behind. A boundary stated against a thing you have imagined into the room is a sentence that keeps the room occupied, and the more solemnly it is delivered the more firmly it is fixed there.

What a boundary is not

A boundary is a statement about your own conduct and your own space. It is not a weapon, it is not a deterrent aimed at a specific adversary, and it is not a description of what is present. The moment the wording becomes a threat, it has changed category, and a threat requires an opponent, which means the practitioner has just introduced one into a situation that may have been empty.

The distinction is also what keeps the practice from becoming talismanic. Talismans accumulate value through association, fear, and ownership, and they ask to be carried, displayed, and maintained. A boundary asks for nothing after the statement is made. If you find yourself reluctant to leave a room without performing something first, or unwilling to travel without an object, the practice has become an object practice and the object is now the load-bearing part, which was never the design.

A working built in one evening

Building your own version takes one evening and no materials beyond a sheet of paper. Write the boundary you actually need stated, in the plainest words that would still be true if read aloud to the person standing nearest to you. Name the space, name the time, name the condition, and finish with a sentence that closes rather than escalates.

Then practise it three times in three ordinary places, at three unremarkable hours, and see whether it survives contact with a room where nothing is wrong. A rite that only works at midnight on a serious occasion will be unavailable on the Tuesday when you actually need it. The test is dullness, not intensity, and the version you can say while putting your keys down is the one that will still be available in a year.

The night it did not work

There will be a night when the fear does not lift and the words come out flat, and the honest response is not to escalate. Escalation is what almost everyone does: a longer rite, more items, a louder voice, a new object. Each step feels like addressing the problem and each step adds weight to the thing that was already too heavy.

The useful response is to note the date, the hour, what you said, and what happened, and then to do the unglamorous version of the same operation. Ground. Walk. Sleep on it. Check in the morning whether anything was actually there, which it almost never was. A flat night is a normal data point in a practice, and treating it as a reason to strengthen the ritual is how a reasonable technique becomes something that requires a certain emotional condition to be maintained.

Reading the weeks after

Keep a short record: the date, the place, the situation, whether you used your own words or the inherited ones, and what happened in the following hour and the following day. Two columns of comparison matter more than the rest. Whether you reached for the practice when you needed it, and whether reaching for it changed what you did next.

The second column is the one that settles whether this is a technique or a comfort. If using the rite reliably changes what you do in the situation, it is working as intended. If it reliably does not, and merely makes the next twenty minutes feel less uneasy, then it is a mood management device, which is a legitimate thing to want and a poor thing to describe as a working.

When the ritual becomes the superstition

The transition is gradual and there is no moment to notice it. The signs are behavioural. You begin to believe the words have power independent of your delivery, so you start worrying about mispronouncing them. You feel uneasy leaving a room you have not closed. You describe the practice as protecting you rather than as something you do, and the passive voice is the giveaway, because the rite was always active.

At that point the honest move is to stop for a while and find out what you actually believe. A month without it will tell you, and it will tell you cheaply, because whatever the rite was doing is available to you without it. The technique is a tool for making a boundary audible under stress, and tools that are kept in reserve get used; tools that are treated as load-bearing walls get inspected at three in the morning.

The two things that actually help

When something has genuinely shaken you, two things do most of the work and neither is a rite. The first is a change of state, and it is available in ninety seconds: stand, walk, get cold water on your face, or breathe out longer than you breathe in. The second is a change of framing, and it takes a question: what specifically am I afraid of, what would I do if it happened, and who would I tell. Naming the fear in these terms shrinks it from a quality of the room to a description of a situation.

The closing rite belongs after both of these, or instead of them when the fear is mild. Its contribution is the last one to be added and the first to be dropped: it marks the transition, tells the body the event is over, and provides a sentence you can say out loud. It is genuinely useful. It is also the least important part, which is the thing almost nobody in the tradition will tell you, and knowing it is the last of the three is what keeps the practice in proportion.

Knowing when to stop reaching for it

Set the threshold in advance, while you are calm, and write it down. The threshold is a description of the situation rather than a feeling: any disturbance involving a stranger, any night without sleep, any room you would not sit in alone, any occasion where you would be embarrassed to be seen doing it. Above the line you use your own words and you ground. Below it, you do nothing at all.

This is a hard rule to hold, because the impulse to reach for something when uneasy is not a belief you can argue with. It is why the rule has to be written while you are not uneasy. Written then, it is a piece of ordinary planning. Written at three in the morning, it will lose to whatever is available, and the practice will quietly become the thing you do every time you are frightened, which is the opposite of a proportionate tool.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a banishing ritual actually do?

Its original function is to state a boundary in fixed words at the close of a formal sequence, making your position unambiguous so that nothing in the room becomes permanent. It does not remove a presence or make a space safe, and most disappointing results attributed to it come from expecting it to do one of those other jobs instead. As phrasing it works; as protection it is being asked for more than it was written to do.

Is the Lesser Banishing of Ritual necessary, or can I write my own?

You can write your own, and for ordinary rooms it is usually better, because a sentence you wrote is a sentence you can stand behind without embarrassment. The inherited text was written for a temple with a defined shape and a defined exit. What matters is the structure it uses: name the space, state the limit in declarative language, and close. The particular wording is a carrier for that structure, not the structure itself.

Why is the physical movement towards the door part of the rite?

Because it is where the boundary becomes an event rather than an idea. Moving towards an exit while declaring a limit combines a verbal signal with a behavioural commitment, and it makes the statement something you have already begun to act on. That is also why the movement is the part most often dropped and the part most likely to be doing the work, and it is available without any apparatus in any space.

How do I know when I have become superstitious about it?

Look for passive constructions in how you describe it. If you say the rite protects you rather than that you use it, that is the signal. Other signs include anxiety about mispronouncing the words, reluctance to leave a room without performing it, and a belief that the words carry power apart from your delivery of them. The test is a month without it, which will show you what it was actually doing.

What should I do on a night when nothing works?

Do the unglamorous version of the same operation, and do not escalate. Ground properly, write down the date, the hour, what you said, and what happened, and check in the morning whether anything was there. Adding a longer rite or a new object in response to a flat night is the most common way a reasonable technique turns into something that requires a particular emotional state to be maintained.
