ATLÁNTIDA

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Two names appear together in almost every conversation about lost civilisations, and they are not the same subject. The Annunaki come out of a specific set of cuneiform tablets excavated and deciphered in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and the text that mentions them can be read by anyone who wants to check. Atlantis comes out of two dialogues Plato wrote as a literary device, and no contemporary source mentions it at all. Treating them as one subject has produced most of the nonsense that circulates about both. This is an account of what a working built on ancient material actually looks like when you insist on the text, including the first attempt at one, where it failed, and why.

Two names that sound like the same subject

The pairing is recent and it is not natural. The Annunaki belong to the Enuma Elish, the Babylonian creation account whose most complete version was found in tablets from Nineveh and dates from the second millennium BCE. Atlantis belongs to the Timaeus and the Critias, which Plato wrote four centuries later as the frame for a dialogue about ideal constitutions. One is a text with a manuscript history. The other is a device inside a philosophical story, and Plato's own framing makes that explicit: he offers it as a fiction, the way an illustration is a fiction, so that the argument about the ideal state has something to point at.

The consequence for a practitioner is practical rather than pedantic. If you want to work with the Annunaki, you can obtain the translation, read the passage, and argue with the translator. If you want to work with Atlantis, you are working with a deliberate invention by a philosopher, and the interesting question is what he was doing, not what happened. Both subjects survive in English translation and both are public. Neither requires belief to be studied.

What the tablets actually say about the Annunaki

In the Enuma Elish a group of younger deities is assigned labour in the underworld. They are tired of the work and appeal to the senior god, who proposes creating humans instead so that the labour can be delegated. Humans are made from clay and animated, and the Annunaki are released from their task. That is the account. It is a creation story explaining the origin of a labouring class, and it sits alongside other Mesopotamian texts that do similar work with different materials.

Nothing in the tablets says the Annunaki came from another planet, built humanity as a food source for some later purpose, or administered a technical civilisation. Those readings arrive in the twentieth century, and they arrive from outside the text. When you read the passage and find none of that in it, you have not disproved anything about the Annunaki. You have established that the text does not say it, which is a narrower and more useful claim.

The reading that was added in the twentieth century

The modern version of the Annunaki story takes a group of beings from a distant planet, follows them to a region in the Middle East, and adds an extensive engineering and genetic programme for the purpose of labour supply. This reading is widely repeated and it is not the reading of any cuneiform scholar. It borrows a figure from a genuinely ancient text and supplies a plot from somewhere else entirely.

The reason to name this is not pedantry. A practitioner who builds a working on the modern version is building it on a twentieth-century addition, and needs to know that. A practitioner who builds on the text is building on something that was written down, in a form we can check, and that has a specific function in its own literary world. Both are legitimate projects. Only one of them is ancient.

Atlantis, and the problem with a literary device

Plato introduces Atlantis in the Timaeus and develops it in the Critias, then abandons the thread before finishing the story. The incomplete narrative is itself a clue. A text written to be a philosophical illustration does not need its invented history to be internally consistent beyond the point the argument requires, and the abandonment is consistent with that. Later antiquity picked the story up, and the modern era made it an invention engine, which is a genuinely creative afterlife that has nothing to do with Plato's purpose.

The material on Atlantis is also uneven in a way the material on the Annunaki is not. There is no manuscript tradition, no competing translations, no cuneiform tablets. There is Plato. Working with it means working with a philosopher's thought experiment, which is a fine thing to do and requires you to say so in advance, otherwise you will find yourself defending a claim that no source ever made.

The texts you can check this afternoon

A workable corpus exists and it is not hard to get. The Enuma Elish is available in translation with commentary. The Epic of Gilgamesh, the Egyptian Book of the Dead, the Norse Eddas and the Mahabharata all have published translations, most of them free. The Enuma Elish and the Gilgamesh material come from overlapping scholarly traditions, and the Egyptian, Norse and Indian corpora are independent of both. That independence is what makes cross-reading worthwhile: two texts that arrived separately and never knew about each other will not agree on details, and the places they do agree are the ones worth looking at.

Translations vary, and the variation is worth attending to rather than resenting. Where a translator renders a term one way and another renders it differently, the difference often carries the interpretive question. Keeping a note of which translation you used, and reading the same passage in a second one, is a twenty-minute habit that prevents you from building on a translator's decision without knowing it was one.

Working with translation instead of pretending otherwise

Every ancient text you want to use is available to you in English and not in the original, with two exceptions most practitioners never reach. Working honestly with that means naming it in your own notes. Write that you are working from the translation, and name the translation. It costs one line and it prevents a specific kind of overreach, the kind where a rendering you did not examine gets treated as if a scholar had confirmed it.

There is a practical second step. Read the same passage in two translations and write down where they diverge. You will find that the interesting material is usually concentrated in the divergent passages, because that is where the translators had to make a decision. A working built on a contested rendering is not forbidden. It is a working built on a specific claim, and the claim should be written down so that a later reader, including you, can disagree with it.

What a myth is for, and what it is not

A myth in this sense is a compressed account that carries a set of relationships: a creator and a created thing, a labour that is resented, a boundary that holds, a price paid for crossing it. It is not a report. Reading it as a report produces the weakest version of every conclusion, because you end up arguing about whether a literal event occurred, which is a question the text was never shaped to answer.

The functional reading is also the one that transfers. If the Enuma Elish is about the origin of a labouring class, then the relationships it carries are about labour, resentment, delegation and release, and those are the relationships a working on it can use. The myth is a source of structure. It is not a source of information about a lost civilisation, and treating it as one wastes the most valuable thing it has.

The first attempt, and where it failed

The first working built on this material tried to do too much. It took the whole creation account, chose a broad aim about civilisational change, and charged a sigil built from a phrase that could not be checked afterward. The result was three months of ambiguous experience and a log that could not distinguish a change in the world from a change in what was being noticed. The failure was in the design, not the material. The material was fine.

What fixed it was making the aim narrow and countable, and opening a record before the charge rather than after. A second working on a single relationship from the same tablets, with an aim that named something a person would do rather than something a world would become, produced a result that a third party could look at. Same corpus, same translator, opposite outcome. The material was never the variable.

Building a working from borrowed scripture

The sequence that works is short. Choose one passage rather than a corpus. Read it in two translations and write down the divergence. Name the relationships the passage carries in a single sentence, without divine or historical framing. Turn that sentence into an imperative aim naming a behaviour. Charge the glyph from the sentence, not from the myth. Then keep a record with a column for what you would have done anyway.

The reason for the last column is that a working on ancient material is unusually easy to over-read. When the material is grand and the change is small, the temptation to credit the material is strong. The column exists to hold that temptation in view. If the outcome is the kind of thing you might have done on a good week, the honest entry is that you cannot tell, and writing that down is worth more than a confident interpretation.

The failure of the grand claim

The most common failure in this area is not a bad working. It is a claim about origins. Once you have said that a group of beings came from elsewhere, or that a continent sank, you have entered a dispute where the evidence is either absent or contested, and the dispute will absorb all the attention that the practice would otherwise generate.

The narrow version avoids the dispute entirely. A statement about what a text says, a claim about the relationships it carries, a working built on those relationships, and a record of whether anything changed. None of that requires you to win anything. It requires you to be accurate, which is achievable, and it leaves the practice available to you afterwards instead of exhausted by a fight you cannot finish.

Reading old material without inheriting its conclusions

There is a specific discipline here that has nothing to do with scepticism. When you pick up a text that has been read for two thousand years by people with commitments, you inherit their conclusions whether you want them or not, because the summaries and the framing arrive before the text does. The countermeasure is unglamorous: go to the passage before the commentary, and write down what you think it says before you read what anyone else thinks.

The habit is easy to describe and hard to keep, because the secondary literature is more comfortable and more rewarding to read. Doing it anyway produces a different relationship with the material. You stop accumulating opinions about the Annunaki and start holding a text, and a text can be worked with in a way an opinion cannot.

The passage you would actually use

If the working is to be useful, it should be small enough that you can describe it in one sentence and check it in a month. The labour and the release are the strongest material here, because the structure is complete: something is asked for, something is made, something is owed, something ends. That is four beats, and four beats is enough to build a glyph from without borrowing a single element that is not in the passage.

Everything else in this area is decoration. The trilobites, the beams of light, the engineers, the coordinates, the cross-references between unrelated texts: none of it survives the question what does the text actually say, and most of it fails it. One passage, two translations, one imperative sentence, a record with an awkward column, and a narrow aim. That is the whole method, and it is enough to work with.

Frequently Asked Questions

Did the Annunaki come from another planet?

The tablets do not say so. The Enuma Elish presents them as younger deities assigned labour who petition to be released, with humanity created so the labour can be delegated. The planet version is a twentieth-century addition drawn from outside the text, and it is worth separating clearly from what the tablets contain when you build a working on this material.

Is Atlantis a real place?

No source contemporary with Plato mentions it. It appears in the Timaeus and Critias as a literary device framing a philosophical argument, and the story is never finished. It has had an enormous creative afterlife since the nineteenth century, which is a genuine cultural fact, but it is not evidence for a place that existed.

Which ancient texts are worth working from?

Any that you can read in a published translation, and the four with the best translation coverage are the Enuma Elish, the Epic of Gilgamesh, the Egyptian Book of the Dead, and the Norse Eddas. The Mahabharata is available too, and is much longer. The practical requirement is narrower than a scholarly one: you need a translation, and ideally a second one to compare.

Why does reading two translations matter?

Because the differences are where the interpretive decisions sit. Translators have to choose between renderings of terms that carry several senses at once, and each choice nudges the passage in a particular direction. Reading two and writing down where they diverge tells you which parts of your working rest on a translator's judgement, which is a claim you can then argue with rather than inherit silently.

Can a sigil be built from a myth?

Yes, and the reliable version uses the myth's structure rather than its content. Identify the relationships the passage carries, write them as a single sentence without divine framing, and turn that into an imperative aim naming a behaviour you could check. Aiming at a lost civilisation produces something you cannot verify; aiming at a named action produces something you can.
