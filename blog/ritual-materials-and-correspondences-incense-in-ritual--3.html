Ritual Materials and Correspondences

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Western ceremonial magic runs on tables. Planets link to metals, colours, numbers, days, angels, and hours, and those links are what turn a sequence of gestures into a system with internal logic. The tables go back to the Agrippa's Three Books of Occult Philosophy, printed in 1533, and they are still the reference most practitioners open first. This article covers what correspondence is for, where the tables came from, why a distinctive smell does more work than most people expect, how to assemble a kit that stays usable, and the practical and legal lines around materials that get people into trouble.

Correspondence as an organising principle

Correspondence is the linking of planets to metals, colours, numbers, days, angels, and hours. It is the skeleton of Western ceremonial magic and the reason a working can be said to be coherent. If Tuesday is Mars and red and iron and a flame is the correct action, the individual elements carry no weight of their own. The structure is what does the work, and the structure is what a beginner is actually learning when they study the tables.

The alternative is accumulating objects for their own sake, which produces a large and inert practice. Practitioners who buy more without learning the tables end up with a shelf of beautiful objects and no way to use them. The tables are cheap, public, and take an afternoon, and they outperform any amount of equipment shopping.

Agrippa and the codification of the tables

The Three Books of Occult Philosophy, published in 1533, were the first major printed Western synthesis of planetary, angelic, and demonic hierarchies. The planetary and elemental tables descend almost directly from them, and most of the correspondences you see printed today trace back to that text with later additions from Johann Weyer and other compilers of the following century.

The fact that they are late sources matters for how much weight they carry. These are Renaissance compilations of earlier material, not ancient revelation, and they contain internal contradictions because they combine several traditions that disagreed with each other. Where two sources assign different colours to a planet, neither is more authentic. Pick one, use it consistently, and stop treating the table as a fact about the universe.

What the tables are actually good for

They are good at constraint. Choosing from a defined set of options based on an established scheme produces coherent choices faster than improvisation, and it produces the same choice twice, which makes a working repeatable. A practitioner who fixes a colour for a planet and uses it every time develops a working relationship with that colour, and that relationship is the part that carries over into results.

They are poor at derivation. Any system that can assign a correspondence to almost anything given enough effort will eventually assign one to whatever you were already thinking. Using the tables to confirm an intention is reasonable. Using them to discover an intention, or to decide whether an intention is auspicious, is the machinery working backwards from your preference.

Incense, and why smell does so much work

the sense of smell is unusually durable and largely separate from verbal memory; scents also carry strong associative load, so a distinctive smell attached to a specific activity becomes a retrieval cue for the whole state of mind around it. That is why the same incense in a working produces a different felt sense than the same incense in a room where you are trying to sleep.

It is also the cheapest element in a working. A stick of incense costs less than the paper you log the working on and lasts several sessions, so it is the highest-return item in any kit. The one caution is tolerance, because a scent you stop noticing stops working, and rotating two or three rather than owning one is more useful than owning six.

Building a kit you will actually use

Start with paper, a pen, a printed copy of the tables, a lighter, and one incense. That is a complete working kit. Everything beyond that is optional, and most of it amounts to optional again once you understand that a ritual is a defined sequence of attention with objects placed at specific points in it.

Then add only what solves a problem you have actually hit. A candle you light and extinguish at a defined point in a sequence does something that a drawn circle does not. A blade used for cutting a cord does something a paper tear does not. There is no need for chalices, athames, or full regalia unless you have reached the stage of wanting them, and reaching that stage by accident is the expensive route.

Setting up an altar without overbuilding it

A table with a cloth, a candle, an incense holder, and somewhere to put the book you are reading from is a working altar. The arrangement should have a left and a right that you remember, because a working builds on the same arrangement each time and you will not notice you have changed it until the practice feels off for reasons you cannot name.

Keep it somewhere you can reach it and out of the way of ordinary life. An altar in a cupboard that you take out twice a month and one on a shelf you pass daily produce different results, and the second is better even though it gets less attention, because incidental exposure counts. The most common altar failure is not lack of grandeur. It is dust and a candle that has burned down to the holder.

Colour as the shortcut that replaces a full chart

If you cannot hold a whole table in mind, colour does most of the work for you. Pick a colour scheme and stick to it, and the associations will build themselves through repetition rather than through memorisation. Green for growth, red for force, blue for calm, yellow for the sun and for clarity. The specific assignments matter less than that they are consistent.

Consistency is what produces the effect. A cloth, a candle, and a card that are the same colour every week will build a stronger association than a technically correct correspondence applied three different ways. Test it by describing the working to someone else. If you cannot say which colour you used without checking, the system is not yet yours.

Materials with legal and practical limits

Some materials in this tradition carry constraints that have nothing to do with magick. Cannabis appears in documented religious and folk-magical use across Hindu, Rastafarian, and some folk-Christian contexts, and its legal status varies sharply between jurisdictions and even between cities within one country. That variability is a real practical constraint and it is the kind of thing that turns a working into an arrest record.

The general rule is worth stating without exceptions. Know the law where you live before you plan around any substance, keep records of what you use if you use substances at all, and never perform a working while impaired, since the point of a defined sequence is that you can follow it. Nothing in this material requires any substance to function, so the risk is being taken on for no operational gain.

The ritual of the eye, and visual anchoring

A visual anchor works the same way an olfactory one does. A specific image, colour arrangement, or object placed in a fixed position becomes a retrieval cue for the state of mind in which you meet it. Looking at the anchor before a working is a way of arriving at the right condition, and it is faster than breathing exercises for people who find those unconvincing.

The most effective version is the simplest. Choose one image and put it where you can see it from your working position, look at it for a fixed count, and begin. Photographs, printed diagrams, and found objects all work. What does not work is a complicated visual system introduced in the same session as the working it is supposed to support, because you will spend the working thinking about the system.

When the materials run out

A defined end to the stock is a better design than an open-ended supply. One stick of incense, four candles, one use of the cord, one sheet of paper. When it is gone, the working is finished, and finishing is what makes it repeatable. An endless supply of materials produces workings that sprawl, which produces outings that get interrupted, which produces records that never get written.

This is the same principle as the ninety-minute alarm for dream work and the short banishing for space work. The terminus is what allows the practice to have a shape. Materials that never run out are materials that let a session extend past the point where it was doing anything, and most rituals that go wrong go wrong by adding a second, third, and fourth optional component rather than by omitting one.

Matching the material to the statement

Write the statement first, then choose one element that matches it, and resist adding a second. The impulse to assemble a complete set of correspondences for a working is strong and it is largely about looking thorough. What the working needs is a small number of objects placed with intent, and every additional element dilutes the attention you were trying to concentrate.

One matching element done deliberately beats five placed because a table said they matched. If you find yourself checking a chart mid-sequence, you have either chosen the wrong material or you are performing rather than working. Set it aside, finish, and note the uncertainty in the log for next time.

Recording which materials mattered

Keep a column in your log for the material you used and one for what you noticed during the working. Over twenty or thirty sessions this produces an answer you cannot get any other way. Most practitioners find that one or two elements carry nearly all the effect and the rest are decorative, and the discovery is worth more than any correspondence list because it belongs to you.

It also makes the kit cheaper and faster. You can drop what does nothing and keep what does, which reduces the time between deciding to work and beginning. A practice you start is worth more than a well-equipped practice you admire. The tables give you the vocabulary for the log, and the log gives you the kit.
