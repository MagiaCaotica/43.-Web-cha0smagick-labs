Sigils: Theory and Technique

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 15 min read

A sigil is two operations with a gap in the middle, and the gap is where the practice is. The first operation takes a sentence you have written and reduces it to a small set of marks. The second takes those marks and holds attention on them. Nearly every book on the subject describes both and almost none of them examines what happens between them, which is a strange omission given that the between is the only part that is not mechanical. Reduction is bookkeeping, and the charge is attention, and neither of those needs explaining. What needs explaining is the step where a piece of compressed text becomes the object you are going to spend time with, and that step is the one everybody performs without deciding to perform it. There is a documented source for the reduction method, there is a long-standing convention for the shapes, and there is a straightforward reason the digital version is not a shortcut. The sequence below puts the gap in the middle where it belongs, because that is where the practice actually is.

Two stages, and the gap between them

Every sigil practice has the same two halves, and they are different in kind. The first is encoding: you decide what the statement is, you write it in a fixed form, you reduce it to marks according to rules, and you arrive at a small drawing. This half is fully specifiable. Two people given the same statement and the same rules will produce the same marks, and any disagreement about the result is a disagreement about the rules, which can be checked. The second half is attention: you spend a fixed time with the marks. This half is not specifiable at all. The quality of it depends on you, on the day, on what else is in your head.

The gap is the step between arriving at the marks and beginning the attention, and it is where most of the interesting variation lives. It is a step most people perform in about four seconds, during which the marks are still underived, still floating as lines, and then suddenly the attention begins on them as though they had always been a meaningful object. The transition does not happen by itself. Something has to convert a drawing into something worth holding, and if you do not know what that something is, you will supply your own, and what you will supply is usually the emotion you already had about the aim. The result is that you charge a feeling rather than a statement, which feels identical from the inside and produces a different record.

The 1904 pamphlet that gave you the method

The reduction technique has a documented origin, and it is worth knowing that it is recent, specific and manual. A short pamphlet published in the early twentieth century by a writer who was also a practitioner presents a method for reducing a statement of intent to a glyph. The method is essentially: strip the statement to its letters, drop the ones that carry no sound, draw the remainder as a single continuous line that closes at the end. The pamphlet also gives worked examples, which is the part that makes it usable, since the rules are easy to state and hard to apply until you have seen three or four of them done.

The reason this matters is that it removes the mystical framing from the operation. What looks like a symbolic art is a compression procedure with a vocabulary, and the vocabulary is: which letters survive, how each surviving letter becomes a segment, and what the shape means as a whole. Once you have that, you can vary it deliberately. You can change which letters survive and see what the alternative looks like. You can draw the shape differently and notice whether the meaning changes. This is not a departure from the source, it is what the source is for, and the people who treat the shape as fixed are making an arbitrary choice into a rule. The pamphlet is short and public and nobody needs permission to read it.

Reducing the statement, letter by letter

The first half of the work is a sequence of small decisions and it should be done in one sitting, in order, without improvising. Write the aim as a single sentence in the imperative, because a wish and a command encode differently and the command is the one that survives reduction better. Then reduce the words to their letters, keeping only the letters that are pronounced, which usually removes the vowels and leaves a manageable set. Then map each surviving letter to a short segment, and join the segments in order, and close the shape. The whole thing takes twenty minutes and it should feel somewhat tedious, because it is a clerical operation and any pleasure you take in it is a sign you are decorating.

Two decisions here are the ones worth being deliberate about. The first is the aim itself, and the discipline is that it should be a thing you can check. Aims that cannot be checked produce marks that cannot be evaluated, which is the most common reason a first set of sigils goes nowhere: the aim was a state of being rather than an event, so there is nothing that could have happened differently. The second is whether to keep the letters or reduce further. Most practitioners keep them, and the reason is not that the extra marks are meaningful but that they preserve more of the original wording, which means you can decode the glyph back to the statement later. That reversibility is worth more than a cleaner shape, and it is the difference between a sigil you own and a sigil you have.

Drawing the glyph, and what the shapes are doing

Across the grimoire tradition the visual grammar is consistent even where the meanings are not, and recognising this is useful because it lets you read glyphs you did not make. The common conventions are few. A circle or a closed figure stands for unity, totality, or the unit of a thing. A single line stands for an element of intention, one segment per surviving element. A cross or a central junction marks the point where the segments meet, and it is usually placed at the centre so the eye has somewhere to start and end. A division or a break marks a boundary within the intent, and is used when the aim contains two clauses that should not be fused.

The consistency of the grammar and the inconsistency of the attributions is the thing to hold onto. Different sources will tell you that a circle means the same thing, the moon, elemental air, or a unit of self, and all three are within the range of what the convention has been used for. This means the shapes carry a rough semantic range and not a fixed lexicon, which is exactly what you would expect from a convention that was transmitted in practice rather than specified in a textbook. Treat the shapes as a way of organising attention rather than as a symbolic language with a dictionary, and you will use them well. Treat them as a dictionary and you will be stuck on the first time somebody else's glyph does not match your table.

The part you skip, and why it is the part

Between drawing the glyph and charging it, the method usually says nothing, and the practitioner fills the silence with whatever is available, which is normally the emotional state they brought to the session. The result is a charge conducted on a feeling rather than on a statement, and the two are hard to distinguish from inside and easy to distinguish in a record. The way to make the transition deliberate is to do something small and physical at that point. Read the aim aloud. Look at the glyph for a full minute without thinking about the aim at all. Trace the line once with a finger, slowly, following the order of the letters. Any of these will do, and none of them is magic.

What this step is for is to establish that the object you are about to work with is the compressed statement and not a mood. The glyph is derived from the aim, and the derivation is checkable, and the eye that just followed the line has now traced the derivation. Without that, the glyph is a picture and the charge is on the picture. With it, the glyph is a fixed form standing for a specific sentence, and the charge is on the sentence. Practitioners who add this step report that their records become more legible, which is the strongest evidence for it, since the change is not in the outcome but in what they are able to say afterwards about what they did.

Fixed time, and why instalments break the charge

The charge is sustained attention on the glyph for a fixed time, and the two things that make it work are the fixity of the time and the repetition of the return. The time should be short enough that you will actually do it again, which for most people is between ten and twenty minutes, and the fixity matters more than the length because a charge you will repeat is worth more than a longer one you will not. During the charge, attention leaves and returns, and the returning is the whole activity. There is nothing to achieve beyond the number of returns, and this is the point at which the practice is unusually well designed, because it is honest about what it is doing.

The other thing that makes it work is doing it in one sitting rather than in instalments. An interrupted charge has a discontinuity in the middle of it, and the mind treats a discontinuity as a boundary, so a charge that is stopped and resumed becomes two shorter charges with a gap, and the gap is where the drift goes. If you have to stop, stop and count it as stopped rather than resuming. The result is a record with fewer entries in it, and the record will be accurate, which is what makes it usable. A practitioner with twenty clean twenty-minute charges has more to work with than one with a hundred interrupted ones, and the difference compounds over a year.

What encoding is, without the metaphor

Encoding is the thing that a sigil does that a written statement does not, and it can be described in plain terms. A sentence of twenty words carries information that you can read in a second and then forget in another. A glyph of twelve marks carries the same statement in a form that has to be held in mind over time, and holding something in mind over time is work. That work is the part with a cost, and the cost is what makes the practice work, for the mundane reason that anything you have to hold in mind over weeks becomes more present than anything you did not.

This is why digital generation is not a shortcut. A generator performs the compression instantly and perfectly, which is exactly the problem: it removes the twenty minutes of clerical work, and the twenty minutes of clerical work was a large part of what made the glyph yours. Information theory is genuinely relevant here and it is genuinely not the whole story, because the theory is about minimising bits and the practice is about maximising the duration of a thought about a particular sentence. What the generator does produce, well and quickly, is the compression. What it cannot produce is the twenty minutes. If you use one, use it and then do the twenty minutes anyway, on something else that matters, and understand what you have traded.

When a generator is doing the work

There are honest and dishonest versions of using a generator and the difference is whether you know what you are delegating. If you use one to produce a quick glyph for a routine working, you have delegated the compression, which is fine, and you should still derive the aim yourself, in the imperative, because that is where the thinking is. If you use one because drawing is difficult and you want a usable mark, that is also fine and there are many people for whom a generated shape works better than a hand-drawn one. The dishonest version is when the generated glyph replaces the statement, which is what happens when you never write the aim in words at all and ask the machine to go straight to the mark.

There is a third case worth naming, which is generating many variants and choosing. This is a legitimate practice and it is better than most people expect, because choosing among variants is a judgement about which mark best carries which statement, and that judgement is part of the work. It becomes a problem when you generate thirty and choose the one you like, because at that point the choice is aesthetic and the glyph has been selected for pleasure rather than for meaning, and the charge is on a shape you found attractive. A useful discipline: generate three, choose the one that is easiest to draw accurately, and be suspicious if a fourth is more beautiful.

The sequence, start to finish

Write the aim as one imperative sentence. Check that you could tell somebody whether it happened, because if not, rewrite it. Reduce to letters. Map to segments and close the shape. Then the gap: read the aim aloud, look at the glyph for a full minute, trace the line once. Then charge for a fixed and short time in one sitting, with returning as the only activity. Then, in the ten minutes immediately after, write three things: what you did, what you noticed, and what you expected. Then close the notebook and leave the aim alone until the review date you set beforehand.

The last instruction is the one that gets dropped, and it is the one that determines whether the practice teaches you anything. Leaving the aim alone is uncomfortable for the first fortnight, because nothing happens and the not-happening is available as evidence. The record you made in the ten minutes after is the one that matters, because it was written before the interpretation could be built. If you set the review date in advance, then a week, you have a before-and-after pair, and the pair is the whole apparatus. Without the pair you have a feeling, and feelings are not a record.

The break that comes from aiming at a state

The sequence breaks at the aim more often than anywhere else, and the symptom is a charge that produces a strong experience and no usable information. The cause is usually an aim that is a state rather than an event, so there is no point at which the world differs. Feeling calmer is a state, and it is not nothing, but it cannot be checked against a record because you cannot have failed to feel calmer. The second common break is at the review, where the practitioner finds the record unreadable and does not know what they were aiming at, which usually means the aim was never written in the imperative in the first place. The third is at the charge, where a session longer than about twenty-five minutes reliably produces drift rather than depth.

There is one break that is not really a break. Practitioners frequently report that the sigil stopped meaning anything to them after a few weeks, and this is described as the method failing. It is more likely that the aim was solved, or the aim was never attached to the glyph in a way that survived the reduction, or the aim was replaced by a new one without making a new sigil. The remedy in all three cases is the same, which is to stop and write the current aim as a new sentence and make a new glyph, accepting that the old one did its job. A sigil that has been superseded is not a failure and does not need to be made to work.

The forgetting, and how to do it properly

A great deal of traditional advice in this area concerns deliberately forgetting the aim, on the reasonable ground that holding the statement in mind during the charge reintroduces the want and reduces the work to thinking about what you want. The reasoning is sound in one direction, which is that attention on the content is attention on the lack, and that a charge conducted on the lack is a form of rehearsal. The problem is that the forgetting is rarely carried past the first session, and a person who has forgotten an aim cannot review it, which removes the record.

The workable version is to separate the two functions in time rather than trying to abolish one of them. During the charge, the object is the glyph and the aim is not held in mind as a sentence. Immediately after, the aim is written down again from the notebook, before anything is interpreted, and this is what the review uses. What you are producing is a charge on the compressed form and a record on the full form, and neither is compromised by the other being in a different part of the session. Practitioners who work this way report that the forgetting is easier and the records are better, which is a rare combination of two things people usually assume are in tension.

The step worth protecting

If you keep only one part of this, keep the gap. The derivation, the drawing and the charge are all specifiable and can be learned from any competent source in an afternoon. The transition from marks to object is the part that has to be decided rather than learned, and it is the part that most determines what a record looks like six months later. A practitioner who has added a deliberate transition will have a legible set of records; a practitioner who has not will have a series of strong experiences and no way to compare them.

This is also the part that makes the practice yours rather than borrowed, and it costs nothing. Reading the aim aloud, looking for a full minute, tracing the line once: none of these is in any manual, all of them are available, and the fact that they are not in the manuals is a statement about what the manuals are for. They are for teaching the mechanical part, which is the part most people need. The rest is where you are on your own, and the people who do well in this field are not the ones who found the right technique. They are the ones who noticed, early, which of the steps were theirs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where does the letter reduction method come from?

A short pamphlet published in the early twentieth century by a practitioner who was also a writer, which presents a method for reducing a statement of intent to a glyph: strip to pronounced letters, map each to a short segment, join in order, close the shape. It is short, public, and includes worked examples. Knowing the source matters because it shows the operation is a compression procedure with rules rather than a symbolic art, and rules can be varied deliberately.

What is the step between drawing the glyph and charging it?

There is no standard step, which is why it is the interesting one. Most people cross from marks to object in a few seconds, during which the glyph is still just lines, and then begin the attention as though it had always been meaningful. Something has to convert a drawing into something worth holding. Reading the aim aloud, looking at the glyph for a full minute, and tracing the line once will do it, and none of those is magic.

Is using a digital sigil generator cheating?

It depends on what you delegate. Using one to compress a statement you have written yourself in the imperative is fine, because the compression is the mechanical part. Using one to go straight to a mark without ever writing the aim is not, because the statement is where the thinking happens. Generating three variants and choosing the easiest to draw accurately is a legitimate practice; generating thirty and picking the most beautiful one is an aesthetic choice, and the charge is then on a shape rather than a sentence.

How long should a charge be?

Short enough that you will repeat it, which for most people is ten to twenty minutes, and in one sitting rather than in instalments. An interrupted charge has a discontinuity that the mind reads as a boundary, so it becomes two shorter charges with a gap where the drift goes. If you have to stop, count it as stopped. Twenty clean short charges produce a usable record; a hundred interrupted ones do not, and the difference compounds over a year.

What does deliberately forgetting the aim actually do?

It is a reasonable response to the fact that holding the content in mind during a charge turns the work into rehearsal of a want. The difficulty is that the forgetting does not survive past the first session, and a forgotten aim cannot be reviewed, which removes the record. The workable version separates the functions in time: charge on the glyph with the aim not held as a sentence, then write the aim again from the notebook immediately after, and use that for the review.
