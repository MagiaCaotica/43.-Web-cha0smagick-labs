Time and Causality

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

Time work is the least examined corner of practical magic and the most reliably misunderstood. It attracts claims about acceleration, retroactive change and reordered chronology that would be unremarkable in fiction and are corrosive as practice. What survives examination is considerably narrower and more useful: attention to sequence, honest duration, and a particular kind of patience most people have never practised. This page works through the material in the order a beginner can actually use it, spends some time on what the popular literature gets wrong about physics, and ends with a working sequence you could run this week without buying anything at all. If the sequence is all you take from it, that is a good outcome, because the sequence is the part that survives complete scepticism about the rest.

The part of time practice that is not time practice

Most practical time work is a sequence in disguise. Every instruction that looks like it is about timing turns out, on inspection, to be about establishing an order of operations and holding to it. The candle, the order of the words, the interval between the charge and the dismissal. That is the component with actual payoff.

This sounds like a deflationary reading, and it is meant to be one. If the useful part of a practice is disciplined sequencing, then the practice is worth doing for reasons that survive complete scepticism about the rest, which is a considerably better position than being committed to claims that cannot be evaluated.

Subjective time is a measurement problem

Time perception is elastic in well-documented ways. Attention narrows it, boredom stretches it, threat compresses it. Retrieval practice confirms that elapsed time is reconstructed rather than stored, which means the sense of how long ago something happened is a reconstruction and can be influenced by how you attended to it at the time.

This is not an argument that anything can be done with time. It is an argument that the experiential side of temporal claims is unusually susceptible to attention effects, so practitioners should be suspicious of conclusions drawn from how long something felt like it took.

What the physics material gets wrong

The observer effect in quantum mechanics concerns measurement of microscopic systems and has no bearing on macroscopic practical causation. Popular occult writing frequently conflates the two, usually in service of the claim that concentrated attention alters material reality.

The honest statement is that attention alters human behaviour, and human behaviour is the only channel through which any result about your circumstances could plausibly arrive. That channel is well documented and unremarkable. A mechanism that skips it requires evidence nobody has produced, and a claim that does not need the evidence is a claim that will not change your behaviour in any useful way.

Duration, and why the instructions avoid clock time

Traditional duration is usually specified in units other than minutes: candle lengths, repetitions, tides, lunar phases, a full watch. That is a structuring device rather than a metaphysical claim, and it works because it makes the duration independent of your availability.

A ritual specified in candle lengths can be performed at three in the morning. A ritual specified in minutes will be skipped on the days you have the least time, and the days you have the least time are exactly the days the practice would have helped most. Units that survive a bad week are worth preferring for that reason alone.

  • Candle lengths, which run to a natural end and require no clock
  • Repetition counts, which scale to whatever time you actually have
  • Lunar phase boundaries, which are fixed and require no personal scheduling
  • Minimum durations, which establish a floor without demanding an exact figure

Step one, name the situation precisely

Begin by writing a description of the situation that could be checked by someone else. Not a state of mind, not an outcome in the abstract, but a condition with a start and an end.

The test is whether two people reading your sentence would agree about whether it had been met. If they would not, the sentence is still an aspiration, and aspirations cannot be tracked or learned from. This step takes longer than it should, which is itself information about the clarity of the situation.

Step two, decide the order before deciding the content

Write the sequence of steps as a numbered list, in order, with nothing else in it. Do not add invocations or specifications yet. You are producing a procedure, not a script.

The reason to do this first is that content decisions feel like progress and are usually avoidance. A workable order is decided in an afternoon. A beautiful ritual that you cannot reconstruct from memory in the right order is worth less than a plain one you can.

  1. Define the situation in a sentence a stranger could check
  2. Write the numbered procedure with no content beyond the steps
  3. Choose duration units that survive a week when you have no time
  4. Set the review date before you perform anything, not after

Step three, build the interval deliberately

The gap between doing something and evaluating it does real work, and almost everyone sets it wrong. Too short and you read noise as signal. Too long and the evaluation drifts into retrospective construction, where you can produce an account of why it worked before you knew it had.

Pick the interval before you start, write it in the log, and do not revisit it. A two-week gap is long enough to escape the emotional state of the working and short enough that your memory of the conditions is still accurate.

Step four, perform the working and log it the same day

The log entry is a small discipline with an outsized effect. Date, what was done, what the stated condition was, whether anything notable happened, and whether anything notable failed to happen. Five fields. Two minutes.

The field that matters most is the fourth, because the bias in retrospective recall runs entirely toward the memorable. Writing a null on the same day as the event is the only thing that stops your record from becoming a highlight reel with dates on it.

Step five, review on the date you chose

At the review, compare the stated condition against what actually happened, in writing, and allow a verdict of no. A procedure that produces a null is information, and a procedure that never produces one is probably not being reviewed honestly.

If the condition was met, resist the temptation to attribute it to the working. Ask instead what else changed, whether you acted differently because of the practice, and whether the change would have happened anyway. The answer is often yes, and writing that down is what makes the next run informative.

The claim about acceleration, examined

Accelerated manifestation is a strong claim about the relationship between intention and outcome, and it is not supported by anything that would survive a controlled test. There is no reason to expect a symbolic procedure to compress a causal chain, and no documented case where it did outside of a self-report.

What does work is a different thing people describe with the same words. Committing to a sequence, and being the sort of person who follows through on a sequence, compresses real intervals. Waiting becomes shorter because you stopped spending it deliberating. The effect is attributable, measurable, and considerably less dramatic than the claim being sold.

Retroactive intention, and why it is worth leaving alone

Practices that claim to change a past event are the most attractive and the most corrosive. They are attractive because the imagined version of the past is more pleasant, and corrosive because the imagining is indistinguishable, from the inside, from the feeling of having done the work.

There is also a straightforward problem with testing them: a change in the past cannot be checked against the past, so any outcome is confirmable and no outcome is disconfirmable. A practice you cannot falsify should not be doing the heavy lifting in your system. Keep the effort on the part of the timeline you can still see.

A month of working, with the review built in

Run one situation for a month at a modest frequency rather than four situations at a high one. The review points are fixed at the start: one mid-point and one end point, both written down before the first working.

At the mid-point, check the log rather than the situation. The question is whether you are still performing on schedule, because adherence is the variable that determines whether the end-point review will mean anything. A missed week is a data point about your schedule, not a reason to increase the intensity.

  1. Week one, write the condition, the procedure and both review dates
  2. Weeks two to four, perform at the stated frequency and log each time
  3. Week two mid-point, audit the log for schedule rather than results
  4. Week four, the verdict, including no, written in the same format as the log

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a time sigil actually for, if it cannot change the past?

It is for committing to a sequence and a review date, which is the part of time work that has observable effects. A sigil stating that you will do something on a particular schedule and then check on a particular day converts an intention into a record with a deadline. The imagery of time is the packaging; the mechanism is commitment plus review, and both of those are worth the effort.

Is quantum mechanics the same thing as magic?

No, and the confusion causes real practical harm because it licenses claims that cannot be tested. The observer effect concerns measurement of microscopic systems. Nothing in it describes human behaviour, intention, or outcomes, and no experiment bridges that gap. What is documented is that attention changes what people do, which is a completely ordinary mechanism available to any practice regardless of what you believe about physics.

Can intention make something manifest faster?

Not in the sense the claim usually means, and the request is worth decomposing rather than dismissing. If what you want is a decision made, the delay is often deliberation rather than cosmic resistance, and writing the decision into an imperative sentence with a review date shortens it. If what you want is an external circumstance to change, no documented method will shorten it, and any that appears to is producing a change you made yourself without recognising it as yours.

What do I gain by paying for a lunar calendar app?

The honest answer is that most of it buys scheduling and record-keeping, and both of those are easy to do without paying. A timer, a paper log and a date in a calendar cover the entire method. What money can add is a durable record that survives your attention lapses, reminders that fire when you have forgotten the schedule, and a review interface that shows you your own history. Those are real benefits for someone who has failed at the free version, and a poor reason to buy anything if you have not.

What should I do if a working produces a result I did not want?

Write it down as a result rather than discarding it, because an unwanted outcome carries information about the procedure and the expectation. Then check the review date you set rather than the one you feel like using now, and resist the urge to reinterpret on the spot. Most unwanted results in practice come from a condition that was worded loosely enough to be met by accident, which is a drafting problem with a clear fix for the next version.
