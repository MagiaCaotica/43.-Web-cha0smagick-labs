Lunar and Planetary Magic

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Lunar and planetary timing is the part of occult practice that survives contact with an astronomer, which makes it a strange place to begin. The moon's phase is a real, observable variable, the seven-day week carries a genuine planetary history, and the division of the day into twelve themed segments is an old and coherent piece of structure. What is less solid is the claim that these windows change what a working can do. This guide walks through both halves: what the timing genuinely is, what it plausibly does, and how to find out for yourself without buying anything. It is written for someone at the very start, because the first-timer's real problem is not technique. It is sorting the observable part from the promised part, and doing it before the promises become your reasons for practising.

What the moon phase is actually telling you

The moon's phase is the proportion of its lit face visible from Earth, and that is a measurement rather than a symbol. It correlates with illumination of the surface and with the timing of moonrise and moonset, both of which vary across the month and across latitudes. Any system built on that variable is building on something real, which is a better foundation than most occult timing systems manage.

What the phase is not is a code. There is no mechanism by which a waxing crescent knows anything about beginnings, and nobody has demonstrated one. The association between waxing and beginning is a cultural mapping onto an astronomical cycle, and it is a very old and very widespread one. Treating it as a mapping is fine. Treating it as a causal link is a step the sources do not support.

The distinction matters because it changes what you expect from a timed working. If the timing is a mapping, then it functions as a prompt: a regular point at which you stop and do the work, which is genuinely valuable for anyone whose problem is not remembering. If it is a cause, you are expecting a difference in result that no experiment has found. The first expectation is achievable and the second is not.

The week you are already using

The seven-day week with named days is not a natural fact and it is not arbitrary either. It traces back to Babylonian and Roman practice, mapping the days onto the Chaldean order of the planets, and the modern names survive that mapping almost intact. Sunday is the sun, Monday the moon, Tuesday Mars, and so on through to Saturn on Saturday.

You are using a planetary calendar. That is worth knowing not because it confers anything, but because it means the day you happen to do your work on already carries an association that most people in the field have never chosen on purpose. If you have always worked on Saturdays you have been working on Saturn's day, and if you would rather work on a different one you have not been restricted from changing.

The practical use is a scheduling constraint that makes the practice easier to keep. One day a week, named, is a smaller promise than a daily one, and a kept weekly practice beats an abandoned daily one every time. The planetary frame is a good way to choose the day, since there are only seven and you can just try them.

Planetary hours, and how the day gets divided

Planetary hours divide daylight and night separately into twelve segments each, giving twenty-four in a day, with the ruling spirit rotating hour to hour through a fixed Chaldean order. The system is inherited from medieval astrology and it has a clear logic: the day is divided rather than averaged, so the first hour of sunrise and the first hour of sunset are treated differently from the noon hour.

The complication is that these hours are not clock hours. They vary in length with season and latitude, which is why tables for them look strange to anyone expecting a neat 24-hour grid. If you use this, use a table rather than working it out, and accept that the boundaries shift through the year.

The honest account of what they are for is structure. A day divided into themed windows gives you a slot for a particular kind of work, and a slot is the thing most people actually lack. Whether Mars's hour makes you bolder is not established. That you will have done the work at a fixed point on a fixed rotation is not in doubt, and that is worth a fair amount on its own.

The part that is real, and the part that is focus

Two effects are operating when you work on a schedule keyed to the sky, and they are worth pulling apart. The first is a very small real increase in the probability of favourable coincidence, because you have restricted when you act to a subset of times, and some opportunities only exist at particular times. This is arithmetic, not magic, and it is small enough that you will not notice it directly.

The second is much larger and much less mysterious. Working to a timetable reliably increases practitioner focus, because a timetable removes the decision. You no longer have to decide whether to do the work today, which removes the point at which most practices quietly stop. The gain comes from the schedule rather than the sky, and it is the more significant effect by a wide margin.

Both of these are ordinary, well-understood behavioural effects that any scheduled practice produces. That is not a criticism. It means the practice can be honestly described and honestly improved, and it means the claims you need to hold at arm's length are a smaller part of the whole than the internet suggests.

First contact, without buying anything

You do not need anything for a first lunar working. A calendar with the phases marked, a private hour, and a piece of paper for the record are sufficient. The phases are visible and can be read off any sky on a clear night, and most people can tell a waxing crescent from a waning one without help.

What the first working should be is small. Pick something with a visible outcome, in a shorter window than feels generous, so that the review has something to review. Something you do rather than something you feel. Then record the date, the phase, the hour and what you did, and stop there.

The temptation in a first working is to add apparatus, because apparatus is available and it feels like progress. It is not. A working performed with no equipment and kept in a notebook teaches you more about whether the method works on you than an elaborate version you cannot attribute, because the version with fewer variables is the one you can learn from.

Timing a first working by phase

The conventional mapping is straightforward enough to state in four lines, and it is the standard that most people mean when they say lunar timing. The new moon sits with endings and beginnings, the waxing phase with starting things and building, the full moon with culmination, visibility and anything brought to a peak, and the waning phase with transformation, release and clearing out.

For money specifically the common advice is to begin on the waxing, treat the full moon as the point of maximum effect, use the waning for turning things around, and reserve the new moon for letting go of a project that has stopped being worth doing. That is coherent and it matches the cycle's actual shape, with one month of growth, one peak and one decline.

Notice what this mapping is: an argument from analogy with a natural cycle, not a claim about how the moon affects money. It is a good argument for structuring your work. It is not a reason to delay a working because the sky is wrong, and anyone who tells you otherwise has a stake in you buying something.

  • New moon, endings and fresh starts, and a good moment to stop or begin a cycle
  • Waxing phase, opening things up and building steadily through the first half of the month
  • Full moon, the peak of visibility, when anything brought to a head tends to arrive
  • Waning phase, transformation, release and clearing out what no longer earns its place

What to do when the timing does not suit the work

Sometimes the phase that suits the work has already passed. A full moon arrives and the thing you have been putting off is not going to wait twelve days for a waxing sky. The correct response is to do the work now and record the phase you did it in, because a practice that has been waiting for ideal conditions is a practice that has not happened.

The same applies in reverse. A beginner who works only at the full moon will do four workings a year, which is too few to learn anything from. Working at a fixed point in the cycle, any point, gives you the repetition that makes a method learnable. The specific phase is the smaller variable.

Where the timing does earn its keep is in sequencing. If you have a multi-step thing, putting the opening on the waxing and the closing on the waning gives the work a shape that mirrors the cycle and makes the stages easy to remember. That is a real gain and it is worth an hour of planning.

Birth charts, and the part you can skip

You do not need a birth chart to do lunar or planetary work, and this is the single clearest place where beginner advice adds cost without adding function. The planetary day and the lunar phase are both knowable from a calendar. Neither requires anything about your particular configuration.

A birth chart becomes relevant only when you move into a system where the timing is calculated relative to a specific chart, and even then it is worth checking whether the extra precision changes anything you can detect. For most people, adding a chart to a practise that was working fine on phase and weekday adds a layer of calculation without adding a layer of effect.

If you are curious, calculate one. It is an interesting exercise and it will show you something about your own patterns. Just do not treat it as a prerequisite, and do not let anybody present it as one, because a practice with a high entry cost is a practice most people abandon before it can be evaluated.

A record that shows whether timing mattered

If you want to know whether the phase does anything, the only route is a record, and the record has to be designed for it. Log the phase, the weekday, the hour, what you did and what came of it. Do this for a full cycle minimum, and for several cycles if you can manage it, since a single month cannot distinguish a pattern from a coincidence.

Then look at something the practitioners who claim strong effects never do, which is the empty days. A log of contact only will confirm any system you start with, and the empty entries are the ones carrying information about whether the timing is doing anything. If results cluster in the full moon and never in the waning, that is a finding. If they are evenly scattered, that is also a finding, and an honest one.

You will probably find that the schedule mattered and the phase did not. That is a defensible, useful conclusion, and it is far better than the alternative of never having looked and therefore being able to believe anything at all about the question.

The habit worth keeping after the novelty

Lunar practice is at its best as a scaffold and at its worst as a source of permission. The scaffold version is the one where the cycle marks the month, giving you a review point and a fresh start every time round, which is genuinely how long projects get finished. The permission version is the one where the sky is treated as a condition for being allowed to act, and that turns a calendar into an excuse.

The most useful thing this material can give a beginner is a regular appointment with your own attention, attached to something outside your control. That is what it is actually for, and it is not a small thing. People who never find this material still arrange their week this way, with a diary and a fixed hour, and they get most of the same result.

Keep the calendar, keep the record, and let the claims shrink over time as your own data accumulates. A practice that you have stopped needing to believe in order to keep doing is in a much stronger position than one that still depends on the belief holding.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to know my birth chart?

No. The lunar phase is knowable from a calendar and the planetary day is knowable from the date, so neither requires anything about your particular configuration. A chart becomes relevant only in systems that calculate timing relative to one, and even there it is worth checking whether the extra precision changes anything you can actually detect.

Which moon phase is best for money?

Waxing for starting something, full for the point of culmination, waning for turning things around, and new for letting a project go. That is an argument from analogy with a natural cycle rather than a claim about how the moon affects money, and it is a good reason to sequence your work. It is not a reason to wait, since a working deferred until the right sky is a working that does not happen.

What are planetary hours used for?

They divide the day and night separately into twelve segments each, with the ruling spirit rotating through a fixed Chaldean order, which gives you a themed slot for each kind of work. The segments are not clock hours and their lengths shift with season and latitude, so use a table. The real function is structural: a slot is the thing most people lack.

What does a paid lunar calendar app actually give me?

Precision and convenience, which are real but small. A free source gives you the phase and the date, and both are also visible in the sky. What you pay for is exact rise and set times, planetary hour boundaries, a history you can scroll back through, and a record you do not have to keep by hand. Worth it once you are tracking several cycles rather than your first.

How do I tell whether the timing is doing anything?

Keep a record long enough to distinguish a pattern from a coincidence, which means at least one full cycle and preferably several. Log the phase, the weekday and the hour along with what you did and what came of it, and write the empty days too, since those are the entries carrying the information. You may find the schedule mattered and the phase did not, which is a usable answer.
