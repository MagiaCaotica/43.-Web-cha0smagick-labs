Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

The full sequence, written as a procedure rather than a description. Chaos magic is unusual among modern occult traditions in that it was designed to be learned rather than received, and that design has a practical consequence: the method is a sequence of small acts in a fixed order, and the order is the part people get wrong. The movement began in the early seventies, with the OTO founded in 1976, is the conventional starting point. Two short texts set the method rather than the doctrine, one by Carroll in 1977 and one by Crowley. Step one, choose a target that could be checked

Write one sentence as a command, with a quantity or a date inside it. The imperative is not a stylistic preference; it changes what you are committing to. The number or date is the part that fails most often. Almost everyone arrives with a goal that has no quantity in it, and the reduction of a vague target produces a glyph for a mood rather than a target, which is worth knowing before you start rather than afterwards.

Step two, reduce it, and keep encoding separate

Drop the vowels, keep the consonants, arrange what is left into a shape you like. Draw it badly and quickly. The method is documented in Austin Osman Spare's 1904 Book of Gates and the resulting glyph is a compression of the sentence rather than a picture of it.

Then stop. Do not charge it yet. Keeping the encoding and the activation as two separate acts is the most useful single feature of the method, because it means the second one can be done once and the first one can be revised without muddying anything.

Step three, pick the moment and the instrument

Most of the tradition's advice about timing amounts to this: the clarity of the target matters more than the accuracy of the calendar. A carefully chosen hour aimed at a vague target underperforms an arbitrary one aimed at something you could act on this week.

The instrument should be something that will hold still afterwards. A piece of paper, a coin, a small stone. Not something on your person, because the whole point of the next step depends on not being able to reach it.

  1. The sentence, in the imperative, with a number or a date.
  2. The reduction, drawn by hand, as fast as you can.
  3. The instrument, chosen so it can be put somewhere you will not touch it.
  4. The deadline, fixed before the charge and not moved afterwards.

Step four, the charge, kept short

Concentrate on the sentence rather than the glyph. A minute is usually enough and twenty minutes is usually a sign that you are trying to convince yourself rather than perform an act. The charge is a declaration, not a negotiation.

Then dismiss it. Not gently, not with lingering doubt. The charge and the dismissal are a pair, and a charge without a dismissal tends to leave you attached to the object, which is the mechanism behind most of the accounts of sigil backfire in the popular material.

Step five, put it out of reach

Somewhere specific, ideally outside the room you are in, and then do not look at it for the length of your observation window. Nearly everybody skips this stage, and it is the stage that makes the practice function at all.

An object you keep inspecting supplies daily anxiety that reads as incompleteness, and you will start re-deriving what it meant, which is the opposite of the intended direction of travel. The window you chose in step three is the window you keep.

Step six, log the entry the same day

One line. What you did, what you expected, and when. Written in the evening, on the day it happened, while you still remember what you actually thought rather than what you have since decided you thought.

This is the least interesting step and the only one that makes the rest knowable. A practice without a record produces no knowledge at all, only a sequence of impressions that get edited every time you retell them.

Step seven, the review on the date you fixed

Compare what you wrote in step one against what actually happened, and write the conclusion before you re-read the individual entries. Reversing that order lets each entry negotiate with the conclusion, which is where honest-seeming records quietly go wrong.

Then run it again, differently. The second run should have a different target, because a second attempt at the same target is a second sample of the same thing rather than a new piece of information.

Where the sequence usually breaks, and why

The order of the steps is the part people get wrong, and the reason is that the tempting order is different. The intuitive sequence is reduce, charge, check, repeat. The working one is target, reduce, charge, dismiss, put away, log, review. Checking comes after you have put it away, not before.

The second common break is editing the sentence after a disappointing week. The reduction and the charge are the only protection against that, which is why they are separate acts, and the only real protection is the written record from step one. A sigil is not a wish, and the difference shows up in how the sentence is written, which is why the whole method begins with writing rather than with drawing.

What the founders were actually proposing

The movement took its name from chaos magic and from the figure of Chaos on the throne in the Book of the Law, and treated the resemblance as a deliberate claim rather than a borrowed piece of vocabulary. What makes the movement unusual is not the metaphysics, which is thin, but the insistence that claims be tested rather than of the Law, and the movement treated the affiliation as intentional. The conventional founding date is 1976. What makes the movement unusual is not the metaphysics, which is thin, but the insistence that claims be tested rather than inherited.

That contribution is methodological and it has outlasted the theory it came with. A person can run this whole sequence and keep every result, whatever they believe, and the record they end up with is better than one produced by any comparable method that asks for belief first.

The whole sequence in one page

One sentence, imperative, with a number or a date. One reduction, drawn by hand. One instrument, chosen so it can be put away. One charge of about a minute, then a dismissal. One log line the same evening. One review on the date you fixed before the charge, and one second run with a different target.

That is the entire method. Everything the corpus adds on top is optional, and almost all of it is more satisfying than the bookkeeping, which is the reason it is so popular and the reason the sequence is so often abandoned.

What the charge actually is, and why it is short

The traditional description of the charge is a focusing of attention on the statement of intent, and the traditional instruction is that it should be brief. The reason for the brevity is not economy, it is that a charge is an act with a shape rather than a state to be achieved.

Most people who extend it are trying to feel something particular, which is a different activity that happens to involve the same objects. The act of reducing a sentence, of drawing it, of speaking it once and putting it away is the whole of the method, and everything after that is repetition dressed as progress.

Reduction methods, and why the choice matters less than it looks

The standard method drops vowels and keeps consonants. There are variants that also fold letters together, and some that begin from the letters of the statement in a fixed order such as first and last. The documented method comes from Spare's 1904 Book of Gates.

The choice changes the glyph and not much else. What matters is that you use one consistently, because a record that mixes methods cannot be compared across entries, and comparison across entries is the only reason to keep a reduction log at all.

  • The standard method: vowels out, consonants kept, arranged by hand.
  • Letter folding, for those who prefer a more compact result.
  • First-and-last letter methods, which produce a different shape from the same sentence.
  • A drawing layer on top, which changes nothing and satisfies something.

The dismissal, which is the part that gets skipped

After the charge you dismiss. The action is deliberate and it should feel like an end rather than a release, because the object has been declared and is no longer in relationship with you. People who skip it tend to report the sigil as unfinished, which is usually an accurate description of what happened.

The physical move that reinforces it is putting the object somewhere you cannot reach it from, ideally out of the room. That is the mechanism behind the traditional advice about not carrying a charged sigil on your person, and it is a piece of attention hygiene rather than a superstition. None of it requires belief in an external being, which is the part that makes the sequence usable by somebody who would find a theology embarrassing.

Timing, and the honest version of the advice

The tradition's guidance is that clarity of target beats accuracy of calendar, and the practical reason is that the effort spent computing a date is effort not spent making the sentence specific. A perfect hour on a vague aim produces less than an arbitrary one on something you could act on this week.

If you want to work with timing anyway, fix the sentence first and choose the date afterwards, treating it as a useful commitment device rather than as a cause. The moment you start computing transits before the target is decided, the calculation has become a way of not deciding, and it will feel exactly like diligence while it happens.

A record that would survive an audit

Six fields, and the sixth is the one that is always left out. Date, sentence, reduction, charge date, pre-registered prediction, deadline, and then what actually happened, including anything that does not fit.

If the prediction is not written before the charge it is not a prediction. If the deadline is not fixed in advance it will move, and it will move without your noticing, which is the single most common way an honest-seeming log turns into a flattering one. Write both on the same page as the sentence and sign the page with the date.

When to stop, and what to do instead

Stop when the record stops producing information, which happens when you start running the same target repeatedly, or when the practice has started displacing the ordinary decisions it was meant to support. Neither of those is a failure of the method and both are reasons to change what you are doing rather than to intensify.

The tradition's own position is a short fixed routine rather than a climbing one, and that is a piece of practical advice about attention rather than a mystical preference. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the very first thing to do when starting from nothing?

Write one sentence, in the imperative, containing a number or a date. Nothing else is needed to begin and nothing else is required to continue. Buying equipment, learning correspondences and choosing an auspicious hour are all things people do instead of that sentence, and they are all ways of postponing it.

Do I have to understand what the sigil means to charge it?

No, and the fact that you do not is the design. The glyph is a compression of a sentence you have already written and already understand, and it is charged by holding that sentence in mind rather than by studying the drawing. The traditional advice is to concentrate on the intent rather than the symbol, which is also the better advice.

How long should a sigil be charged for?

About a minute, and the reason is that the charge is an act rather than a meditation. Anything longer is usually a sign that you are trying to persuade yourself, and persuading is not the operation. Short and done is the traditional instruction and the tradition was right about it.

What if the deadline passes with nothing having happened?

That is a result, and the most common one. Write it down as an outcome rather than a failure of the method, and run a second cycle with a different target and the same procedure. The people who learn anything from this are almost never the ones whose first two attempts worked, and a null is only uninformative if you expected otherwise.

Is any of this worth paying for software to do?

The method is free and complete on paper, and that is not a marketing position, it is the actual state of the method. A purchase buys a place to keep the log and the date arithmetic, which is the half most people fail at, and a digital reduction if you would rather not do it by hand. If you already keep a disciplined notebook, nothing on sale improves it.
