Red Magic in Chaos Magick: What Survived, and Why It Vanished

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Chaos magick was named in 1972 and had already abandoned its sexual branch by then, and the abandonment is usually described as the founders being embarrassed. That is close to the truth and it leaves out the part that matters. The branch was not dropped because anyone tested it and found it wanting. It was dropped because of an organisational conflict, a generational change, and a decision by one man about what he wanted his book to contain. That leaves four claims stranded on the far side of the fence, and three of them are load-bearing for everything the tradition ended up doing well.

What red magic was before chaos magick arrived

Red magic in the western tradition is sexual magic, and the term itself is a nineteenth-century one built on a colour scheme: red for the passional and generative, white for the rational and solar. Crowley used it constantly and treated sex as a technical instrument rather than a sacrament. In Magick in Theory and Practice he writes about the union of the current with the king, which is a description of a circuit being closed rather than a description of an experience. The Ordo Templi Orientis under Crowley was explicit about sexual offices, and Liber IV dedicates long passages to them, and a succession of later OTO bodies kept the material in print and on the internet for decades.

None of that is chaos magick. It predates it by decades, and it comes with a lineage, a initiatory structure, and a set of authority claims about who is permitted to perform it. Austin Osman Spare, working in London before the First World War, produced no sexual material at all, and his own practice was contemplative and solitary. So by the time chaos magick was named, the sexual branch already belonged to a different institution with a different structure and a different claim about who may enter it.

The split, and what actually caused it

The concrete event is documented and it is not a doctrinal argument about whether sex generates power. Peter Carroll was a member of the OTO and he broke with it over its sexual practices, and he was expelled in 1969 after publishing a letter making his criticism public. The criticisms he made were about the organisation and its leadership, not only about the rites themselves. Four years later he and Stephen Flowers put out Liber Null and Psychick, in which sexual practice is simply absent, and neither founder of the subsequent chaos magick scene made it a central concern.

That sequence produces a misleading picture if you read it as a considered verdict. What actually happened is that one man decided what his new book should contain, and the absence of sexual material from chaos magick is downstream of that single decision rather than a position the tradition examined and rejected. Generations of writers have since read the absence as principled. It is more honest to say it was editorial, and that the material was never tested and then discarded, because a discarded hypothesis and an untested one leave identical evidence behind and very different lessons.

A parallel claim to the same name, and why it is not chaos magick

Between roughly 1970 and 1980 a different group was using the word chaos for something else. The Temple ov Psychick Youth, founded by Genesis P-Orridge, fused ritual, sex, altered states, music and an explicit refusal of the mainstream in a single practice, and it circulated its ideas through zines, tapes and later the internet. The word chaos was in wide use among British punk and industrial musicians and their audience years before chaos magick was noticed by the same people. William Bennett, who founded the Industrial Culture department of the(OTo) in New York, drew on both traditions, and his own account of where his practice came from names the punk and occult scene rather than Carroll.

So when someone tells you there are two chaos magicks, a sexual one and a dry one, the accurate statement is that there are two lineages that share a word. The punk one treats chaos as a stance toward the culture that surrounds you. The Carroll one treats chaos as the entire domain of everything not governed by a declared order, which is an older sense of the word and closer to Empedocles than to a guitar. Neither is a corruption of the other. If you are looking for practices with a documented prehistory that includes sex as central, you are looking at a different tradition and it would be wrong to call it chaos magick in the Carrollian sense.

The first survivor: attention is the scarce resource

Strip away the metaphysics and the strongest thing the sexual branch claimed is an instrumental one. Sustained high-resolution attention on a single object, held without drift, is difficult, exhausting, and improves with practice. Sexual activity is an unusually efficient machine for producing that state, which is why so many contemplative traditions have borrowed imagery from it, and why the vocabulary of union and gnosis keeps turning up in systems that never mention sex at all.

This claim survives intact without the apparatus. Any demanding concentration practice supplies the same thing, which is why the surviving chaos magick canon is full of methods that feel more like concentration training than like ritual: writing the intent down, designing a method with a test, holding to a date, reviewing the result. Those are attention exercises with dates attached. If you want the red branch, take the instrument rather than the claim about what it produces.

The second survivor: consent belongs in the design, not the etiquette

This is the survivor with the best track record, and it is worth being precise about why. Every serious failure documented in the OTO and in the punk scene involved a boundary that had been written down in advance by one party and assumed by the other. The 1986 to 1988 case brought against the OTO in Britain was not about theology, and the later Honest History Project documents the pattern rather than denying it. Whatever one concludes about the rites themselves, the record is unambiguous about the design failure: a practice that involves other people and has no consent procedure in its own structure will eventually find someone who did not agree.

Chaos magick's most durable inheritance is not its sigils, which many traditions share, and not its attitude, which is borrowed. It is that a working is specified. You write the function, the trigger, the refusal and the review. A practice built on that habit, extended to any working that touches another person, produces a consent structure for free. The red branch lost this and never had it. The version that survives the split is the design principle, and applying it to other people is a better practice than any of the material that was dropped.

The third survivor: seriousness tracks taboo, not effect size

Practices that are forbidden feel more consequential than practices that are merely available, and there is a good reason for that which has nothing to do with the supernatural. A boundary tells the mind that something is at stake, and a thing marked as costly recruits more attention than a thing marked as optional. This is a well documented property of motivation, and it explains a great deal about why ceremonial magic feels weightier than chaos magick and why both feel more serious than a checklist.

The implication is deflating and useful. If seriousness is partly manufactured by the prohibition, then the prohibition is a tool rather than a truth, and a practice that anyone may do at any time is not thereby trivial. A beginner can build the same quality of attention by choosing a working they actually care about, writing it down properly, and putting a date on the review, without adopting anything that is dangerous or socially costly. What you lose is the borrowed gravity. What you keep is the attention, which is the part that was doing the work.

The fourth survivor: intensity is not a causal claim

This is the survivor that is really a warning. The argument that sex produces magical power is an argument from intensity, and intensity is evidence about the observer rather than about the world. The same substitution runs through a lot of popular magic: an intense experience is taken as proof that an external thing happened, when it is equally consistent with a large change in the person having it. The red branch is where this error is easiest to see, because the experience is real and the conclusion drawn from it is not supported by anything but the experience.

You do not need the sexual material to notice this, and noticing it makes you better at everything else. The useful discipline is a single question asked after any strong experience: what would have had to happen in the world, as opposed to in me, for this to be the result of what I did? A practice that cannot answer that is doing something, but it is not doing what you think it is doing, and the two cases call for different amounts of scepticism.

What does not survive, stated plainly

Four things from the branch have no defensible residue. The first is that sex generates a substance that can be directed, which requires a mechanism nobody has specified and which the tradition itself never described in operational terms. The second is that particular anatomy confers power, which is a claim about the body that no evidence supports and that would be embarrassing to write down in a serious technical manual. The third is that permissiveness is a requirement, which is a claim about who may be present rather than about what a practice does, and it is also the claim that most directly enables the abuse the tradition's own history records. The fourth is that the practice works better when it is not discussed, which is false as a general statement and appears to be true mainly about practices whose effects are consensual.

Notice that the fourth is the one most likely to be defended, because it sounds like a mystery and mysteries are flattering. It is also the one that produced the organisational rot. A practice nobody may describe is a practice nobody may correct, and the reason the OTO and the punk scene both ended in litigation and in decades of unfalsifiable claims is not that secrecy is powerful but that secrecy is unfalsifiable and the people inside it eventually stopped being able to tell the difference.

Five ways the sexual branch gets taken out of context

The first is borrowing a framework without its consent design. Reading the historical material and adopting the intensity without the agreement structure produces a practice that is more intense and less safe than either the original or the version chaos magick actually kept. The second is treating intensity as evidence, which is the error in its purest form and the one the tradition's history should have taught. The third is using someone's sexuality as a resource without asking, which is not a grey area and does not become one by being framed as practice.

The fourth is the purity spiral. Removing sexual material from a tradition did not make it more serious, it made it more priggish, and a lot of what circulates as austere chaos magick is the direct result of this. The fifth is importing a practice whose only evidence is the testimony of the people who did it, which is a reasonable place to start and an unreasonable place to stop, because the participants are exactly the people with the strongest reasons to be satisfied.

What to build instead, and what it costs

If you want the tradable version of what the red branch was reaching for, it costs almost nothing. Pick one aim you actually care about. Write it as a single imperative sentence that a stranger could check. Write down what you will do about it if the review date arrives and nothing has happened, before the date rather than on it. Set a date. Then run something that makes the date a real constraint rather than a note, which usually means a physical record you will be embarrassed to falsify. That is the whole method and every claim in the red branch that survives scrutiny is a description of something in it.

The tradition's own catalogue is worth adding to that rather than replacing it. Liber Null and Psychick is the shortest serious manual in the field and it costs less than a takeaway, and Codex Chaoticus is the longer treatment for when the short one is no longer enough. Beyond that the honest accounting is that free tools cover the mechanics of a sigil and a chart, the expensive half of this work is your own attention, and any course or service selling you entry to a lineage is selling a thing the practice does not contain.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sexual magic part of chaos magick?

No, and it never really was. Chaos magick was named in 1972 and its foundational manual contains no sexual material at all. What you are thinking of is either the Crowleyan tradition that preceded it by decades, or the punk and industrial scene that used the word chaos for a different thing in roughly the same years.

Why did chaos magick abandon sexual practices?

Because the founder broke with the organisation that practised them, over organisational and leadership issues, and then wrote a book without them. That is an editorial decision rather than a tested conclusion, which is why people argue about it: no experiment was run and no evidence was gathered. A dropped hypothesis and an untested one leave identical records behind.

Did the sexual rites actually work?

Nobody knows, and the honest position is that nobody knows because the tradition never set up a way to find out. The participants report strong experiences, which is evidence about the participants. The most useful thing the history offers is not an answer about power but a documented pattern of boundary failure, which is a real finding about the design of the practice rather than about its effects.

Is there anything worth taking from the tradition?

Three things, all of which survive without any sexual content. Attention is the scarce resource and demanding concentration is how you get more of it. Consent belongs in the specification of a practice rather than in its manners. And seriousness is partly manufactured by prohibition, which makes it a tool rather than a measure. The fourth thing worth taking is the warning: intensity is not evidence about the world.

Does chaos magick require sexual practice to be effective?

No, and the ones who say otherwise are making a claim about a mechanism they cannot specify. The practice works, to the extent that it does anything, through attention, specificity, a date, and a review, and none of those four have anything to do with sex. If your practice feels ineffective, the productive questions are about the specification and the review, not about what you have left out of it.
