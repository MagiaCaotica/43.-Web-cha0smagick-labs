Dreams, Astral Projection and Tulpas

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Three practices get bundled together under the phrase astral dreaming, and separating them changes what a person can reasonably expect to get out of practice. Lucid dreaming is a documented sleep phenomenon with a measurable research base and reliable induction methods. Astral projection, or the out-of-body experience, is reported by a small minority of people and tends to arrive unbidden, often near sleep onset or during illness, rather than on command. The tulpa belongs to a third tradition entirely, coming out of Tibetan Buddhist visualisation rather than Western ceremony. Treating the three as one discipline produces muddled results and a lot of confusion about what went wrong. This guide takes them one at a time, describes what a working session with each actually looks like, and stays explicit about the failures.

What a lucid dream feels like from inside

The reliable description is boring, which is part of why beginners distrust it. You are asleep, you notice you are asleep, and the dream keeps running with an extra layer of commentary attached. The dreamer in the dream can think, hold an intention, and occasionally answer questions put to them later. Most reports describe the same handful of features: text that will not stay legible, mirrors that lag, light switches that do nothing, and a sense that the place was built from whatever was nearest at hand.

What changes on practice is control, and control arrives in small increments. Awareness without control is common. Controlling one object, like switching on a light, takes longer. Moving something deliberately, opening a door, changing the scene, all come later. Reports of flying, altering scenery, or meeting people rarely happen early. A practitioner expecting cinematic control in the first month is measuring the wrong variable, and usually concludes the skill is fake.

Why the last hour of sleep carries the most yield

REM periods are not spread evenly across a night. They start short, recur roughly every ninety minutes, and lengthen toward morning, so the final hour of sleep holds the longest dream episodes. Waking at that point and holding still for twenty minutes produces lucid dreams more often than the classic alarm method that jolts you upright. The grogginess that makes the alarm method unpleasant is also what keeps you in the hypnagogic zone where the dream is still reachable.

Set an alarm for ninety minutes before your normal wake time, not for it. When it sounds, do not stand, do not check the phone, do not try to reconstruct the dream in detail. Close your eyes and go back down. Most people report one lucid episode per session this way inside a fortnight. The failure mode is impatience, the same impatience that makes people leap out of bed and destroy the state they were trying to catch.

The induction that survives repetition

The technique that holds up best is a short verbal reminder repeated on a fixed schedule through the day, paired with an intention set at bedtime. Write the phrase you will use, keep it under ten words, and say it every time you check the time. Repetition matters more than wording. What you are building is a conditioned response to the sensation of falling asleep, and conditioning is the one mechanism in this field with unambiguous laboratory support.

Mnemonic techniques from lucid dreaming research give a better yield than imagery alone, because they encode an intention in a memory and re-run it inside the dream. Imagery rehearsal, where you rehearse the act of noticing you are dreaming, works partly through that route. What does not hold up is a complicated ritual performed once at bedtime with great conviction. Adherence to the schedule is doing all the work in the methods that produce results.

What an out-of-body report actually contains

Reports cluster tightly. A small percentage of the population reports some version of the experience, and the description holds steady across cultures: a sense of leaving the body, a first-person view looking down at or from the physical form, a feeling of weightlessness or of passing through a surface, and often a sense of presence or of another awareness in the space. The frequency is a few percent rather than a few in a thousand, which matters, because it means the capacity is common enough to study and rare enough to stay ambiguous.

The triggers people report are all involuntary. Sleep onset, waking, severe fatigue, fever, meditation, and lucid dreams themselves all produce these episodes, and nobody manages them reliably on cue. Attempts to force the experience tend to yield either a dream with a flying motif or a near miss at sleep paralysis. Read that as information about how the phenomenon behaves rather than as a personal shortcoming.

Where projection and sleep paralysis overlap

Sleep paralysis produces the same sense of presence and the same immobility, with the added feature of full waking awareness. Hallucinations during paralysis are common across populations and include figures in the room, voices, and pressure on the chest. The condition is well described medically and correlates with disrupted sleep schedules, irregular hours, and sleeping on the back. Episodes last seconds to minutes and end on their own.

Culturally the event has been read as an intruder, an incubus, or a visiting spirit, and those readings show up in wildly separated traditions. Reading them as a demon encounter produces fear, and fear is the correct response to the interpretation but not to the event. People who name an episode as a known sleep phenomenon stop dreading the second occurrence. Several practitioners treat the paralysis frame as an accidental overlap with the thing they were originally chasing.

Remote viewing, and what the training buys

Remote viewing was funded heavily for two decades on the strength of the Stargate programme, which claimed prospective results that later review found were not reliably reproducible. The defensible summary is that the operational record does not support the operational claims. What the training does appear to produce is better visual description, less reliance on expectation, and measurable gains in the specificity and structure of what people report about unseen scenes.

That split matters for how you spend your time. If the goal is to move information across distance, the evidence base is thin. If the goal is to train disciplined, non-confabulated visual reporting, the exercises are sound and the discipline transfers to ordinary observation. Most serious practitioners now sit in the second camp and say so plainly, which is a healthier position than either the true believers or the debunkers.

The tulpa belongs to Tibetan practice

A tulpa in its source tradition is a visualisation exercise. The practitioner imagines a figure in detail, holds the image, and develops it across weeks, then relates to it as a presence with which certain qualities can be shared. It sits inside Tibetan Buddhist deity yoga and emanation work, where the aim is to develop qualities of mind rather than command a servant. In that context it is not a contract with an external being, and reading it as one misreads the source material.

The Western servitor tradition that adopted the name in the twentieth century borrows the vocabulary and changes the aim. Carroll and Crowley both treated such constructs as mental patterns the practitioner works with, not inhabitants of an independent realm. The shift is defensible as psychology, questionable as cosmology, and the modern internet version, which markets tulpas as spirits on retainer, has almost no relationship to either ancestor.

Choosing a form you can sustain

The most common failure is an over-elaborate figure. A tulpa with fine detail, specific clothing, and a complete face needs more sustained visual attention than most people can give for weeks, and the practice collapses into a series of half-remembered images. Long-term practitioners converge on the opposite advice: begin with silhouette, proportion, and one identifying feature, then grow detail only once the image holds on its own.

Consistency is what makes a figure recognisable, and recognisability is what makes the relationship feel like something other than a private daydream. Fix a time, use the same posture for the visualisation, and record the image in the same place each session. Expect the first weeks to be dominated by the difficulty of holding a mental picture at all, which is a perceptual skill independent of anything mystical.

Logging the session instead of upgrading the story

The most useful habit in any of this is a log written immediately on waking. Date, what you did the evening before, whether you drank alcohol or caffeine, what you recall, and how confident you feel in each detail. Two or three lines is enough. The reason is that memory smooths. A remembered dream tends to become a better story with each retelling, and after a week you are logging the version of it you find satisfying rather than the experience.

Confidence ratings are the part most people skip and the part that carries the information. Marking an image as certain at the time and finding it half-remembered later is the ordinary outcome, not a special failure. Over months this yields an actual profile: which impressions are reliable for you, which are reconstruction, and how long you can hold a dream before it degrades. That profile is worth more than any technique added afterwards.

The failure modes worth expecting

Several disappointments are common and mostly mechanical. Effortful lucid dreams collapse the moment attention touches them. People dream of being lucid while asleep, which is a dream about the topic. Long stable lucid nights usually end in a sudden jolt and full waking. Tulpa work stalls when your sleep or attention is poor, because both practices depend on holding attention across a boring interval.

The more serious risk lives in the framing, not the practice. Sleep paralysis intensifies when it is read as persecution. Visualisation work intensifies when the figure is treated as an autonomous being issuing demands, because that pattern pulls in self-attributing interpretation rather than disciplined looking. Neither failure is a reason to stop, and both are reasons to keep the work modest, private, and anchored in ordinary life.

Where the three accounts stop agreeing

The research literature supports lucid dreaming as a real phenomenon with measurable prevalence and learnable induction. It does not support the claim that lucid dreaming is a portal to an external space. Reports of out-of-body experience are real experiences with contested interpretation and no reliable mechanism. Tulpa practice has a documented origin, a documented method, and no documented external referent. Each of these statements is defensible on its own evidence.

What fails is blending them into one narrative where each supplies the gaps in the others. That narrative is enjoyable, and it is the source of most bad advice in this area, because every claim gets defended using the credibility of a different one. Holding them apart is less satisfying and considerably more useful, because you can then direct your effort at whichever one you actually care about and report the results honestly.

A version you can keep up

Pick one lane for six weeks. For dreams that means a fixed reminder phrase said ten times daily, an intention at bedtime, an alarm ninety minutes before waking, and a log written on rising. For projection it means noting when episodes occur and under which conditions, with no attempt to force them. For tulpa work it means fifteen minutes on a single simple image at the same time each day plus a written record of that image.

At six weeks you either have logs with more detail than you started with, or you do not. That is the whole test. None of these practices requires belief in order to work, and none of them works well if you are scanning for proof that the world is secretly strange. The interesting part is finding out what your own attention does when you point it carefully at nothing in particular, which turns out to be an unusual amount.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you really astral project on command?

Not reliably. Out-of-body experiences arrive near sleep onset, during lucid dreams, or under conditions such as fever, fatigue, and meditation, and most practitioners cannot produce one deliberately. Forcing it usually yields a dream with flying imagery or a sleep-paralysis episode instead. Treat spontaneous episodes as something to record and stay calm through rather than something to engineer.

How often should I expect lucid dreams?

For most people a few per week becomes reachable inside a fortnight once the alarm and the daytime reminder are used consistently. Without both, results are slower and less frequent. The first weeks tend to be dominated by dream recall rather than lucidity, so raising recall is the sensible first target, since recall is a prerequisite for noticing at all.

Is a tulpa the same thing as a servitor?

Not in the tradition the practice comes from. A tulpa is a sustained visualisation developed in Tibetan Buddhist emanation practice, aimed at developing qualities of mind. A servitor is a Western chaos-magick construct, usually a short charged sigil given a task. They share a word in casual speech and very little else, and most online content merging them gets both wrong.

Does remote viewing actually work?

Split it into two claims. That a viewer can report unseen targets with unusual accuracy has a thin evidence base, and the large funded programme that claimed otherwise was not reproducible on review. That the training improves disciplined, non-confabulated visual reporting is well supported by the exercises. Most current practitioners work in the second camp and say so openly.

What should I do during a sleep paralysis episode?

Stay still, stay calm, and let it pass. Episodes last seconds to minutes and resolve without intervention. Trying to move makes it worse, and reading the presence or the chest pressure as a spiritual attack keeps the fear response alive, which makes the next episode more likely. Naming the event as a known sleep phenomenon usually leaves most people far less frightened by the second one.
