Altar

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

An altar is a table with a job. In Goetic practice it is where the working stops being a thought and starts having a location, a time and an end. The seventy-two spirits come with a lot of frightening reputation and very little ceremony, so most of what an altar actually needs is much smaller than the reputation suggests.

What an Altar Is For

Three things, in order of usefulness. A surface that means something, a defined boundary for the working, and a place to leave the record afterwards. That is the whole list. A Goetic altar is not a shrine to anything; it is a container for a session, and the session is what does the work.

The boundary is the part people skip. A square of cloth, a chalk line, a line of salt, a closed door: any of these converts an ordinary hour into a bounded hour, and a bounded hour is far easier to remember later. Memory is the actual product of a working, and the altar is the thing that gives your memory a fixed coordinate to attach to.

The record is the third job. A sheet of paper on the altar, written during the working, gives you something to read back at the end. Without it you are left with a feeling, and a feeling is not evidence of anything. With it you have a date, a time and a set of notes, which is the difference between a practice and an anecdote.

The Seventy-Two and How They Are Arranged

Three ranks divide the seventy-two spirits named in the Ars Goetia: nine Presidents, thirty-six Dukes and thirty Kings. Underneath the ranking they sit in families of two, three or four. The arrangement is not decorative. It is a functional taxonomy, and once you see it as one, choosing a spirit becomes an ordinary decision rather than a dice roll.

A President rules a broad domain, a Duke runs a narrower one, a King handles a specific craft or circumstance. So a question about a whole area of life, a question about one recurring situation, and a question about one specific task are three different kinds of lookup. Working out which kind you have is most of the work.

The material itself is older than the Ars Goetia. The material descends from Jewish and Christian demonological sources, the Book of Enoch in the Pseudepigrapha above all, and reaches the grimoire lineage in the seventeenth century by way of the Conjuratio Ben-Maimon and the Lemuria. Read that line and the picture gets clearer: this is a catalogue of offices, not a list of torturers.

Where the Reputation Comes From

The modern Goetia as a brand, the video essays, the apps, the grimoire commentary layered on top, is a twenty-first century phenomenon sitting on top of that older material. What it did was pull the spirits out of their classical context. In the original scheme they were administrative or disciplinary officials, the kind of figure that enforces a rule, not the kind that harms the innocent for sport.

That shift matters for how you work. If the office is administrative, the working is about authority, permission and structure. If the office is disciplinary, the working is about boundary and consequence. Neither of those requires you to role-play being menaced, and both of them are more useful than menace.

The 168 also matters. The spirits do not act independently, so there is no doctrine of propitiation or appeasement here, and nothing in the classical material suggests they can be made to act against your own interests if you keep your end of the arrangement straight.

Setting the Altar in Ten Minutes

Step eight is where most of the ceremony lives and it takes four seconds. Speaking a name once, on your own surface, inside a boundary you drew, is enough to make the working distinct from ordinary speech. Repeating it, adding titles, or performing a dialogue adds weight of impression rather than operation.

  1. Clear a surface roughly the size of a book. Wipe it. A clean table beats a decorated one every time.
  2. Lay a dark cloth or a sheet of paper. Its only job is to tell you where the working begins and stops.
  3. Mark the boundary. Chalk, a line of salt, or the edge of the cloth if the cloth is square.
  4. Put the paper for the record in the middle, with a pen. Write the date before you do anything else.
  5. Set one object that names the domain. A coin for wealth, a book for knowledge, a key for a door or a decision.
  6. Set the lamp or the stick. Light it last, and light it deliberately.
  7. Write the office you are asking for in one sentence, in the first person, in the present tense.
  8. Say the spirit's name once, out loud, and stop. One name, one time.
  9. Do the working while the lamp burns. Do not multitask.
  10. Snuff the light, read the record back, and put the paper away with the date on it.

Choosing a Spirit Without Guessing

Read the stated office in the source text before you work with any name. Not the video title, not the app summary, not the listicle ordering. The office line in the source, which is usually one sentence, is the only part that tells you what the spirit actually does.

Then check that the office matches the domain you need. This is where beginners go wrong in a specific and avoidable way: they pick a famous name, discover it is the spirit of something they did not want, and conclude the working failed. The working failed because the lookup was wrong, not because the spirit was unresponsive.

Where money is the question, the source names Mammon as the spirit of wealth and Clauneck as a duke said to grant riches, with Paimon granting dignities and confirmations of favour. Those are three different shapes of the same request and they are not interchangeable. Decide which one you are asking for before you light anything.

The Working Sequence

The hand work is not filler. It occupies the interval between the request and the answer so that you are not sitting in silence, where every thought gets louder. Washing a cup, folding a cloth, writing the date four more times: any of these holds the space and keeps the working from turning into a conversation with yourself.

  1. Name the spirit once, out loud, in the present tense.
  2. State the office in your own words, in one sentence, as a request rather than a command.
  3. State the timeframe out loud. A date, a number of days, or a clear condition.
  4. Do something with your hands for the length of the asking. Write, draw, wash the object, sort the papers.
  5. Ask once more, in the same words, so the request is identical both times.
  6. Snuff the light. That is the close, and it is not optional.

Why These Workings Fail

The most common failure is a mismatched lookup, and it accounts for a large share of the reports you will read about Goetic work being pointless. Close behind it sits the untimed request, which by construction cannot land badly and therefore cannot teach you anything either. The third is never closing, so the session never has an end and the next hour inherits the first one's pressure.

The fourth is doing three workings on one altar in one evening. The boundary you drew held for one thing. Reusing it immediately dilutes the thing you were trying to strengthen, and it is a common mistake precisely because it feels efficient.

The fifth is escalating. A flat result gets answered with a bigger name, a longer ritual and more equipment. The size of the working has no relationship to the size of the effect. What changes results is the accuracy of the office and the honesty of the record.

Testing Without Fooling Yourself

Write the check before you start, not after. Not the outcome you want, which is too generous, but the specific thing that would count as a result: a document arriving, a call being made, a decision finally being made, an opportunity you were already offered becoming visible.

Then look at what happened, not at how you feel. Feeling moved is not a result. It is common, it is cheap, and it follows from any hour spent concentrating on a problem. The record is what separates the two.

Three attempts with a corrected lookup and a stated timeframe is a fair trial. If all three come back with nothing you can name, the working is not landing and the most useful thing you can do is change the question rather than the method.

Ground Rules Worth Keeping

No working is a necessity. If you can solve the problem without the altar, solve it without the altar. The apparatus should be a deliberate choice on a slow afternoon, not a dependency for anything urgent.

Nothing is binding. Classical practice treats the arrangement as a request with a stated office, not a contract, and a working that leaves you feeling obligated is a sign the frame was wrong rather than that the spirit is difficult.

Read before you work. A name you have never researched is the real hazard in this material, and it is a completely avoidable one. Fifteen minutes with a source text is enough to know whether the office matches the domain.

Keep the record and keep the boundary. Those two are what turn a series of evenings into something you can look at and learn from, and they are the only two items on this list that cost nothing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are the seventy-two spirits demons in any real sense?

Historically they are entries in a demonological catalogue, with no evidence of real agency behind them. Practically, they are a rich and usable symbolic vocabulary: seventy-two named offices covering the ordinary situations a person actually runs into, which is more than most systems of personal symbolism offer. Treat the names as a lookup table and the risk in this material drops to nothing.

Which spirit gives money?

In the Ars Goetia, Mammon is the spirit of wealth, Clauneck is a duke said to grant riches, and Paimon grants dignities and confirmations of favour. Those are three different requests and they do not substitute for each other: one is a general orientation toward wealth, one is a specific grant, one is about standing and being recognised. Read the stated office in the source text for whichever you pick, and state the timeframe out loud.

Who are the nine Presidents?

In printed order: Bael, Agares, Vassago, Samigina, Marbas, then Valefor, Aamon, Belphegor and Saleos. Each carries a documented rank, a visible form and a broad area of power in the source material. Presidents are the widest of the three ranks, so they suit questions about a whole area of life rather than one specific task. Reading the office line before working with any of them is still the step that decides the outcome.

Do I need to build a permanent altar?

No. A cleared table, a dark cloth and a drawn boundary is a complete altar for a short working, and it is the setup most practitioners actually use. Building something permanent makes sense only if you are doing weekly work and want the setup to stop costing you attention. The boundary and the record are what matter, and both of those fit in a drawer.

What if nothing happens at all?

Then check the lookup before you check anything else. Most flat results trace to a name whose stated office does not match the request, and the correction is free. If the office matches and the timeframe was stated and three separate attempts still produced nothing, change the question. Repeating a working that has failed three ways tends to produce a fourth failure and a stronger attachment to the wrong approach.
