Dreams, Astral Projection and Tulpas

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

People use the words dreaming, astral projection and tulpa practice as if they were three rooms in the same house. They are not. Lucid dreaming is a documented sleep phenomenon with measurable prevalence and reliable induction cues. Out of body experiences are rare, involuntary for almost everyone, and clustered around sleep onset, illness and deep meditation. A tulpa is a Tibetan Buddhist visualisation practice whose original purpose had nothing to do with creating a companion. Keeping those three apart changes what you can reasonably expect from a month of work, and it tells you which failures are your technique and which are the ceiling. This guide works through each one separately, gives a session protocol you can run tonight, and tells you plainly which claims survive contact with a record.

Three different things people call going somewhere else

Lucid dreaming means knowing you are asleep while still asleep. The body stays where it is; only the dreamer's sense of location changes. It requires no esoteric apparatus, and a large fraction of people have done it at least once without training. Induction techniques exist in enough numbers and enough variety that you can pick one and test it.

An out of body experience reports a sense of leaving the physical body and perceiving a location from outside it. Surveys find this in a small percentage of the population, and the reports cluster around the hypnagogic border, around illness, and during long meditation. The fact that it is hard to produce on demand is the single most useful fact about it.

What the sleep research actually supports

Lucid dreaming is the most tractable item in this whole domain. It has a measurable population base, documented prevalence rates, and induction methods that have been tested in sleep laboratories. That does not make it validated in the sense practitioners sometimes imply; it makes it a real, measurable, learnable human phenomenon. The rest of this article keeps that distinction visible.

Remote viewing is a separate case entirely. Work programmes such as the Stargate project produced prospective results that later failed to replicate reliably. The training itself does appear to improve the ability to describe a scene in structured, non-generic language. Whether that is psychic perception or trained description is precisely the question the replication failures left open.

Why the morning is the productive window

REM periods lengthen as the night goes on, and the longest stretches sit in the final third before waking. Waking at the end of a REM period gives you a dream you have just been in, which is the material lucid recall needs. This is why every reliable induction method in the literature is anchored to the moment of waking rather than to bedtime.

The practical protocol is unglamorous. Set an alarm for your ordinary waking time, keep a pen and paper beside the bed, and on waking do not move. Write down everything you can still feel, including fragments, before you stand up. The motion of standing and the flood of sensory input destroy the memory faster than most people expect, which is why a phone-based note typed in bed performs worse than a scribbled page.

The tulpa in the setting it came from

Tulpa practice originates in Tibetan Buddhist traditions, where the method serves visualisation exercises and deity yoga. The practitioner holds a mental image with extraordinary concentration until it is treated as a real presence, and the discipline is a training in sustained attention rather than a conjuration.

Reading it as the creation of a separate autonomous being imports a Western metaphysical assumption the source material does not require. Within the original framework the work develops concentration, and the figure serves as a focus for that development. Taken that way, the practice is demanding but coherent, and it is not a relationship with a person.

Building a first tulpa image

Choose a form with a limited number of features. A figure with too many limbs, too much ornament or shifting proportions consumes the concentration it is meant to train. Begin seated, clothed, and roughly your own build, then narrow it further. The tradition's advice about finishing the image in detail rather than by chance is there for a practical reason: an ambiguous image gives the attention somewhere to slip.

Sit in a fixed position for a fixed time, twenty minutes being generous, and hold the image without narrating a story about it. Narrating is the common failure; you are building a single object of attention, not running a film. Stop the session before you are tired rather than pushing to the timer, because the last minutes of a depleted session teach you nothing except how to drift.

Running a session from start to finish

Two or three short sessions a week beats one long one, because the skill being trained is returning to a precise target, and that return is what a long session lets you avoid. Settle, fix the posture, build the image, hold, and close deliberately rather than drifting off. Closing matters more than beginners expect; an abrupt end teaches the mind that the practice can be abandoned.

Keep a dated note of the session length, whether you held the image continuously or lost it, and one sentence on where the attention went when it wandered. That single sentence is the most useful data you will generate, because the location of the slip tells you whether the problem is the image, the posture, or your own fatigue.

Where this method actually breaks down

The wall most people hit is that the image dissolves the moment attention softens, and the temptation is to raise the stakes, push harder, and extend sessions until they are unpleasant. Concentration is not a muscle you can train by straining it. If your notes show repeated losses at the same point in the session, the fix is a shorter session and a simpler image, not more effort.

A second wall appears when the practice is assigned a job it cannot do. Tulpa work is a concentration discipline with a long history of use in ritual. It is not a technique for summoning help, contacting the dead, or resolving a relationship, and any source that tells you it is has misunderstood what it is describing.

A night-time notebook, and why it stays boring

A dream symbol dictionary is worth having as a lookup, and worthless as an oracle. Its function is compression: once you have logged forty dreams, you can see that a corridor, a late train or a wet stair recurs, and you can look at what preceded each occurrence. The pattern is in your own record. The dictionary only supplies a word for a symbol you have already seen repeatedly.

Log dreams within minutes of waking, in plain language, with no interpretation column. An interpretation column written at the moment of waking is contaminated by whatever you hoped the dream meant. If you want interpretation, do it a week later, once, and mark it as interpretation.

Grounding before and after

Every tradition that works with altered states includes a way back, and the reason is not ritual decorum. A practice that ends in ordinary daylight and ordinary tasks tends to be stable; one that ends in rumination does not. Have something physical and slightly boring ready: a glass of water, a walk, a list of small household tasks you can finish in twenty minutes.

This matters most after an out of body report, because the sense of having been elsewhere can persist and can be misread as a significant event about your life. If the experience is destabilising, the correct response is less practice, not more, and possibly a conversation with someone who can tell you what dissociation looks like in the ordinary clinical sense.

Separating a result from a coincidence

Write down the prediction before the session, not after. This is the entire discipline. A dream that follows two days of anxious waiting will feature the thing you were anxious about; you will remember it vividly and forget the four unremarkable nights that preceded it. The record does not prevent selective memory, but it makes it visible, and visible is most of what you need.

For tulpa practice the equivalent control is a session with no intention at all, held with the same posture and the same duration. If the image forms as readily when you are not trying to build anything specific, then what you have measured is your visual imagination responding to a long still session, which is a real finding and not the finding you wanted.

What the honest monthly review looks like

Once a month, count sessions held, sessions where you held the image continuously, and lucid dreams recalled. Three numbers, no adjectives. If the second number is not rising, the method is not being learned, whatever your notes say about how deep the experiences felt. The subjective impression of depth is a feeling, and feelings about concentration are notoriously flattering.

Expect the first month to produce almost nothing measurable. That is normal and it is not a reason to escalate. A steady, boring schedule of short sessions produces more reliable results in six weeks than a heroic fortnight followed by abandonment, and the boring schedule is the only one that survives contact with ordinary life.

When to stop, and what to keep

Stop if sessions reliably end in agitation rather than calm, if you begin losing sleep, or if the practice starts crowding out work and relationships. None of these are purification signs. They are the ordinary way a demanding discipline announces that it has exceeded what your current life can hold.

If you stop, keep the notebook. The log is the durable asset here, far more than any technique, because it is the only part of this work that will still be true in two years. The techniques can be picked up again in a week; two years of dated notes cannot be reconstructed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you really astral project on demand?

For almost no one, and the honest answer is that most reported out of body experiences happen near sleep onset, during illness, or after long meditation rather than at will. The skill people are actually practising when they try to leave the body on command is inducing a lucid dream and then imagining a location outside it. That is a legitimate and trainable experience, and it is worth doing for itself rather than as a substitute for something rarer.

Is a tulpa a real being that I have created?

Within the Tibetan Buddhist framework the practice develops sustained concentration, and the image is the object of that concentration rather than an autonomous entity with its own will. Nothing in the source material requires the conclusion that you have manufactured a person. Practising it as a visualisation discipline is coherent, demanding, and considerably safer than treating the image as a companion who might be harmed.

Does remote viewing work?

The prospective programmes, Stargate included, produced results that later failed to replicate reliably, so the honest position is that the headline claim is unestablished. The training does seem to improve how precisely and non-generically people describe scenes they have not seen, which is a real and useful skill for artists, investigators and witnesses. Whether that improvement is psychic or perceptual is precisely the unresolved question.

How long should a tulpa session be?

Twenty minutes is already long for a beginner, and three short sessions spread across a week will teach you more than a single hour. The skill being built is the return to a precise target after losing it, and a long session mostly lets you avoid practising that return. Stop while you are still capable of holding the image, because closing deliberately is half the discipline.

Why do I keep forgetting my lucid dreams?

You are almost certainly moving before you have written, and the memory of a dream degrades within seconds of the body registering wakefulness. Keep paper beside the bed, write without sitting up, and record fragments rather than trying to reconstruct a narrative. Dated fragments are recoverable months later; a clean story assembled in the morning is already an invention.
