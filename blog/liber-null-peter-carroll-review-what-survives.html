Liber Null, Reviewed by Use: What Peter Carroll's Book Is For

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

Peter Carroll's Liber Null and Psychick is about a hundred pages, was written by a man who had broken with the only organisation he belonged to, and has since been read by perhaps a few hundred thousand people. Most of them have read it as a philosophy and been disappointed, because it is not a philosophy. It is a worksheet, and it is one of the few in the tradition that you can finish. The confusion is not the reader's fault, because the book is full of sentences that sound like doctrine and mean something much more practical.

What kind of document this actually is

It is a manual with a thin philosophical frame. The frame exists and it is worth having, but the manual is the bulk of the text and the frame is not load-bearing. Carroll's position is that everything not governed by a declared order is available, which is an older sense of chaos than the one in circulation now, and the practical consequence of holding it is that you do not need permission from anyone. That single idea is what lets a hundred-page book do the work that initiations do elsewhere, and it is the reason the book matters historically more than its arguments do.

The giveaway that it is a manual is the shape of the instructions. They are short, they are imperative, and they do not depend on a system you have to learn first. There is no lineage to study, no temple to join, no curriculum with a year two. You can begin working from page forty of a paperback on a Tuesday. Everything the book is famous for is downstream of that property, and the people who love it are mostly people who had previously been told they needed a path and discovered they did not.

The parts that carry the most weight

Three sections do most of the practical work. The first is on intention, and its actual content is narrow: a vague intention produces a vague result, and the fix is to write the aim as something that either happened or did not happen. That is the whole teaching and it is the one piece of the book you cannot take away and still have the method. Carroll was not talking about focus or state of mind, he was talking about making a question that has an answer.

The second is on method selection, and it is the part most people skip. The method is the least important part of the working and it should be cheap, quick and reversible, because the method is what you will be tempted to make complicated. The third is the design of the sigil, which Carroll borrows from Spare and simplifies: reduce the statement, let go of the result, and do not try to keep the mark. That last instruction is the one beginners resist hardest and it is the one that most reliably produces a working sigil rather than a talisman, and the difference is that a talisman is a reminder you have made for yourself.

The parts worth skipping on a first pass

The flattery. The book addresses the reader as a practitioner in a register that was standard in 1972 and reads now as a club's manner, and there are paragraphs where you will find yourself being told what kind of person you are for having bought the book. It is not seriously dishonest, it is just dated, and it is a good reminder that the people who founded traditions were often lonely and the tone comes from there.

The cosmology goes too. The material on the nature of chaos is a stance rather than a mechanism, and it is useful as a stance because it dissolves the need for permission, and it is useless as a mechanism because there is nothing in it you can apply. If you find yourself trying to work out what chaos is in order to use chaos magic, that is the wrong question and the book will not stop you from asking it. The stance works without the answer and the answer will not improve the working.

How to read it, in the order that makes it work

Read it twice and do different things each time. On the first pass, read only the instructions, and try to follow one of them exactly, from beginning to end, without improvising anything. Pick the least interesting one. Do not use it on anything you care about, do not tell anyone, and write down in advance what you will check afterwards and on what date. That run is worth more than every subsequent attempt, because it establishes whether you are the sort of person who follows an instruction past the point where it stops being interesting.

On the second pass, months later, read it for the reasoning, and the reasoning is where the historical weight is. Carroll came out of a formal initiatory tradition and then deliberately refused to reproduce it, and the reasons he gives are structural rather than mystical: an initiatory structure reproduces exactly the gatekeeping the material was supposed to remove, and a lineage you inherit is a claim you cannot check. Everything in the surviving chaos magick canon is downstream of that decision, including the log that most practitioners now keep without ever being told to.

Who it is for, and who will waste time on it

It is for someone who wants a working method this week and is willing to keep a record. It is not for someone looking for a tradition with a community, because there is no community in the book and the community is the part you will have to build. It is also a poor choice if your interest is ritual atmosphere, because the book has almost none and its tone actively discourages the sort of practice that produces atmosphere.

The people who waste time on it are usually the ones who read it as the beginning of a path rather than a tool. They finish it, feel underinformed, and go looking for the missing parts, and the missing parts are not in any book, they are in the hours afterwards that the book did not describe because they could not. If you take one instruction from the book and do it a hundred times with a record, you will know more about what magic is than you can learn from any amount of additional reading. That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the specific thing this book was built to make possible.

What actually happened after publication

The first edition was small and the second printing is the one everyone has. The book's real event was not sales, it was price and length, and it is worth being blunt about this because the standard account credits Carroll with inventing a school of magic and the more accurate credit is that he removed the barrier that had nothing to do with the difficulty of the material. The sources were short and public. What kept most people out was cost, availability, and the assumption that a serious practice required an introduction they had not received. A cheap paperback of a hundred pages addressed all three at once.

The cost of the result is a tradition that is scattered, undocumented and hard to learn from, because there is no centre and no teacher and the practice has to be reconstructed from documents written by people who each thought they were writing the whole thing. If you want the canonical treatment rather than the working manual, Codex Chaoticus is the longer book and it is where the later thinking went after the first one had done its job of making the method available.

Five ways readers get it wrong

The first is treating the book as an introduction to a larger system, so you finish it and go looking for the rest. There is no rest, and the rest is practice. The second is skipping to the sigil section and starting there, which produces marks with no statement behind them and no review, which is a hobby rather than a method. The third is improving the method before you have run the plain one, which is almost always a sign that the plain one is going to be abandoned.

The fourth is the one that costs the most time. Reading the book and not doing anything produces a very convincing feeling of having made progress, and the feeling is not the progress. The fifth is buying a bundle instead. There is a genuine argument that one short book and one reference work beat any collection, because a collection is mostly material you will not read, and the cost of a bundle is proportional to the number of books in it rather than to the quality of the one you needed. Two books and a notebook will carry a beginner further than ten and a subscription.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Liber Null a good first book?

It is the best first book, for a specific reason. It is short enough to finish, cheap enough that finishing is free, and it contains an actual working method rather than a description of one. The mistake is reading it for the philosophy. Read it for the instructions, follow one exactly, and record the result, and you will have more usable technique at the end of a month than from a shelf of introductions.

Do I need to buy a PDF or a print copy?

Any legitimate copy you will actually read. This book is short and old and the argument about format is a distraction from the fact that the barrier it removed was financial. If a free scan is what gets you started, start. If you are going to annotate it and refer back to it for years, buy a copy you can write in.

Is Liber Null still relevant?

More than most of the field, and for a structural reason rather than a thematic one. The techniques it describes are unglamorous and the attitudes around magic have changed considerably since 1972, but the design of a working, the discipline of stating an intention precisely, and the refusal to build a gate have all survived, and they are what everyone in the tradition is still using underneath whatever they have added on top.

Is Codex Chaoticus better for a beginner?

Not for a first book, because length is a real cost at the point where you have not yet established a practice. Codex Chaoticus is the deeper treatment and it is the right second purchase once you have a log with entries in it and specific questions, because then you have something to ask it. Read in the wrong order the second book becomes a substitute for practice rather than an aid to it.

Why is Liber Null so cheap and why does that matter?

Because Carroll was trying to remove exactly that barrier. The sources were short and public; what kept people out was cost and the belief that entry required something you had not been given. A short, cheap, complete manual removed all three constraints at once, and that is the real historical significance of the book. It is also the argument against buying bundles: the constraint that mattered was always access, and access scales with the number of books rather than their price.
