Hidden, Lost and Forbidden Knowledge

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

The word forbidden does most of the selling in occult publishing, and it is worth examining before you build a shelf on it. Forbidden implies a gate, and a gate implies someone decided. In practice the word covers at least three quite different things: secrecy deliberately maintained, exclusion by an authority that had the power to exclude, and marketing applied retroactively to whatever sold. Only the middle of those is about texts, and even there the interesting cases are rarely the ones advertised. This article takes the Book of Enoch as its central case, because it is the clearest genuine exclusion in the Western scriptural record: a substantial work known to the church fathers, present among the Dead Sea Scrolls, and absent from most modern Bibles for reasons that are documented and not mysterious. Around that case you can build a triage method, learn to cite honestly when provenance fails, and distinguish the practical difference between secrecy and censorship. The working assumption throughout is the one most often forgotten: this tradition is plural, and the search for a single definitive book is the commonest error people make with it.

The word forbidden, and what it hides

Forbidden is a claim about intent, and it is usually made on behalf of people who are not present and cannot be asked. A text described as forbidden asks you to believe a story about its own suppression, and the story is supplied by whoever is selling it to you, usually in the first paragraph of the introduction.

Strip the word and ask what is actually true. Was the text withheld from readers, or was it removed from a particular canon, or was it simply not printed because nobody had the money? These have different implications and the modern occult shelf is full of books whose introduction implies the first while the evidence supports only the third.

Two reasons a text disappears from a canon

Exclusion and suppression turn out to have two quite different structures, and the distinction is the most useful thing on this page. The first is institutional: a body with authority over the canon makes a decision, and the reasons are usually recorded, usually theological, and usually concerned with how the text reads rather than with what it contains.

The second is commercial and technical. A text is not copied, not translated, not printed, and therefore disappears from circulation through no decision at all. The medieval book trade lost enormous quantities of work to nothing more than damp and disinterest, and a great deal of what looks like suppression is really the ordinary mortality of documents. When you read a claim of suppression, check first whether anyone actually had the authority to suppress.

The Book of Enoch, and a real exclusion

The Book of Enoch is the clearest genuine exclusion in the Western scriptural record, and it is worth studying precisely because it is not mysterious. Substantial fragments were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, so the text was being copied and read in the first century. The church fathers knew it and cited it. And it is absent from most modern Bibles, for reasons that are documented in the historical record rather than inferred from a publisher's introduction.

A brief note on the arithmetic, because it is often confused in both directions: the Ethiopic Orthodox tradition treats Enoch as canonical, which is a useful reminder that its absence from other traditions is a decision rather than a verdict on the text. The exclusion was made by authorities with the standing to make it, and their reasons are on the record. That makes it a real case, which is precisely why it is a good one to think with.

Reading a rejected text without a rescue fantasy

There is a predictable failure when reading a text that was excluded, and it has two forms. The first treats exclusion as proof of importance, which inverts the reasoning: the authorities who excluded it were not selecting for quality. The second treats exclusion as evidence of suppression of dangerous knowledge, which requires assuming an intent nobody documented.

Read it instead as a text in a specific historical situation, and ask the ordinary scholarly questions. What is it about, what did its first readers use it for, what were the anxieties of the period, and what does its survival tell us about who was copying what. This is less satisfying than a conspiracy, and it is the version that produces knowledge rather than a story about knowledge.

The pamphlet and the codex, priced differently

Two different objects appear repeatedly in occult publishing and they are priced for entirely different reasons. Peter Carroll's Liber Null and psi, published in 1977, runs to barely a hundred pages, costs almost nothing, and is still where most people in this field begin. It is a beginner's manual that happens to be ideological, and its virtues are proportion and accessibility.

A codex or a grimoire edition costs fifteen to sixty times as much, is usually a facsimile, and is usually bought for the object rather than the content. Neither is a better purchase. The pamphlet teaches; the facsimile satisfies an appetite for material that the pamphlet does not. Recognising which one you are about to buy prevents the common waste, which is accumulating facsimiles and finding later that you have no instructions in any of them.

Anonymous authority and what the byline is worth

The Kybalion, published in 1908, is the standing example of a text whose authority is borrowed. It is unsigned, its claimed provenance is not supported by anything outside the text's own claim, and it is structured as the teachings of a purported ancient order. It has been enormously influential and it is, as a historical document, a fiction with excellent production values.

The practical rule is simple. Weight a claim by whether you can name who is speaking. Anonymous material can be excellent and it cannot be checked, so treat it as a source of phrases rather than of claims. This is a reading-hygiene rule rather than a debunking exercise, and it costs nothing: the Kybalion is more useful to you as a well-made source of usable formulations than as an authority on anything, and treating it that way costs you nothing you were entitled to.

Plurality is the tradition's actual shape

The single most common error people make with occult books is searching for the one definitive text, as though a real tradition had a nucleus and the periphery were noise. It does not. The tradition is plural, and it has been produced by a large number of people with incompatible aims, over a long period, in several languages, most of whom did not know each other.

This reframes the whole project. You are not assembling a canon, you are choosing a reading list, and a reading list is a set of decisions you make and can defend. Six books you have actually finished and can discuss are more useful than sixty you own, and the six are also the only ones that will ever shape what you do.

Assembling a shelf you could defend in public

A defensible shelf has three tiers and the tiers have different purposes. Entry points, at least one, short and cheap: Carroll's 1977 pamphlet is the standing recommendation for a reason. Working references, at least one, that you consult rather than read: a table of correspondences, a symbolic dictionary, anything you will look things up in. History, at least one, that tells you where the material came from so you stop attributing things to the wrong century.

Then the rule that makes it defensible: you should be able to say, for each book, what it is for and who wrote it. If you cannot name an author, you have an anonymous text and it belongs in a fourth category, which is a shelf of things you find evocative. That category is legitimate. It just should not be the one you draw technique from.

A triage you can finish in one evening

An hour and a half, and it works on whatever pile is in front of you. Lay out ten candidates. For each, write three lines: who wrote it or that nobody is known to have, what it is for, and one sentence on how you would know it was doing something for you. Then delete five.

The deletion is the method. Most people's shelves fail not from bad choices but from an inability to choose, and the five you delete this evening are the five that would otherwise have remained unread forever while providing the feeling of a substantial collection. What remains is five books you can finish, and a written record of why, which is the thing that makes the shelf defensible rather than merely owned.

Citing honestly when provenance fails

When you pass on material from a source whose provenance you cannot verify, say so in the same sentence. Not as a disclaimer at the end of a post, and not by omission. The practice costs nothing and it is the single change that most improves the reliability of what circulates in this field.

Concretely: a formulation you got from a text with no traceable author can be used, attributed as circulating unattributed. A historical claim needs a source that can be checked. A mechanism claim needs evidence, and if there is none the sentence should say that rather than implying that use over time is a substitute. This is boring discipline and it is what separates a working practice from a collection of inherited impressions.

The practical difference between secrecy and censorship

Secrecy is a practice: somebody chose not to write down, or chose an audience, or kept something in a form that was not meant to travel. It can be genuine, it can be ordinary craft discretion, and it produces texts whose survival is accidental. Censorship is an act by an authority with the power to exclude, aimed at a specific content.

The distinction matters because the two generate completely different evidence. Secrecy leaves traces that are often tantalising and rarely conclusive. Censorship leaves a record: who decided, when, on what stated grounds, and who was on the losing side. So when you encounter a claim of forbidden knowledge, ask which of the two is alleged. Most of them are the first, described in language borrowed from the second, and the borrowing is what generates the sense that something important was taken from you.

When collecting stops being the work

There is a point at which buying and reading about practice becomes the practice, and the symptom is easy to spot: the reading is entirely about how to do it, and none of it is about doing it. Nobody has written a working. Or the working exists and has not been repeated. Both are common, and neither is a failure of discipline, they are just the natural resting point of a certain kind of curiosity.

The cure is a threshold rather than a prohibition. Do not add a book to the shelf until one is finished and has produced at least one written aim. That rule is annoying and it works, because it makes the collecting serve the practice instead of replacing it. A shelf of five finished books and a notebook with a dozen dated aims in it is a better position than any collection, and it is reachable in a year.

Frequently Asked Questions

Was the Book of Enoch really suppressed?

It was genuinely excluded from the canon that most modern Bibles represent, and that exclusion was decided by authorities who had the standing to decide it, with reasons that are on the historical record. Substantial fragments were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, so the text was being read and copied in the first century, and the church fathers cited it. The Ethiopic Orthodox tradition still treats it as canonical, which confirms the point: its absence elsewhere was a decision, not a verdict.

Is the Kybalion worth reading?

Read it as a well-made source of usable formulations rather than as an authority. It is unsigned, its claimed ancient provenance is not supported by anything outside its own claim, and it has been enormously influential anyway. Treating it as a document about early twentieth-century New Thought publishing rather than about ancient teaching costs you nothing you were entitled to, and it is genuinely readable.

Should I read Liber Null and psi or a grimoire first?

The pamphlet, and not as a compromise. It came out in 1977, runs to barely a hundred pages, and is still where most people in this field begin, because proportion and accessibility are what a first book has to have. A facsimile codex costs many times as much, is usually bought for the object, and usually contains no instructions. Read the pamphlet, finish it, then decide whether you want material or technique.

How do I know if a book is any good before I finish it?

Check three things in the first twenty pages: whether an author is named, whether the introduction makes a claim about mechanism or only about intention, and whether there is anything you would not have expected. The first two tell you whether you are dealing with something you can check. The third tells you whether the text has a point of view worth your attention. A book failing all three is an object, not a reading.

What is the most common mistake people make with occult books?

Searching for the single definitive text, as though a real tradition had a nucleus and the rest were noise. The tradition is plural and was produced by many people with incompatible aims who mostly did not know each other. You are not assembling a canon, you are choosing a reading list, and the useful outcome is six books you have finished over sixty you own.
