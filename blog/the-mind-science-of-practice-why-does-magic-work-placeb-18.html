The Mind Science of Practice

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

Four research findings get quoted in a single flat voice, and the flattening is what makes the claims sound stronger than any of them is. Separated by height, they turn into something quite different: a solid floor, a real and small middle, a piece of arithmetic, and a model that explains without measuring.

Four findings at four different heights

A ritual changes breathing and heart rhythm within minutes, and that is measurable with a cheap sensor. Expectancy shifts some reported outcomes, and the effect is small. A five-symbol card deck is a constraint on guessing rather than a discovery about the mind. Perception is described as hypothesis management, which is a model rather than a measurement.

Stacked like that, the four are not one claim. They sit at different heights, do different work, and fail in different ways. Most of the overreach in this area comes from reading a claim at the top of the stack and attributing it to the base.

The base, which is yours to measure

Take a baseline before anything. Sit quietly for ten minutes and record heart rate and breathing, then sit for ten minutes with whatever your practice consists of and record again, three times across a fortnight. Most people find a change they did not expect, and it is usually smaller than the story they told about it.

This is the only layer where you do not need anyone's permission or anybody's vocabulary. It is also the layer that carries the least metaphysical weight, which is exactly why it is the foundation. A claim that rests only on the top layer is a story about perception. A claim that rests on the base is a description of a body, and it is much harder to argue with.

The middle, real and small

Expectancy effects have been measured in medicine for a long time, on reported pain and on some physiological outcomes, and the size is modest. Trials in which participants are told directly that what they are given is inert still show reported changes, which puts a ceiling on the simple story that the whole thing is belief.

Real and small is a good place to be, and it is a bad place to build a theory on. If your practice works partly through expectancy, you are relying on something with a measured magnitude, and the honest version of the claim says how big.

The calibration layer, which is arithmetic only

Five symbols, one in five. That is not a psychic claim, it is a design constraint, and it follows from the deck regardless of who is guessing. One hundred calls at chance gives twenty hits on average, and the spread around twenty is wide enough that a striking run is unremarkable.

The layer's real function is calibration. It gives you a number to practise against before you try anything that cannot be checked, and it is a humbling one. Almost everyone who runs the hundred calls first finds they were counting selectively.

The top layer, which explains the others

A forecasting account of perception is the most interesting of the four and the least evidentiary. It proposes that seeing is prediction under uncertainty, which would explain why expectation changes experience without requiring anyone to be lying about what they see.

It is worth holding because it does real explanatory work. It is not worth holding as a mechanism, because a model that accounts for a phenomenon is not thereby evidence for the phenomenon. This is the layer that gets quoted as though it were the base, and that inversion is most of the bad writing in this area.

Why the stack has no floor

Here is the shape that should worry you. The base is solid, the middle is real and small, the calibration layer is arithmetic, and the top is a model. Every layer is measured, or is a constraint, or is an explanatory frame. Nothing at any height supports the claim that a ritual treats a condition.

Which means the popular claim and the evidence are not in tension. They are about different subjects. The evidence describes a body that changes when you sit still with attention. The popular claim describes a body that changes because something outside was contacted. Both sentences use the word ritual and neither implies the other.

What science does to a magic claim

It narrows it. Take a claim about a sigil producing an outcome and ask which layer it needs. If it needs the autonomic change, it has a real but small effect available. If it needs the card deck to do something impossible, it is asking for arithmetic to break. If it needs the model, it is confusing an explanation with a cause.

Most claims survive this and get smaller. That is the useful outcome. A smaller claim you can actually check is worth more than a large one you can only believe, and the reduction is not a loss of meaning. It is the difference between a practice and a story about a practice.

What science does not do to a magic claim

It does not make the practice pointless. Attention, expectation, rhythm, symbolism and record-keeping are all real tools with real effects, and none of them requires believing a mechanism. You can use all five and hold every one of them lightly.

It also does not settle the question of what people experience. Some practitioners report experiences that no instrument has captured, and the honest position is that the reports are interesting, unmeasured, and not evidence. Held that way, they cost you nothing and support nothing.

The gap the shape leaves open

The stack explains why a ritual changes your state and why the change is not what practitioners say it is. It says nothing about whether the symbol means anything beyond the person using it, and it says nothing about what happens in the hour when the record is not being written.

That gap is where most of the actual difficulty lives, and it is not a gap science can close with this material. The honest position is that the gap is unmeasured, that you are allowed to keep a practice inside it, and that a practice kept there is one you will still be doing in five years.

Testing your own claim against the height

Take your own central claim and place it against the four layers. Which one does it need? If the answer is the base, you can test it this week with a cheap sensor. If the answer is the top layer, you are running a theory of mind and you should say so. If it needs something from outside all four, you have a belief, and a belief is a fine thing to hold as long as you have not filed it as a finding.

This exercise takes ten minutes and it is the single most useful thing in this whole structure. It turns an undifferentiated sense of power into a set of separate questions, and separate questions can be answered separately.

Where the shape misleads

The ladder invites a reading where higher means truer, and it does not. The model at the top is not more established than the measurement at the bottom, it is just more interesting. Reading the stack as a hierarchy produces a practitioner who has abandoned measurement because the explanation felt better.

It also invites the opposite mistake, which is to treat the base as the whole story and conclude that nothing is happening. A ten-minute change in heart rate is not a small thing to experience. It is a well-measured thing, and being well-measured is a description of the evidence rather than a verdict on the practice.

The structure worth keeping

Four layers, each with its own failure mode, and no layer permitted to support a claim that belongs to another. The base is yours to measure. The middle has a known size. The calibration layer is arithmetic. The top is a model. And the claim that a ritual treats a condition has no floor anywhere in the stack, which is worth knowing before someone tells you otherwise.

The sentence I keep is shorter than the structure and does most of the work: the ritual changes your body, the symbol tells you what to do with that, and neither of those facts requires a third one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the research actually support about ritual practice?

That a fixed sequence changes breathing and heart rhythm within minutes, which is measurable, and that expectancy has real and modest effects on some reported outcomes. It does not support the claim that a ritual treats a condition, and the popular claim and the evidence are about different subjects rather than in conflict.

Why does the five-symbol card deck matter if it proves nothing?

Because it is arithmetic. Five symbols means one chance in five, regardless of who is guessing, and running a hundred calls gives you a calibration point you can practise against. Almost everyone who runs them first finds they had been counting selectively, which is a useful thing to learn before trying anything that cannot be checked.

Is the predictive processing account evidence that magic works?

No, and the confusion is worth naming. It is a model that explains why expectation can change experience without anyone consciously deceiving themselves. An explanation that accounts for a phenomenon is not evidence for the phenomenon, and treating the model as the mechanism is where most of the overreach in this area comes from.

Does any of this mean ritual practice is pointless?

Not at all. Attention, expectation, rhythm, symbolism and record-keeping are all real tools with measurable effects, and none of them requires belief in a mechanism. What the evidence does is narrow which claims survive. A smaller claim you can check is worth more than a large one you can only believe.

How do I use this on my own practice?

Place your central claim against the four layers and ask which one it needs. If it needs the autonomic change, you can test it this week with a cheap sensor. If it needs the model, you are running a theory of mind and should describe it that way. Ten minutes of that is worth more than most of the reading on the subject.
