Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 11 min read

Every tradition has a vocabulary, and every vocabulary has words that do more work than the others and words that mostly travel. This account is a year of testing the basic terms against actual practice rather than against the texts that define them, and the result is a short list. Six words turn out to carry almost the whole method: experiment, sigil, charge, aim, results, and paradox. Each of them has a tight definition, and each of them obliges you to something specific. Two further words are much more widely used than they are useful, and one of those two I stopped using entirely after a year of noticing what it did to my reasoning. The glossary that follows is not a summary of the tradition. It is the subset of the vocabulary that survived contact with a real year of trying to work this way, with the failures attached to the words rather than filed separately.

The vocabulary, tested against a real year

Reading the foundational texts gives you a vocabulary with a lot of furniture in it. Liber Null and Liber Kaos use a deliberately narrow set of terms and spend most of their length on the aims, which is why they are short and why they are still in use. The wider tradition adds words at the edges, and the edges are where the precision goes. A year of running workings is a filter, and the filter is blunt: it keeps the words that made me do something and discards the ones that made me feel that something was being done.

The reason to test the vocabulary rather than just adopt it is that vocabulary is load-bearing in a specific and unnoticed way. If your word for the central operation is a noun, you will collect nouns. If it is a verb, you will do things. The whole difference between a practice that accumulates and a practice that decorates is contained in which part of speech the tradition hands you for the central move, and the year of testing was largely a year of noticing which part of speech I was using for each of them.

Six words that do most of the work

Experiment, sigil, charge, aim, results, and paradox. Experiment is a verb and it is the only one of the six that describes the whole activity rather than a step. Sigil is a noun for a compression, and its operational value is that you have to forget it deliberately. Charge is a verb meaning to hold attention on the thing you have just compressed, and it is the step most often replaced by enthusiasm. Aim is a noun that should be written in the imperative. Results is a noun that obliges you to keep a record. Paradox is the one people quote and the one that does the least work, which is worth saying plainly because it is the most quoted.

Six words is enough to run a practice for a year without a purchase or a teacher, and the fact that it is enough is itself a finding. The vocabulary I began the year with was about four times this size and produced no more results, because the additional words were describing rather than instructing.

What each word obliges you to do

Experiment obliges you to a stated outcome in advance. Not a favourable outcome, a stated one, which is a much lower bar and the only version of the word that does any work. Sigil obliges you to a sentence, since you cannot compress a wish and only a sentence can be compressed. Charge obliges you to an amount of time, since attention held flat for four minutes is a different act from attention engaged for four minutes. Aim obliges you to a countable behaviour, since an aim you cannot count is a mood.

Results obliges you to a record that exists before the outcome does, and this is the only one of the six that is genuinely difficult. Paradox obliges you to nothing at all, which is why it belongs in a different category. The test I ended up using on every candidate term was whether it can be disobeyed and whether the disobedience is visible. A term that can be quietly disobeyed is not load-bearing, and the six above are the ones where the disobedience would show up in the record.

Where loose usage does the damage

Loose usage does its damage in one specific way: it lets a practice look like it is running when nothing is running. The most common instance is using experiment to mean trying things, which is a definition with no constraint in it. If your experiment has no outcome you could be wrong about, then the word describes your year and tells you nothing about any week in it. The second instance is charge, which in loose usage becomes a synonym for intention, and intention is free.

A third instance is results, which in loose usage becomes a story. This is the dangerous one because it is the point at which a self-monitoring scheme turns into a self-confirming one, and the mechanism is not dishonesty. The mind generating a narrative that connects the practice to an outcome is doing exactly what minds do, and the narrative will feel true because it is coherent, and coherence is not evidence. The word results is worth keeping in the vocabulary precisely because it names the thing that has to be counted before the story is written.

Experiment, and the flattering version

There are two versions of experimentation and the difference between them is the entire point of the word. The first states an outcome in advance and accepts whatever comes. The second states a feeling in advance, which is a paraphrase of the first, and accepts the feeling. The second is the flattering version and it is used far more often than the first, mostly by people who have not noticed the switch because both feel like open-mindedness.

The switch is easy to make and hard to notice, because the flattering version never produces a moment where you have to say the thing failed. I made it twice in the year and both times the experiment was about a framing rather than a technique, which is where the temptation lives. A technique is easy to falsify and a framing is not, because a framing is a way of seeing and cannot be caught being wrong by a single fortnight. Stating the outcome in advance is what closes the exit.

A year of results, counted rather than remembered

At the end of the year I counted rather than remembered, and the count is duller than the memory. Twenty-six workings attempted. Fourteen produced something I could point at. Six produced a result that arrived later and outside the fortnight I had predicted, which is the category people usually leave out when they tell you about a good year. Six produced nothing distinguishable, and three of those six I would now call misfired rather than failed, because the aim turned out to be one I did not actually want.

The interesting number is the six that produced nothing. If the year had been told as a story I would have reported the fourteen and probably the six late ones, and the six blanks would have become an anecdote about a hard patch. Counted, the six blanks are the largest single category of non-result in the year, and that is the number that should have changed what I did. It did not change what I did, which is the more interesting finding.

The word I stopped using

I stopped using real, and specifically real magick, and the reason is more than aesthetic. The word carries a commitment to the empirical that the practice does not honour, and a commitment you do not honour is worse than no commitment, because it gives you a way to describe a favourable week as a confirmation. Once the practice is called real, the word sets up a comparison with the physical world, and a practice that cannot enter that comparison loses it by default.

What replaced it was either nothing, or a description of what was done. This sounds like a downgrade in eloquence and it is an upgrade in accuracy. Aiming at a specific countable behaviour and calling it a practice rather than a piece of real magick is a description that can be checked, and the description is the part that carries information. The word I dropped was not load-bearing in any sense except the sense of making things sound better than they are.

The word I kept

I kept paradox, but for a narrower reason than the tradition gives it. The tradition uses it to license contradiction. The use that earned its place for me is much smaller: paradox is a good name for the state in which a practice works and you cannot say why, and having a word for that state is worth something because it prevents you from filling the gap with a story. The word is a placeholder that stays a placeholder.

The narrower use also stops paradox from functioning as a trump card. In loose usage, any contradiction in the material can be resolved by invoking paradox, and a rule that resolves everything resolves nothing. Used narrowly, as a name for a specific unexplicable outcome, it marks a place where you do not yet know something. That is a different job and it is the one that survived the year.

What the movement actually contributed

The durable contribution was methodological and it is smaller than the reputation suggests. What the early seventies made acceptable was testing a claim instead of inheriting it, and the practical mechanism was a ritual small enough to run, cheap enough to repeat, and specified enough to be wrong about. Ordo Templi Ouroborus in 1976 is the conventional start and the two pamphlets that follow it are short, opinionated, and mostly about aims rather than doctrine.

What did not follow from it was a body of results. Fifty years on, the honest summary is that a great many people ran a great many small workings and very few of them published anything that could be checked, and the field's preference for testimony over record is a direct result of the founding documents being pamphlets rather than protocols. The method is real. The results are mostly private, and treating the method without the record is how the movement ends up where it is.

The two claims I stopped making

I stopped claiming that results follow from intention, and I stopped claiming that any particular symbolism is doing causal work. The first was abandoned after noticing how often the claim was used to explain an outcome in advance rather than to predict one, which makes it a description with a causal word attached. The second was abandoned because I could not find a version of it I would be willing to defend to someone who knew the history of the term.

What replaced them is narrower and does more work. I claim that a written aim, a compressed charge, and a fortnight of countable follow-through are a way of making a commitment harder to abandon, and that the abandonment is the thing most worth noticing. That claim is falsifiable in a way the originals are not, and being able to say a thing that could turn out to be wrong is what the movement's own method buys you.

The terms worth passing on

The terms worth passing on are the six, and passing them on means being able to state the obligation each one carries without the tradition attached. Experiment with a stated outcome. Sigil as a compression to be deliberately forgotten. Charge as a fixed amount of held attention. Aim in the imperative and countable. Results as a record that opens before the outcome. Paradox as a name for a specific gap rather than a resolution of any contradiction.

A person given those six can run a practice for a year and evaluate it. A person given the wider vocabulary can run a practice indefinitely and never evaluate it, because the wider vocabulary is mostly made of words that describe the tradition rather than constrain the practitioner. The test for whether you have understood a term is whether you can be disobeyed by it, and that test eliminates most of the vocabulary of this field.

A one-page glossary you could hand someone

The glossary is one page and it has a column for the definition and a column for the obligation. Experiment: a test with an outcome you could be wrong about, and the obligation is to write the outcome first. Sigil: a compression of a sentence, and the obligation is to forget it on purpose. Charge: held attention on the compression, and the obligation is a fixed number of minutes. Aim: a sentence in the imperative describing a behaviour, and the obligation is that it can be counted.

Results: a record opened before the outcome, and the obligation is a required entry on bad days. Paradox: a name for an outcome you cannot account for, and the obligation is to leave it unexplained. Six rows, two columns, no belief required. That page is the entire apparatus I would hand to someone who asked what this costs and what it buys, and it is a thinner document than the tradition and a more useful one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which six terms does basic chaos magic practice actually need?

Experiment, sigil, charge, aim, results, and paradox. Each has a tight definition and each imposes an obligation: an outcome stated in advance, a sentence to compress, a fixed amount of held attention, a countable imperative aim, a record that opens before the outcome, and a placeholder for an outcome you cannot explain. Six terms are enough to run and evaluate a practice for a year.

What is the difference between real experiment and the flattering version?

The real version states an outcome in advance and accepts whatever comes, including a clean failure. The flattering version states a feeling in advance, which is a paraphrase of the first and accepts the feeling, so it never produces a moment where you have to say the thing failed. The switch usually happens with framings rather than techniques, because a framing cannot be caught being wrong by a single short run.

Why did the word results matter more than the others?

Because it is the only one that can be quietly disobeyed and the disobedience is invisible until you look for it. Once results becomes a story rather than a count, the practice becomes self-confirming, and the mechanism is not dishonesty: minds generate coherent narratives connecting a practice to an outcome, and coherence is not evidence. Keeping results as a countable object is the defence.

What did the movement contribute that is still worth using?

Something methodological and modest: testing a claim instead of inheriting one became acceptable, and the ritual it supplied is small enough to run, cheap enough to repeat, and specific enough to be wrong about. What it did not produce is a body of checkable results, largely because the founding documents were pamphlets rather than protocols and the field has kept preferring testimony to record.

How do you tell a load-bearing term from a decorative one?

Ask whether the term can be disobeyed and whether the disobedience would be visible. A term that can be quietly ignored is not carrying the practice. Load-bearing terms force something you can count: sessions, minutes, entries, aims. Decorative terms describe a tradition, a lineage, or a quality, and nothing happens if you stop observing them, which is exactly what makes them safe to carry for years.
