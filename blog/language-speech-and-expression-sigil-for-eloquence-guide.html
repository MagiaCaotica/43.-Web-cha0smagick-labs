Language, Speech and Expression

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

There is no better target in this whole tradition than language, and it has received almost none of the attention it deserves. Every other working asks a power to change something in the world. A working aimed at speech asks for nothing at all: it organises a capability you already have, in a body you already carry, according to a method that has been known to work for centuries and is used daily by people who have never heard of sigils. Spaced repetition, deliberate retrieval, and practice in front of a mirror are unglamorous, widely replicated, and exactly the sort of thing a compressed sentence is good at committing you to. The throat and tongue correspondence in the western tradition adds a symbolic layer that is pleasant and entirely optional. What follows takes the method seriously first and the correspondence second, in that order, because the method is what will still be useful to you in ten years.

The instrument you already carry

A sigil works by compressing a sentence you have already decided to act on, and the sentence has to be something you could act on. Language is unusually well suited to this because the abilities involved are continuous, trainable, and already partly yours. You can already speak. What is usually missing is a schedule, and a schedule is exactly what a glyph supplies: a compressed instruction that you do not have to re-derive on the days when re-derivation is least likely.

This is why a language working is more likely to produce a visible result than almost any other area, and also why it can feel anticlimactic. The result is not a change in what you are capable of saying. It is that you did the boring thing often enough, for long enough, that the capacity grew. A practitioner who expected something else will read a successful working as a failure, and then stop doing it.

Why a sentence changes behaviour more reliably than a charm

Language is the most directly effective symbolic technology humans have built, and the evidence for that is not occult. A written instruction reliably changes the behaviour of someone who cannot read the language it is written in. A warning label, a price tag, a legal notice, a set of house rules: each one modifies conduct across a language barrier, through no channel except its being a stable arrangement of symbols that everyone in a society has agreed to treat as meaningful.

That is the mechanism worth naming here, and it is entirely mundane. A glyph is a compression of an instruction, and instructions work by being legible to a system that already knows how to act on them. When you charge a glyph, you are not doing anything to it. You are making yourself the system that acts on it, which is why the charge is best understood as the moment you commit to the instructions the glyph contains.

The throat correspondence, and what it does not claim

The western correspondence tradition places the throat and tongue under creative expression and under will, and a good deal of symbolic practice attaches to them. As a symbolic claim this is unobjectionable and rather elegant: the throat is the organ that converts intention into public form, so it belongs with both the making and the asserting of things. As an anatomical or causal claim it has nothing behind it, and there is no reason to pretend otherwise.

Keeping the two apart is easy and worth the trouble, because a correspondence read as a mechanism produces disappointments that get attributed to a failure of technique. Read as a symbol, the throat correspondence does something more modest and still useful: it gives you a place to look when you are trying to understand why one intention produces fluent speech and another produces hesitation, and it does so without requiring any claim about energy.

Turning a wish to speak into a countable practice

The most common aim in this area is to speak better, and it is unusable because better is not countable. Narrow it until it is. Not fluency, but ten minutes of speaking aloud with nobody to correct you, four times a week. Not confidence, but three conversations a week in which you let a sentence finish before you qualified it. Not vocabulary, but twenty retrieved words a day rather than twenty read. Each of these can be marked true or false, and the difference matters because a countable aim is one you can keep when the week is bad.

The narrowing also tells you something. Somebody who says they want to speak better and cannot produce a countable version of that usually has a specific situation in mind and has not named it. Find the situation, name it, and the countable version usually writes itself. The aim was never about capability in general; it was about a particular kind of conversation in a particular room, and the glyph is a far better instrument when pointed at that.

The retrieval schedule the sigil can commit you to

Spaced repetition and deliberate retrieval are among the most thoroughly replicated findings in second-language acquisition, and their practical consequence is a schedule rather than an effort. A sigil cannot give you better spacing, but it can hold the sentence in front of you at the moment you would otherwise skip, and a compressed statement of when to do the thing is precisely the kind of instruction that gets abandoned under fatigue.

This is the load-bearing part of a language working and it is also the least interesting to write about, which is a good sign. The method is already known, already validated, and available without any ritual. What the sigil adds is a commitment device that does not require motivation on the days when motivation is the thing that is missing, and that is a modest but real addition rather than a metaphysical one.

A first evening, hour by hour

Choose the countable version of the aim and write it as one imperative sentence, under fifteen words, with a time in it. Reduce it to a glyph. Charge it once, standing, in a room where you will be doing the practice, not in a special one. Then do the first session immediately rather than tomorrow, because the delay is where most of these workings end and the first session is the only part that is entirely up to you.

Keep the session short enough that you will not dread it. Ten minutes is better than forty for the first month, and the reason is that a dreaded session is skipped and a skipped session ends the practice faster than a short one does. Log the date, the duration, and one thing you noticed about your own speech during it. The third column is optional at the start and becomes the most valuable part of the log by week three.

Reading your own speech, recorded

Record yourself. This is uncomfortable for almost everyone the first time and becomes the single most useful diagnostic in the whole practice, because the gap between how you sound in your head and how you sound on a recording is the source of most of what people mean when they say they want to speak better. Almost nobody has heard themselves from outside, and the correction is immediate and unflattering and completely free.

Do not practise from the recording; that produces a slightly stilted imitation. Use it as a description. Note the points where you rushed, the sentence openings you fall back on, and the number of times you apologised before making a point. Those are habits, and habits are countable, and a habit that has been counted is much easier to charge than a general sense of not being good enough.

The week the anxiety came back

Speech rate and fluency are reliably affected by anxiety, and this is where a language working intersects with the rest of your practice in a way that is easy to misread. If your fluency improved over several weeks and then a bad week arrived, it is tempting to treat the regression as evidence that the working lapsed. The more likely explanation is that something else got harder, and the fluency is responding to that rather than to anything you did or failed to do.

The honest test is whether you performed the practice during the bad week, not whether you felt fluent. Someone who sat for ten minutes a day through a difficult fortnight and stopped speaking well in meetings has a working in progress. Someone who felt fluent for a good month and skipped the practice entirely has a pleasant memory. Only the log distinguishes these, and the log is the whole reason to keep one.

What fluency practice and ritual practice share

They share a structure, and the structure is the reason this area of the tradition works better than most. A fixed sequence, repeated at a fixed time, producing a state that is more available afterwards. That is the shape of a sitting practice, a warm-up routine, and any number of physical warm-ups, and it is shared by ritual sequences for reasons that are probably not mysterious. The effect is the effect of a rehearsed transition, and calling it something else does not improve it.

The place where ritual and rehearsal genuinely differ is in what the sequence is pointed at. A warm-up rehearses a physical transition. A ritual sequence, in the version this tradition prefers, rehearses a transition into a particular kind of attention: not relaxed, not excited, but flat and sustained. That is a real and learnable state, and it is why a language working benefits from a short charged sequence before each session rather than only at the beginning of the month.

Where the correspondence reading misleads

The misleading move is to go from the throat being the seat of expression to the throat being capable of being adjusted. Read symbolically, the correspondence tells you where to aim. Read causally, it starts to look as though intention directed at the throat should produce audible changes, and then a month of visualisation alongside a month of no practice produces a result that is entirely attributable to the visualisation and entirely misinterpreted as something else.

There is a cheap and reliable correction. Any practice in this area that produces a result in a month where the countable work was not done should be treated as unproven regardless of what else was happening. This rule is dull, it applies to every working, and it is the reason the throat correspondence is presented here as a source of images rather than as a technique.

A month of measured sessions rather than impressions

At the end of the month, read the count first and the impressions second. If you did what you said you would do, roughly on the number of occasions you said you would do it, the working is doing whatever workings do and the exact mechanism is beside the point. If the count is short, the shortfall is the finding, and no amount of fluency recovered in the second half of the month makes the first half a success.

The two columns worth comparing are the count and a line about the situation you were actually in. Language ability is specific: a person can become markedly better at presentations and no better at conversation with friends, and the difference has nothing to do with the method's quality and everything to do with what was practised. Writing down which situations the sessions were in turns a general improvement into a specific one, and specific improvements are the ones you can repeat.

The sentence you would keep

If you keep one thing from a language working, keep the sentence rather than the glyph, and keep the version you would be willing to read out loud. Something like: four times a week I will speak for ten minutes without editing myself. It is countable, it is specific about the thing that is hard, and it does not require anyone to believe anything to understand why you are doing it. That is the property you want in a compressed statement of intent, and most aims in this tradition do not have it.

The rest of the apparatus, including the glyph, the charge and the correspondence, is worth having for reasons that are not mechanical. A sequence you have set up once and can recall in ten seconds is a more reliable doorway into the state you want than a fresh decision every morning. That is true of a ritual and it is true of a cold shower, and saying so is not a demotion of the tradition. It is a description of what the tradition is actually doing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is language a good target for a sigil?

Because the abilities involved are continuous, trainable and already partly present, so a compressed instruction can be attached to them. Unlike most working targets, nothing has to be granted by anything external: the sentence is a commitment to a schedule for a skill the practitioner already partly has, which makes a visible result far more likely.

What does the throat and tongue correspondence mean?

The western tradition places the throat and tongue under creative expression and under will, symbolically, because the throat converts intention into public form. Read as a symbol it gives you a place to look when understanding why one intention produces fluent speech and another produces hesitation. Read as a mechanism it has nothing behind it and produces disappointment.

How does a sigil help with learning a language?

Through spaced repetition and deliberate retrieval, which are among the most replicated findings in second-language acquisition. A glyph cannot give you better spacing, but it holds the sentence in front of you at the moment you would otherwise skip, and a compressed statement of when to do the thing survives the days when motivation is missing.

Why should I record myself speaking?

Because the gap between how you sound in your head and how you sound on a recording is the source of most of what people mean by saying they want to speak better. Almost nobody has heard themselves from outside. Use the recording as a description of habits, not as material to imitate, since practising from it produces a stilted copy.

How long should a language session be for a beginner?

Short enough that you will not dread it, which for most people in the first month means ten minutes rather than forty. A dreaded session gets skipped, and a skipped session ends the practice faster than a short one does. Count the sessions rather than the minutes, and log the situations so that a general improvement can be narrowed to a specific one.
