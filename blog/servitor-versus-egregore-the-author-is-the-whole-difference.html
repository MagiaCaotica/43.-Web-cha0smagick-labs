Servitors and Egregores: The Author Is the Whole Difference

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

The secret usually promised about servitors and egregores is that the second one is a stronger version of the first. That framing is backwards, and it is backwards in a way that causes real damage. An egregore is a servitor specification with the author removed. Remove the author and you remove the two fields that make the construct workable: the refusal clause, which is the only statement of what it will not do, and the review date, which is the only mechanism that ever retires anything. What is left is a construct that runs indefinitely, cannot be corrected by anyone, and cannot be stopped. That is not a more powerful magic. It is an unmaintained one.

The same mechanism at two scales

Both are thoughtforms sustained by attention, and in chaos magick the attention does not have to come from anybody in particular. A sigil you release and then forget still runs. A sentence a group repeats still runs. The sustained attention is the engine in both cases, so from the outside the two look like the same phenomenon at different volumes.

The design difference is not volume. It is provenance. Somebody wrote down what the thing is for, what it will refuse, and when it gets looked at again. That somebody is not a decorative detail. They are the only component in the system capable of noticing that it has stopped being useful.

This is why the framing of a stronger collective version misleads. The material does not gain power when more people attend to it. It loses the fields that let you tell whether the power is doing anything. A construct with an author can be wrong and be told so. A construct without one can only be interpreted, and interpretation has no failure state.

What the author actually supplies

Four things, and the first two are the ones that get dropped.

A specification in the imperative mood. One sentence, readable by a stranger, that says whether it ran this week. Without an author nobody decided what counts as running, so there is no unit to count.

A refusal clause. The list of things it will decline to do, plus what it does when the conditions are wrong. Nobody else in the system is positioned to notice a limit being crossed, so this is the only record of them that will ever exist.

A review date. Fixed at the build, not chosen at the review. A date chosen when you already feel the answer you want is not a review.

A name. One or two words, no adjectives, no animal, no colour, and never the function. A construct named for what it does will collide with the next one that does the same thing.

Drop the refusal and you have something that expands. Drop the review and you have something permanent. Most unbounded trouble in this area comes from those two omissions rather than from anything anyone did.

The transference problem, which is the actual operational secret

A group egregore is fed by the group's attention. That sounds like a strength and it is a specific, predictable weakness.

Attention is not evenly distributed in a group. It clusters on whoever is currently preparing something, whoever is hosting, whoever is newest, and whoever is anxious. So the construct that was built to represent seven people gets shaped, sustained, and answered by the two of them who are present, and it will report back in their terms.

The result is a shared construct that functions as an elaborate private one. You ask it a question and it returns the answer of whoever in the group is most available. Because the construct has no author, nobody in the group is in a position to notice that the two of you have been having a private conversation through it for six weeks.

This is checkable without any supernatural premise. Ask the group to write their answers independently, then compare what each person says the construct told them. If three people genuinely get three different answers from the same shared object, the object is reflecting rather than answering, and that is worth knowing before you build anything on it.

Why a group construct is harder to retire than a personal one

A servitor can be retired because one person decided to retire it. The withdrawal of a specification ends it. Nobody has to agree and nobody has to be told.

A group construct has no such single point. To retire it you need either everyone to stop attending to it, or one person to declare it finished, or an occasion strong enough that the attention moves on by itself. All three are fragile, and the second one produces an argument.

This is where the folklore about these things stops being reassuring. The traditional posture toward large collective constructs is one of respect, and the respect is not superstition, it is bookkeeping. A construct you cannot retire is a standing obligation you cannot discharge, and the pressure from standing obligations that cannot be discharged does not stay where you put it.

The practical rule follows directly: build a personal construct when the function is yours, and build a shared one only when the function genuinely requires more than one person, and write down what happens when the group dissolves. If nobody can answer that, the construct has no termination condition, and you have built something you will have to move away from rather than something you can put down.

The measurement problem, stated honestly

A personal servitor is countable. You wrote the function, so you know what counting it means, and the quarterly number is the entire review. A shared construct has no such unit, because no individual wrote a function that a stranger could check.

The closest available proxy is whether the construct's behaviour changes when attention is withdrawn, and the difficulty is that you cannot withdraw other people's attention as an experiment. You can only withdraw your own. A construct that continues to feel present when you have stopped attending to it is either being sustained by the others, which is what it was for, or it is being sustained by your own expectation, which is a result about you rather than about the construct.

There is no version of this that produces a clean answer, and the material promising one is selling you a number. The honest position is that a collective construct is a thing you maintain rather than a thing you test, and the maintenance is the commitment. Decide whether you want that commitment before you build, not after.

Six questions before you build either kind

Is the function mine, or does it genuinely need a group? If the answer is mine and a group is available, build the personal one, because the personal one is countable.

What will it refuse? If you cannot write that sentence, you are not ready, and no amount of ritual will substitute for it.

What is the review date, and what is written down now that counts as the acceptable conclusion? A conclusion written at the review date is a justification.

What happens when the group dissolves? If the answer is nothing, that is the design and you should be comfortable with it explicitly.

Who retires it, and do they have the standing to do so alone? If the answer is a committee, the construct will outlive the committee.

What does it cost? The honest cost of a personal construct on a small function is a notebook and about twenty minutes. The common real expense in this area is a subscription, and a subscription converts a practice into a recurring decision you keep re-making, which is the opposite of what the construct is supposed to do.

How the two map onto a working week

A personal specification is the right instrument for anything you do weekly on your own account. Flagging an overdue item, noticing a recurring obligation, discarding a backlog entry you have been carrying. Small, boring, countable, and the count is the entire review.

A shared construct earns its complexity when the function is genuinely collective and no individual can perform it. A recurring meeting that keeps missing the same decision, a group practice with a schedule nobody holds, a shared piece of knowledge that keeps getting re-derived. In these cases the group genuinely is the mechanism.

The mistake is running a shared construct for something a person could do, because a group feels more powerful and the feeling is the only difference. The cost of that choice is an unowned construct that nobody can correct, in exchange for a sense of scale that the evidence does not support.

If you are unsure, the personal version is the cheaper error. You can always convert a specification into a shared one later, and you cannot convert a shared one back without starting over, because the shared one has no author to ask.

What the tradition keeps right about this

The older material treats personal constructs as workshop tools and collective ones as something you approach carefully. That is not superstition and it is not marketing. It is a description of the maintenance burden, written by people who did not have a way to make the burden smaller.

The two errors available here are symmetrical and both are common. One is building a shared construct for a personal function and ending up responsible for something you cannot quit. The other is insisting everything is personal, refusing any collective practice, and discovering that some work genuinely needs more than one person and cannot be done alone.

The usable position is instrumental rather than spiritual. Ask what the construct is for, who holds its refusal, who reviews it, and who retires it. If all four have real answers, the question of whether anything supernatural is involved becomes much less important than whether the design is sound, because the design is the part you control and the part that determines whether you keep doing this in a year.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do a servitor and an egregore actually differ from each other?

The mechanism is the same either way: sustained attention holds a thoughtform in place. What differs is who stands behind it. A servitor has one author, who wrote the function, the refusal, and the review date, and who can retire it alone. An egregore is fed by attention that is not individually anyone's, so nobody wrote a function a stranger could check, nobody stated a refusal, and nobody can retire it on their own authority. The difference is not strength. It is whether the construct is maintainable.

Is an egregore more powerful than a servitor?

There is no good reason to think so, and the material that claims it is usually selling the group version. Sustained attention is the engine in both cases, so a construct fed by seven people is not more potent than one fed by one, it is fed by a source that is harder to observe and impossible to withdraw as an experiment. The practical reason people reach for the collective version is that it feels larger, and the cost of that feeling is an unowned construct that nobody can correct.

Can I build an egregore with friends?

Yes, and it works best when the function genuinely requires more than one person, such as a recurring meeting that keeps missing the same decision or a shared schedule nobody holds. Before you start, answer two questions in writing: what happens when the group dissolves, and who can retire it alone. If nobody can retire it alone, the construct will outlive the group that made it, and that is a standing obligation you cannot discharge. Also write each person's answer to the same question independently and compare them, because attention in a group clusters on whoever is currently most available.

How do I know if a collective construct is working or just reflecting?

You cannot get a clean answer, and any material that gives you one is inventing it. The best available check is to have each member write down what the construct told them for the same question, separately, and compare. Three genuinely different answers from three people means the construct is reflecting the most attentive member rather than answering, which is worth knowing before you build anything on it. What you cannot do is withdraw the group's attention as an experiment, so a collective construct is maintained rather than tested.

Is it worth paying for an app to run these?

The mechanics are free and the tools on this site already cover them, so a paid app is buying scheduling, a log, and a library rather than a method. The honest cost of a personal construct on a small weekly function is a notebook and about twenty minutes. The most common real expense in this area is a subscription, and a subscription converts a practice into a recurring decision you keep re-making, which is the opposite of what the construct is meant to do. If you want the log and the schedule in one place, a one-time purchase suits this work better than anything that bills you monthly.
