RONDA

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Money is the most popular target for occult practice and the worst one, and the reason for both is the same. It is a quantity that argues back. Every other aim a practitioner might name is difficult to falsify, because the outcome depends on other people, on chance, or on the practitioner's own judgement. Money can be counted. That property makes it a superb training ground for anyone who wants to know whether their method does anything, and it makes it a torture chamber for anyone who does not, because the number will be there every morning with or without the sigil.

The strongest argument for, and the reason it is the objection

The case for aiming a practice at money is good and nobody should dismiss it. Money is universal, quantifiable, and legible, which means it is the one domain where you can build a practice, run it for a month, and find out what happened. Ambition, clarity, health, love: all of these have outcomes that cannot be cleanly attributed. Money does, at least partially, and that is a genuinely valuable property for a beginner.

It is also exactly why it is a poor target for a first substantial investment of attention. The moment the aim is countable, the practice acquires a stake in the count, and a stake in the count is a stake in a particular explanation. You will find yourself attributing every improvement to the sigil and every absence to an error in the working. Neither conclusion is available to you, and both are actively encouraged by the structure of the aim.

Money as a quantity that argues back

The number appears every morning and it does not care what you did last night. This is an excellent property for a measuring instrument and a terrible one for a mood. Most practices fail silently, and the failure looks like nothing at all. A money aim is a practice with a daily readout, and the readout will tell you things you did not want to hear.

The other consequence is that the aim becomes entangled with worth. A practice aimed at a number is very difficult to keep separate from an assessment of yourself, and the identification is easy to miss because it happens through repetition rather than through a single decision. If the first month produces no change at all, the most likely explanation available to you is something about the sigil, and the structure of the aim makes that the easiest interpretation to reach.

The borrowed twelve, and what it is worth

The numerals 741 are used constantly in money practice, and the reason is arithmetic: seven plus four plus one is twelve, and twelve is a figure that recurs in temperament charts and in numerological systems, so the number appears to be confirmed. This is a borrowing. It is a real and interesting pattern in a body of symbolic material, and it is not a mechanism by which digits influence revenue.

Say so in your own notes, because the alternative is a slow drift into a numerology that cannot be wrong. If a practice can be justified by any arrangement of the digits, then the arrangement can be changed to justify any result, and the practice has lost the capacity to tell you anything. The number twelve is a decent hook for a piece of imagery. It is not evidence.

Why the sigil is a command and not a wish

A sigil is a compression of an imperative sentence, and the mood matters more than practitioners usually acknowledge. I will invoice on the first rather than the last. I will raise my rate for the second client this month. I will stop doing the work that is not paid. Written in the imperative, addressed to yourself, in the present tense, and then forgotten, that sentence becomes the thing you do.

Written as a wish, it becomes a thing you hope for. A wish has no agent and no tense, and it survives contact with a difficult week no better than a resolution does. The distinction is easy to state and easy to lose, because a wish and a command feel similar from the inside and both produce a feeling of having done something when you build the glyph. Write the sentence in the imperative, read it aloud, and if it sounds like a request, rewrite it.

Nobody has ever audited a wish

The obvious objection to any practice is that the participants want it to work, and the ordinary defences are that the method is honest, that the practitioners are serious, that the practice is symbolic rather than causal. None of those defences has ever been tested against a record, and no serious practitioner in this field publishes one, which is a reasonable thing to notice rather than a reason to abandon the work.

What you can do is audit yourself, and the audit is easy: a running column of numbers that has nothing to do with the sigil, kept without reference to the practice. If the practice is doing anything at all, the count should be readable by someone who thinks it is nonsense. That is the only audience worth convincing, and it is the only one whose agreement would tell you something you did not already believe.

What a money working can and cannot move

A practice of this kind operates on your own conduct, and the list of things that fall inside that boundary is longer than most people expect. What you charge. What you accept. How quickly you follow up. Which work you decline. How many hours you spend on the things that lead to money as against the things that feel like work. These are all decisions you make repeatedly and none of them feel significant when you make them.

What sits outside the boundary is everything else: the market, the timing, the other party's decision, your own circumstances, and the entirely unconnected events that constitute most of what determines a year's income. A practice aimed at money is a practice aimed at the first list, and the second list is where most of the outcome lives. Being clear about which list you are working on is most of the honesty available here.

The first month, audited

Run the practice for a month and keep three columns: what you did, what happened, and what you would have done anyway. The third column is the difficult one and the one that does the work, because most of what improves in a month would have improved anyway, and a record without that column will credit the sigil for the improvement.

Do not change anything else during the month. Practices aimed at money tend to arrive alongside new effort, new offers, new contacts, and new determination, and it becomes impossible to tell which is producing the result. If you want to test whether the sigil does anything, this is the only month where the variable count is one, and it is the month most people spend changing five things at once.

A ledger that cannot be argued with

A real ledger, kept daily, with dated entries and no retrospective adjustments, is the most useful object in this whole area and it costs nothing. Its value is not that it will show the practice working. Its value is that it will not let you be vague, and vagueness is the failure mode that the entire literature on manifestation is organised around.

Keep the accounts boring. Number of invoices sent, number of quotes followed up, hours billed, number of uncollected invoices chased. These are countable, they are yours, and they sit upstream of the number you actually care about. A month of those entries is worth more than a year of daily affirmations, and it is the only part of the practice that will still be informative in two years.

Where the aim drifts into vagueness

The drift is always the same and it is always gradual. Abundance becomes financial stability, which becomes having more options, which becomes feeling freer, and by the third month the sentence in the notebook could be satisfied by a single good lunch. Nothing about that was an error of technique. The aim simply became untestable, and untestable aims feel successful for exactly as long as you do not examine them.

The repair is to rewrite the sentence in the imperative and check whether you would know on a given day whether you had complied. If compliance is a matter of feeling, the aim has drifted. If compliance is a matter of fact, it has not. This is a stricter standard than most people apply to their aims, and applying it will usually make the aim smaller, which is the correct direction.

A bad month that is not evidence

A month with no change in income is not evidence that the practice failed, for the ordinary reason that most of what determines income is outside the boundary the practice operates on. It is not evidence that it worked either. It is one observation of a variable you do not control, and treating it as a verdict is the error the ledger exists to prevent.

What is evidence is a run of behaviour. Did the numbers in your own column go up, over months, while the aim stayed the same? That is a real signal about something real, and it is worth more than any single month's result. One month of income is noise. Six months of consistent conduct is a pattern, and patterns are the only thing in this field that can distinguish a method from a coincidence.

What the practice is actually for

Strip the claims and the practice is a way of making a set of decisions you have been avoiding into a scheduled, written, countable commitment. That is a genuinely useful thing and it is not nothing. Most people know what they should be doing about money and do not do it, for the ordinary reasons: it is unpleasant, it is uncertain, and nobody is watching.

So it is entirely reasonable to build the sigil and entirely unreasonable to attribute the results to it. Both of those positions can be held at once, and holding both is what makes the practice durable. People who believe the sigil caused their income and people who believe it did nothing are both more likely to abandon the underlying commitment than people who recorded the behaviour and let the interpretation stay open.

Closing the working honestly

Decide in advance when you will stop and write it down, because the decision to continue is made daily in conditions of low information and a slightly elevated sense of having invested something. A working aimed at money is the one most likely to be extended past the point of usefulness, because the belief that it is nearly working is the main thing sustaining it.

When you close it, look at the conduct rather than the balance. Count the sessions, count the entries in your own column, and write a paragraph about what would have happened without the practice. That paragraph is usually the most honest thing in the notebook, and it is the one that makes the next working better than the last, which is the entire return on the exercise.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a money sigil actually affect income?

There is no evidence that a sigil has any effect on revenue, and no serious practitioner in this field asserts one. What the practice can affect is your own conduct: what you charge, what you accept, how quickly you follow up, how many hours go to work that leads to money. Whether those changes are sufficient to move your income is an ordinary economic question that the sigil neither helps nor hinders except by making you attend to it.

Is the number 741 meaningful?

It is a borrowing, not a mechanism. Seven plus four plus one makes twelve, and twelve recurs in temperament and numerological material, which is why the number is satisfying. As a hook for imagery in a working it is fine. As a justification for why a particular number should help you earn money it is circular, because any arrangement of digits could be made to argue the same point.

How long should I run a money working?

Long enough to get a real sample and short enough that the belief does not become self-sustaining. Six weeks is usually enough to establish whether your own conduct changed, and three months is where most people stop being able to distinguish effort from effect. Write the stopping date down at the start, in the same notebook as the aim, because the decision to continue is otherwise made every day in conditions where nobody is watching.

What if nothing changes after a month?

Then look at what you actually did, in numbers, and not at your bank balance. Most people find their conduct column is full and their attribution column is full of confidence, and that combination is the more interesting finding. If the conduct is genuinely consistent, the practice is working as a commitment device and you can keep it on those terms. If the conduct column is empty, nothing about the interpretation matters.

Is aiming a practice at money unethical?

No, and it is worth separating the ethics of the aim from the ethics of the attribution. Aiming at your own income involves no one else's autonomy, so there is no consent question, unlike practices aimed at another person. Attributing the result to a sigil is where the harm usually sits, because it substitutes an unfalsifiable explanation for one you could check, and it discourages the professional help that a genuine financial problem requires.
