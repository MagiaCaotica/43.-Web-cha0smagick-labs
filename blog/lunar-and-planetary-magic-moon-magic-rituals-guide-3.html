Lunar and Planetary Magic

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Lunar and planetary timing is the most popular piece of scaffolding in modern occult practice and the least examined one. The claim underneath it is modest and mostly true: the phase of the moon and the hour of the day are real, observable variables, and using them as a scheduling frame makes a practice easier to begin, easier to keep, and easier to compare across months. The claim on top of it is much larger and much less supported, that particular windows attract particular kinds of outcome. Keeping the two apart is the whole job. What follows treats the lunar cycle and the planetary week as what they are, a calendar and a clock, then shows how to build a working schedule on them, what each window is actually good for, and where practitioners consistently talk themselves into believing more than the frame delivers.

The Cycle Is Observable, and That Is Its Real Strength

The proportion of the moon's visible face that is lit changes on a schedule anyone can check with a calendar, and that proportion correlates with illumination at ground level and with the timing of sunrise and sunset. A dark new moon and a full moon differ in how much light is actually available during a working, which is a physical difference and not a symbolic one. Practitioners who work outdoors at night are responding to a real change in conditions, whatever they call it.

The practical consequence is worth stating plainly: lunar timing is best understood as a scheduling convention that makes a practice repeatable and easy to begin. That is not a trivial benefit. Practices that fail most often fail on the grounds of never being started, and a named window on a shared calendar solves that failure directly.

It also gives you a comparison structure. A working done in each phase across two cycles produces sixteen data points under comparable conditions, which is a better test of a technique than a single strong attempt ever will be.

The Planetary Week Is a Piece of Administrative History

The seven-day week with weekday names assigned to planetary bodies traces to Babylonian and Roman practice, and the mapping is a naming convention rather than a discovered correspondence. Saturn gave Saturday, the sun gave Sunday, Mars gave the middle day that English later renamed. The correspondences then accreted: metals, colours, numbers, times of day, directions, organs, and a body of attributes attached to each of the seven.

Planetary hours partition day and night into twelve segments each, with the ruler rotating forward through the sequence by one each hour. The result is a themed window roughly ninety minutes long that recurs daily with a different ruler. Medieval astrologers used this as a working frame, and it is the ancestor of the timing tables in most modern practice.

The honest position is that the window is a category, not a mechanism. If you work on a Jupiter hour and something goes well, the hour was a reasonable place to have worked. If you work on the same Jupiter hour next week and nothing happens, the hour has not failed you. Treating a category as a cause is the step that turns a useful calendar into a source of superstition.

What Each Phase Is Actually Good For

The obvious question is which phase suits a request for money, and the answer from the table is that it depends entirely on what the money is for. Beginning a business wants a waxing moon. Liquidating something wants a waning moon. Asking a court for a hearing wants a full moon. The framework is more precise once you stop treating it as a lookup table for outcomes and start treating it as a question about what stage your situation is in.

  • New moon for beginnings, planting, and anything that should not be visible yet.
  • Waxing crescent for a request that needs to build momentum before it is exposed.
  • First quarter for the first real friction, which most people try to skip and should not.
  • Full moon for culmination, naming something, and anything involving visibility or testimony.
  • Waning phases for finishing, letting go, clearing, and dismantling what has served its turn.
  • Dark moon for review, rest, and closing accounts with a cycle you have not yet looked at properly.

Building a Working Calendar

Step three sounds trivial and is where most schedules break. A timing system that assumes you are available at a particular hour is a system that quietly waits for you to become someone else. Pick the time you are actually free, then let the planetary hour refine within it.

  1. Choose one request that can run over several cycles rather than a wish that needs a single date.
  2. Pick the phase that matches the stage your request is in, and mark the next four occurrences.
  3. Decide the time of day from your actual routine, not from an idealised schedule.
  4. Add one planetary hour per cycle once the phase schedule is being kept without effort.
  5. Write a prediction with a measure and a date before the first working of the series.
  6. Log the timing you used, the outcome, and your state, on every working including the misses.

A Simple Sequence for a Phase Working

Step six sounds ceremonial and is the most skipped item on the list. Naming what the phase means for the specific request is what converts a timed operation into a working, and it costs about fifteen seconds. A series done without it is sixteen identical rituals with different dates attached.

  1. Confirm the phase and hour you are working in, from a reference rather than from memory.
  2. Write the request as a stage-appropriate sentence, in the first person and present tense.
  3. Draw or write the mark while the specification is still in front of you.
  4. State the timing aloud once, so the window is named rather than assumed.
  5. Sit with the specification for a fixed count, and stop at the count rather than at a feeling.
  6. Close by saying what the phase means for this request, which is the part beginners skip.
  7. Record the date, the phase, the hour, and one line about your state.

Why Timed Practice Fails

The dominant failure is missed windows. A system with a narrow opening that you miss once becomes a system you abandon, and a system that is easy to do late is a system that survives. The repair is to keep a backup convention, such as doing the working within a day of the window rather than skipping it, and to record the fact that it was late.

The second failure is treating a strong result as proof about the timing. A working in a favourable phase will sometimes coincide with whatever the real cause of the outcome was, and the coincidence is indistinguishable from a result unless you recorded a prediction in advance. Prediction before the working, result after, is the only order that produces usable information.

The third is accumulation. Adding the full cross-reference of phase, hour, day and element produces a specification of two hundred and fifty-six combinations, which is a research design rather than a practice. Pick two variables and hold them. The third variable can wait until the first two have produced enough records to be worth crossing.

Testing a Timing Hypothesis

Run a comparison rather than a single test. The design is simple and it costs nothing beyond discipline: hold the request constant, run the same short working in a waxing window and in a waning window across two cycles, and record both. Eight data points, four each, is enough to see an effect that is large.

Add a control when you can. One window per cycle where you do the identical working with no timing claim attached gives you a third column, and the third column is what tells you whether the difference is about the moon or about which weeks you happened to have a good run.

Write the conclusion down, including the possibility that there was no difference. A timing system tested and found to be neutral is still a scheduling tool, so the practice is not wasted, and knowing the answer is worth considerably more than an assumption you never checked.

Tools That Earn Their Place

One thing not worth buying is an elaborate timing apparatus that only your system understands. The value of this material is that anyone can check a moon phase, and a proprietary calendar that cannot be verified has given up the one advantage the frame actually has.

  • A lunar phase reference to check the current phase rather than infer it.
  • A planning tool that shows the next several windows at once, so a missed one is visible in advance.
  • A banishing form for closing, kept simple enough to do without thinking about it.
  • A rune or correspondence reference if your specification uses correspondences, consulted at the writing stage.
  • A tracker for intentions, so a request does not get timed twice in conflicting windows by accident.

Timing as a Discipline Rather Than a Cause

The strongest practitioners in this area are not the ones who find the rarest window. They are the ones who work on a schedule they have kept for years, with a record they have never edited, and who can therefore say something specific about what their timing did for them. That capability is available to anyone willing to keep a log and unavailable to anyone who does not, which is an unusual situation in which the disciplined beginner ends up ahead of the advanced one.

A second habit worth adopting is to treat the timing as a constraint rather than an enhancement. It works harder when it decides when you work, and works less well when it is asked to explain outcomes afterwards. A frame that constrains your behaviour will change your results more than a frame that merely decorates them.

Keep the ordinary version. If a working is due in a window you will miss, do it the next day and write down that you did it late. The record with imperfect timing in it is more valuable than the perfect calendar you abandoned in month three.

Where the Convention Meets the Evidence

The claims worth defending are narrow. The moon's phase is a real variable with observable effects on night-time conditions, and a lunar cycle is an excellent scheduling device. The planetary week is a historical administrative framework that provides a usable vocabulary of domains, and planetary hours are a convenient way to categorise a day into workable windows.

The claims worth declining are broader. Nothing supports the idea that a Jupiter hour increases the chance of a particular external outcome, and the categories are inherited rather than discovered. Where the tradition and the evidence diverge, the divergence is usually in the direction of a story that is more satisfying than the mechanism.

Practices built on the narrow version tend to be kept for years. Practices built on the broad version tend to end in arguments, because the broad version asks for evidence that is not available and the argument can never be settled by doing the practice better.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are planetary hours used for?

They divide the day into themed windows, each ruled by one of the seven traditional bodies, so you can match a task to a category. Work, communication, money and rest each fall under a different ruler, and choosing the window by category rather than by outcome is what makes the system usable at all.

Do I need a birth chart for lunar and planetary work?

No. Astrological timing is helpful for some requests and irrelevant for most of them, and it is not a prerequisite for the basic practice. If you do not know your chart, the phase and hour conventions work fine on their own, and skipping the chart keeps a lot of beginners from spending months on preparation they did not need.

Which phase is best for a request about money?

It depends on the stage rather than on the topic. Beginning a venture wants a waxing phase, finishing or clearing one wants a waning phase, and anything needing to be seen by other people wants a full moon. Treating it as a lookup for outcomes gets you less than treating it as a question about where the request currently stands.

Do I have to work at night for lunar magic?

No, though working with the moon visible is pleasant and makes the timing feel less arbitrary. What matters is that you record the phase and time honestly and repeat the same arrangement across cycles. Daytime workings are entirely valid, and they are also easier to sustain, which usually matters more.

What if I miss a window?

Do the working within a day of it and note that it was late, rather than skipping the cycle. A schedule with a late entry in the log is a usable record, and a schedule that gets abandoned over a missed window produces nothing at all. The backup convention is what keeps a timing practice alive past its first busy week.
