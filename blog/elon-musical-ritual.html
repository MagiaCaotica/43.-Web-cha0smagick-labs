Elon

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

Almost every occult tradition that anthropologists have written down uses sound as part of the work, and almost none of them would recognise the phrase binaural beats. That gap is the whole subject here. A musical working is not a piece of music played while you visualise; it is a deliberate use of rhythm, repetition and volume to put your body into a state you can then act from. The mechanism people reach for first is entrainment, the tendency for neural oscillation to fall into step with an external pulse. Entrainment is real and reasonably studied. Its behavioural consequences are modest, and anyone who tells you a drone at 40 Hz rewires your brain is selling something. What survives the evidence is less dramatic and more useful: tempo couples to breathing, breathing couples to arousal, and a fixed sequence of sounds gives attention somewhere to stand. The second tradition to hold onto is older than the laboratory one, namely that music in ritual has often functioned as a curtain over speech that must be heard without being understood. Both of those give you something to work with. This article builds a musical working from a named piece, keeps the claims narrow enough to survive scepticism, and is honest about the parts of the literature that do not replicate.

What a musical working actually changes

A musical working uses a chosen piece of sound as the carrier of an intention. That is the whole mechanism claimed, and it is worth stating plainly because the phrase has drifted toward implying something more mystical than it deserves. You pick a track, you play it in a defined context, and you use the state it produces to do something you could not have done in a neutral state. The variable being manipulated is you. The music is the handle.

The reason this is worth building rather than improvising is that sound is the one input you cannot easily lie to yourself about. You can decide a sigil charged beautifully. You cannot decide that a 40-minute drone did or did not settle your breathing into a slow rhythm. That gives the musical working a built-in honesty check that most visual methods lack.

Entrainment, and the modest version of the claim

Entrainment names the tendency of neural oscillation to align with an external rhythm. It is a real phenomenon, measurable in the ordinary way, and the modest behavioural consequences are also reasonably well established. A pulse at a steady tempo will pull your own activity toward it. That is not mysticism; it is a system that synchronises to periodic input.

The large claims fail. There is a substantial popular literature asserting that specific frequencies produce specific cognitive states, and controlled studies have not delivered it consistently. If a working depends on a frequency number, the number is decoration. If it depends on a tempo you can feel and a duration you can survive, the tempo is doing something.

Binaural beats, and why the studies keep disagreeing

A binaural beat requires headphones and works by presenting a slightly different frequency to each ear, producing a perceived pitch in the middle rather than an actual tone. The perception is genuine; the entrainment and cognitive claims attached to it are not well supported. Studies disagree often enough that you should treat any specific protocol built on one as an experiment rather than a technique.

There is also a practical problem that gets ignored. The effect only exists for a listener who is wearing headphones and is not moving, which is a narrower window than practitioners imply. If your working needs the stereo effect to work, it needs an hour of stillness, which means the music is the least interesting part of the hour.

Tempo, breath, and the physiology you can feel

Here is the part that survives scrutiny. Tempo couples to breathing. Breathing couples to arousal. Tempo that sits slightly below your resting heart rate tends to pull the breath down with it, and a slower breath with a longer exhale is one of the few reliable non-pharmacological state shifters available without equipment. This is why a 60 bpm piece is a common choice and why it is not magic.

You can test it on yourself in five minutes. Sit quietly for three minutes and count your breaths. Play four minutes of something at a steady 60 and count again. Repeat with something fast and jagged. Run it in that order, on different days, and write the numbers down. You are not proving a theory; you are finding out which tempo you actually respond to, which is the only figure that matters for your own practice.

What the anthropology says about music in ritual

Music appears as a structural component in most of the traditions anthropologists have written down, which is a stronger claim than it first sounds. It is not decoration applied to an otherwise complete rite. It holds the rite together, marks transitions, and in a number of documented cases does something more specific: it masks speech that must be heard without being understood.

That second function is the one most useful for modern work, and it is the one nobody copies. A recorded drone is not a mask, but it does the same job in a different register. If you are reading a long passage and want it to be present without being argued with, sound is the cheapest way to build that arrangement.

Building a sound working from a named piece

Choose one specific recording and write its title into your notebook. Not a genre, not a mood, a title. This sounds pedantic and it is the difference between a working and a preference. Any later question about whether it worked requires knowing exactly what you did, and a genre cannot be reconstructed.

Then write three lines. What tempo is it, roughly, and does it have a pulse you can count. Whether you would be willing to sit through it for forty minutes. Whether it pulls attention inward or outward, which is a judgement you make once and should not revise every session. If the answer to the last question is that it does neither, it is not your carrier.

The charge, with the player running

The charge is the least mysterious part of the working and the part most often skipped. You sit, you start the track, you state the aim in one sentence, and you stay for the length of the track or a fixed number of cycles. The track does the work of occupying the part of your attention that would otherwise narrate.

The discipline is in not stopping early. Stopping at the moment of peak intensity feels significant and produces nothing durable, because the state was never allowed to settle. Treat the charge as a fixed duration you decided in advance, the same way you would decide the length of a run. Intensity is not the objective. Accumulated sessions are.

A session, hour by hour

A workable first session is about an hour. Ten minutes to write the aim as a single imperative sentence and to set the track. Five minutes to sit before you start, so that the change in state has a before to be different from. One full track, or a fixed number of cycles if the piece repeats. Ten minutes afterwards with the notebook, before you analyse anything.

The ten minutes afterwards matter more than they should. The state produced by the track is at its most accessible in the minutes right after it stops, and that is when the material is least organised. Write the aim again, write what came up, and stop. Do not design a second working in the same sitting; the material is too unprocessed to design with.

Keeping a musical working from becoming wallpaper

The predictable failure is that the track becomes background and the working becomes a pleasant interval. You can tell because you realise partway through that you have been thinking about lunch. This is not a discipline failure so much as a design failure: you chose a piece with too little structure, or a duration with no shape.

Fix it by changing one variable at a time. If attention drifts, cut the length rather than adding effort. If the first ten minutes are wasted, put a task in them instead of a silence. If you stop noticing the piece entirely, that piece has finished its job and you need a new one. A carrier you are bored of is not a carrier, it is a habit with extra steps.

What counts as a response

Responses to a musical working come in three rough kinds, and only the first is worth much. The first is a behavioural change you can point at afterwards: you did the thing you had been avoiding, you slept differently, you produced something. The second is a state you could not have reached as easily another way, which is real but hard to separate from the effect of sitting still for an hour.

The third kind is a coincidence you have decided to read as a message. This category grows fast if you keep a list of coincidences, because the number of coincidences available to you is enormous. Track the first kind only. If you want a record, write one line a day with a yes or a no, and leave the interpretation for a month when there is enough of it to look at.

The ethics of using someone else's music

If you build a working on a recorded piece, you are using a recording made by people who were not asked and are not paid. Most ritual-audio discussion skips this because the amounts are trivial, and for a private practice the amounts really are trivial. That is not the same as the question being settled.

Two habits resolve it cheaply. Keep a list of the pieces you rely on, and where you can, pay for the ones you use often. Better, record your own. A single sustained note on any instrument, or even a kettle, gives you material with no third party in it, and you can shape it to the tempo you actually need rather than the tempo the composer chose.

When to stop and just listen

One version of this practice turns into an elaborate justification for listening to music you enjoy, and nothing distinguishes the two from the outside. The tell is that the aim keeps getting more general. Clarity becomes peace becomes a better life, and each rewrite is a little less falsifiable than the last.

That is the moment to put the notebook down. Not as a failure but as a natural endpoint: a piece of music you love, played often, with no claim attached, does most of what the claims were for. If you want the working back later, the aim and the track are written down and it can be restarted in twenty minutes. Everything you would lose by stopping is already on paper.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do binaural beats actually work for magic?

The perceived pitch is real and requires headphones. The entrainment and cognitive benefits claimed for specific frequencies have not replicated consistently in controlled work. Build your working on tempo and duration, which you can measure on yourself, rather than on a frequency number, which you cannot.

How long should a musical working last per session?

Roughly an hour for a first session, structured as writing the aim, sitting, one full track or a fixed number of cycles, and then ten minutes of notes. Consistency across many sessions matters considerably more than length in any single one, and shortening a session you keep skipping is better than preserving a length you never reach.

Can I use a track I listen to for pleasure?

Yes, and many people do. The two conditions that matter are that you can sit through it deliberately and that its tempo or texture actually shifts your state. A favourite piece is often ideal precisely because the association is strong. The failure mode is not the piece but the aim becoming so general that the music is only providing a pleasant frame.

What should I write down afterwards?

One line per session: the date, the track, and a yes or a no for whether you did the thing the aim referred to. Resist interpretation during the first weeks, because the state right after the track is unorganised and will produce confident conclusions that a month of entries will not support.

Is playing music during other rituals a problem?

Not inherently. Sound as a curtain over speech that must be heard without being analysed is a documented function of ritual music, and a low drone under a long reading reproduces that fairly well. The problem appears when the added layer is so loud it competes for the attention the rest of the working needs.

Put this into practice

Tempo and entrainment are measurable, and a journal that records the track alongside the result is what makes the measurement possible.
