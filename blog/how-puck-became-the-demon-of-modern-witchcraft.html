How Puck Became the Demon of Modern Witchcraft

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

Puck is a good case study in how a piece of folklore becomes an occult authority, because every step in the chain is documented and each step changed what the figure was for. A trickster hob in an English play became the court jesters of a fairy mythology, became a name attached to a class of trickster spirits, and was finally listed in a 1740 grimoire as a spirit with a rank, legions, a description, and the ability to summon storms. None of those steps is secret and none of them is invisible. The whole process is visible from the outside, which is either reassuring or unnerving depending on what you think a demon is.

Start with what Puck is in the play

In A Midsummer Night's Dream, written around 1595 and first performed 1600, Puck is a servant of the fairy king Oberon. His name is a variant of Robin, a diminutive of Robert, and he is also called Robin Goodfellow, a name that had real currency in English rural folklore. Shakespeare was drawing on a stock of country tales about a hobgoblin and not inventing a character.

The character's function is comic disruption. Puck is given a task: put a love potion on an Athenian man's eyes. He immediately asks the cheapest possible clarifying question, which is whether the potion works on a person who is not in love. The status check, in other words, inside the first scene. He also transforms into whatever is on hand, a lion, a bear, a monkey, a fish, and he is the one who has to explain the whole plot to the audience at the end.

Shakespeare invented almost none of it

This is the part that gets skipped, and it matters for everything after. Puck's three most characteristic features — shapeshifting, malicious delight in confusion, and a taste for dirty practical jokes played on bumbling humans — are not Shakespearean inventions. They are the standard hobgoblin, and the hobgoblin is a much older and much more widespread figure than any single play.

What Shakespeare did was take a well-known hob and put him in the service of a fairy court, which he did not invent either. The fairy court in the play is a thin, aristocratic, comic invention assembled from Renaissance literary material, and the same handful of ideas fed Oberon, Titania, Peaseblossom, Cobweb, and Moth at once.

So at this point Puck is a comic servant in a comedy, derived from country folklore, and it is already true that his function is mischief that is reversible and nobody is actually harmed.

The next step is the fairy court, not the demon

The move that matters is not from Shakespeare to the grimoires. It is from a comic hob to a member of a pantheon. Once Puck belongs to a fairy court, he has colleagues, a hierarchy, a place, and rules. That is the step that made him respectable enough to be written down by people who were compiling a serious catalogue.

The construction happened in folklore as much as in literature. The fairy court as a coherent institution, meaning a monarch, named subjects, a place that is not this place, and a fixed set of fairy months, was assembled largely in the eighteenth century by antiquarians collecting fragmentary rural material and by writers who wanted the material to hang together.

This is the moment the tone changes. As long as Puck is a comic hob he is a joke. As soon as he is a courtier he is a subject, and a subject can be summoned, and once a figure can be summoned the moral character of the summoning stops being the same question.

Puck appears in the 1740 grimoire as Puck

In the Clavicula Salomonis of 1740, the Key of Solomon, there is a spirit named Puck, given a rank, a day, a place in a hierarchy, legions under him, and a set of offices. He is given the ability to summon horses, to make horses run and drive cattle, and to give good and bad answers to questions. He is a power of the sort the grimoire calls useful.

The name fit because the name was already famous. That is the whole mechanism, and it is worth stating plainly. A compiler with a gap in his hierarchy reached for the best-known hobgoblin in English folklore and attached an office to him. Puck did not acquire authority. He was assigned a job by a man with a filing problem.

This is a documented case of folklore being used as scaffolding for a system that then treated the folklore as its own source material. Within two hundred years, practitioners were citing the grimoire as evidence that Puck was a classical spirit, when the record shows the direction of the dependency runs the other way.

What the entry actually says, and what it is useful for

Read the office list as a job description rather than a biography. A spirit that makes horses run, drives cattle, and gives answers is a spirit for weather, for animals, and for questions. That is a country spirit with a practical portfolio, and the portfolio is entirely consistent with what the hobgoblin of the countryside always did.

Nothing in that list makes Puck a general-purpose demon. Practitioners who reach for him as a chaos figure, a deceiver, or a destabiliser of circumstances are importing a much later and much darker reading from a different source entirely, and the entry will not support it.

If you want to work with him at all, the description in the grimoire is the better instruction than any modern gloss: a spirit of animals, weather, and answers, with an established habit of lying. Those two together are the working brief. The rest is inherited reputation.

The three transformations that did the actual work

One: a country hob with a practical joke habit became a court subject, which gave him colleagues and a hierarchy and made him respectable. Two: the court became a real institution in the eighteenth century, assembled from fragments by collectors who wanted it to cohere, which gave him a pantheon rather than a story. Three: a grimoire compiler needed a name for a tier and reached for the famous one, which gave him a rank, legions, and a job.

None of these is a corruption of a genuine older tradition. The folklore is old, the pantheon is eighteenth-century construction, the grimoire entry is a catalogue job, and the modern demon is a fourth layer that borrowed the name and brought a new temperament with it.

The case where the borrowing is a good sign

This is not purely cautionary. A famous name filling a structural gap is a form of inheritance, and it is how a lot of usable material gets into circulation. Puck ended up in a grimoire because someone thought he was the right tool for a set of jobs, and that judgement has held up over two and a half centuries of use, which is a longer track record than most invented names in the field can claim.

The better way to read a grimoire entry is as a claim about usefulness, made by somebody who had heard of the figure and tested it against a set of jobs. The entry says this spirit does these things. It does not say this spirit has always done these things, and the difference between a tool catalog and a genealogy is exactly that gap.

Where a practitioner wants a figure with real depth, they should look for one whose evidence runs the other way, backward from the early source rather than forward from the catalogue.

Why folklore is not documentation of the supernatural

The most interesting thing about Puck's chain is that it is completely traceable without any supernatural claim being made. A rural hob exists because a story needs a mechanism for small, reversible, anonymous harm. A story about a hob will be retold and the hob will acquire tricks. A compiler will hear of the famous one. That is the whole sequence, and it needs nothing beyond human behaviour and the fact that stories travel.

This is not a debunking. It is a different explanation with a much better evidence base than the alternative, and the reason to prefer it is not scepticism but predictive power. The account above can tell you what will be in the next grimoire, and the supernatural account cannot tell you anything in advance.

The folklore is excellent evidence for what people believed and how the beliefs organised themselves. It is weak evidence for the entities, and the difference is worth keeping in mind every time a folkloric figure turns up inside an occult system.

How to use the material without either error

Read the grimoire entry for what it is: a claimed portfolio, in a late source, with an assignment history. Read the play and the folklore for what it is: a much older and better-documented body of trickster narrative. Read the modern demon for what it is: a persona assembled by twentieth and twenty-first century occult writers who needed a trickster and had a famous name available.

What you can build with is a working brief: animals, weather, questions, and a documented habit of lying. That is a complete enough specification to work from, and it is the same way you would read a Goetia spirit, as a service specification rather than a biography.

Where you should be careful is claiming lineage. A four-hundred-year gap between a Renaissance comedy and a grimoire entry, filled by collectors rather than by practice, is a gap. Filling it with a claim of unbroken tradition is the error that most of the modern material makes and the one most worth naming.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Puck the same entity in Shakespeare and in the grimoire?

Most likely not, and there is no evidence that anyone intended them to be. The play figure is derived from a country hob through a literary tradition. The grimoire entry is a compilation that assigned a spirit to a set of offices and reached for a well-known name. The two share a name and a habit of mischief and almost nothing else that can be documented.

Was Puck in the Ars Goetia?

No. Puck does not appear in the 72 spirits of the Ars Goetia. He appears in the Clavicula Salomonis of 1740 as a separate spirit with his own rank, day, legions, and offices. People often merge the two because both are spirit catalogues, but they are different lists with different structures, and the 72 have not changed very much in structure across editions while the Key of Solomon is built on a different hierarchy entirely.

What can Puck actually do in the grimoire?

The entry gives him horses, cattle, and answers to questions, both good and bad. That is the whole portfolio. The offices that get attributed to him beyond that in modern writing — chaos, deception, destabilising circumstances — are not in the source text, and it is worth noticing that 'gives bad answers' became 'a deceiver', which is a considerable upgrade that happened somewhere in translation from one kind of document to another.

Is it disrespectful to work with a modern demon assembled from folklore?

There is no entity with feelings about it, so the question is about your own accuracy rather than anyone else's comfort. The useful discipline is to name the layer you are working with, because the layers give different results. The grimoire layer gives you a specific, dated, checkable brief. The folklore layer gives you a body of narrative that knows things about mischief and consequences. The modern layer gives you a temperament, which is the least evidenced and the most often used.

So is any of the Goetia material worth taking seriously on those grounds?

The best-grounded entries are the ones whose portfolios are consistent across sources and whose names are old enough that the folklore and the catalogue entry do not contradict each other. Those are worth reading as specifications. The ones to be most careful about are the ones whose only evidence is a single late manuscript, and the ones that have accumulated large modern personas that the source text does not support — which is the same problem Puck has, in a slightly different shape.
