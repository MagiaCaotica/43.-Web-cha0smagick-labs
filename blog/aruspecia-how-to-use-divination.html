Aruspecía: How Divination Is Actually Supposed to Work

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 6 min read

Divination has a bad reputation for the wrong reason. People judge it by whether it predicts the future, and it does not, and it was not designed to. The I Ching was a decision procedure, tarot was an instrument for structuring a situation, and both work on the present tense. When you ask what will happen next Thursday, you are using a tool outside its operating range, and then blaming the tool. What follows is an account of divination as a thinking practice, including the psychology that explains why readings feel accurate and how to use that deliberately rather than being used by it. The claim is narrow and defensible: a good system gives you a vocabulary for states you did not have words for, and the value is in the vocabulary.

The question you bring is the instrument

The single most important variable in a reading is the question, and almost no attention is paid to it. People arrive with questions about a future event, which is the one kind of question a symbolic system cannot help with, and then conclude the system failed.

Ask about structure instead. What is the real shape of this situation. What am I not seeing. What is the next move I have not considered. These are questions about the present, and a symbolic system is a machine for generating structured answers to exactly that.

The reason is mechanical. A hexagram or a card spread is a fixed vocabulary. It cannot know your week. What it can do is put a name in front of you that you would not have reached on your own, and once the name is in the room your judgement fills it in. That is the whole operation.

Why present tense beats future tense

A question about next Tuesday requires the system to do something it structurally cannot do. A question about now only requires it to describe a pattern you are already inside of, and you can check that against your own experience immediately.

Future-tense questions also have a hidden cost. They invite the reader to make a prediction, and predictions are unfalsifiable in the moment. You will find a way to reconcile whatever happens with what was said, which means the reading will feel confirmed regardless of its content.

The discipline is easy to state: no dates, no names of strangers, no lottery numbers. Situation, obstacle, direction. If you cannot phrase the question that way, you are not ready to cast, and the honest move is to sit with the situation for a day and come back.

Systems differ in speed, vocabulary and honesty

Each system encodes a different scale of situation. The I Ching is built for decisions with a binary hinge: change or hold. Sixty-four hexagrams is a systematic situation vocabulary, and its strength is speed. A coin toss and two minutes of interpretation answers a question you already had an opinion about.

Tarot is built for narrative. Seventy-eight cards give you a much larger space, and a spread that lays out a story forces you to say more sentences out loud. Its weakness is speed, because a full reading takes twenty minutes and most of the value arrives in the first five cards.

Runes and scrying are faster than both and correspondingly shallower. Being able to name the difference is what lets you pick the right instrument. Wanting a decision made in ninety seconds and wanting to understand a relationship are different requests, and using the same deck for both is why people conclude the deck is unreliable.

Barnum effect, named precisely

The Barnum effect is the tendency to accept a vague personal description as uniquely applicable. Statements about a person's tendency to feel misunderstood by friends, or to have a strong drive to be appreciated, are accepted at high rates by most people, including those who rate themselves as highly discriminating.

This is the entire basis of the psychic reading industry and it is also the basis of a well-written tarot interpretation. A skilled reader produces descriptions that are general enough to fit and specific enough to feel personal. That is a technique, not a power.

The test is simple: read the description and ask whether it would apply to your neighbour. If it would, the reading told you nothing about you. If it would not, the reader made a specific and checkable claim. Learning to run that check on your own readings is the single highest-value habit in this field.

Consistency is not the same as accuracy

The most defensible claim for any divination system is internal consistency, not external accuracy. Given a stable system and a stable situation, repeated casts should mostly agree. If your readings contradict each other every time, your method is unstable and the system is not being used correctly.

External accuracy means the reading matches an outcome you could not have influenced. That is a much harder test, and the results are not encouraging for any system. A large body of testing on psychic functioning has not produced reliable above-chance performance, and pretending otherwise discredits the parts of the practice that do work.

Hold both claims. The system gives you a consistent vocabulary for your own situation. It does not forecast Tuesday. Both of those are compatible with everything you can measure.

Cold reading moves you should recognise in yourself

The moves that make cold reading work are mostly on the reader's side. You volunteer more detail than you think. Your eyes and voice leak emotional state. You look for confirmation, and you will find it in a description you would have rejected cold.

Recognising the move in yourself is worth more than learning to catch a fraud, because it works on sincere readers too, including the ones you most respect. When a card lands and you feel a jolt, notice whether the jolt matches the position scheme you set. If it does, the reading is doing work. If it does not, the jolting card was simply the one you hoped for.

A useful discipline is to write the interpretation before discussing it. Predicting the card you will draw, on paper, for eight readings in a row. Accuracy will sit near chance. Doing this once is the fastest way to dissolve the feeling that the system knows things.

A four-week structure for learning one system

Weeks one and two: learn the raw vocabulary. For the I Ching that is the sixty-four judgments, read as descriptions rather than instructions. For tarot it is seventy-eight cards in the positions you will actually use. No spreads, no reading for other people, no interpretation practice.

Week three: cast daily with a fixed present-tense question and the same position scheme every time. Write one sentence per card, no editing. The goal is not quality, it is volume, because you are training yourself to commit to a reading before your doubt arrives.

Week four: reread the twenty-eight entries and mark the ones that felt off. That is your first real signal, and it will tell you more about your own reading habits than any interpretation course. Most people discover they consistently over-read the second card or consistently ignore the third.

Journaling the readings that were wrong

A divination journal has two columns: the question, the reading, and later, what actually happened. The second column is what separates a practice from a mood. Without it you accumulate a growing archive of readings that you remember as accurate, because the ones that were not do not stay salient.

The errors are the useful part. If the same card keeps meaning something you do not want it to mean, you have found a bias in your own interpretation and you can correct it. If a whole position scheme keeps failing, you have found a bad question format.

Tools exist for this and they earn their place by making the comparison automatic. A phone app that dates each entry, stores the position scheme, and lets you filter by card or by month turns an exercise you would abandon into a dataset you actually read. The cost of that function is a one-time payment, and the practice itself is entirely free.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is divination just self-delusion?

It is a structured self-observation technique that produces useful results under narrow conditions, and it is not a forecasting technology. If you use it to get vocabulary you cannot otherwise reach, it works. If you use it to learn about next week, you are asking it for something it was never built to deliver.

How do I know if a reading was accurate?

You usually cannot, in the moment, which is why the journal matters. Judge a system by internal consistency first: are repeated casts on the same situation broadly coherent? Only then consider outcomes, and record all of them including the ones that contradict what you hoped for.

Should I learn tarot or the I Ching first?

The I Ching, because it has fewer items and a clearer decision structure. Sixty-four hexagrams is a vocabulary you can hold, and the coin method removes every mechanical obstacle. Move to tarot once you want a larger and more narrative space, not before.

Does it matter which deck I buy?

A great deal less than the price gap suggests. A well-printed deck with a consistent system you will actually use beats an expensive deck you leave on a shelf. Rider-Waite imagery is the most widely studied, so interpretive material is easier to find for it, which is the only technical advantage that matters.

Can an app replace learning the system properly?

Paying once for a tool that makes the casting effortless does not remove the twenty years of study behind the symbol; it just moves the mechanical part out of your way. The learning still has to happen, and no amount of software substitutes for having the vocabulary in your own head when the reading arrives.
