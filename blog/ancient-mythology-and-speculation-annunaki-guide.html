Ancient Mythology and Speculation

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

Every few years the same set of ancient tablets is rediscovered by somebody who has noticed a sentence in them about a planet, and the conclusion is announced: humanity was created by beings who arrived from elsewhere, and the texts have said so all along. The texts are real, the translations are public, and the sentence is real. The conclusion does not follow from it, and understanding why is one of the more useful exercises in reading ancient material at all. This piece works through what the Sumerian and Akkadian sources actually say about the Annunaki, how they reached the popular imagination, and what a beginner can do with the corpus that is genuinely there underneath the speculation. The tablets are worth reading. The conclusion attached to them is not.

Where the names actually appear

The term comes from Sumerian and is used of a class of deity in the Enuma Elish and elsewhere. The creation account in that poem describes a quarrel among the gods followed by the fashioning of humans from the clay of the earth, so that the gods would not have to labour.

That is the whole of the textual basis for the extraordinary version. A labour-saving device in a mythic poem about divine politics is not the same kind of statement as a report of visitors from another world, and treating the two as interchangeable is the step that makes the rest of the claim possible.

How the reading became what it became

The ancient astronaut hypothesis took a detail from a translation and treated it as a technological report. The move requires ignoring the genre, since a creation poem is not a log, and it requires reading one ambiguous term as a planet rather than as a divine epithet.

It went from being a fringe reading to a mass-market one through a particular kind of cultural formation: a story that flatters the reader, offers a hidden history, and cannot be disproved by evidence because it is unfalsifiable by construction. Those three properties are worth naming, because they are common to a whole genre and understanding the genre is more useful than the specific claim.

  1. A mythic poem is a different genre from a report.
  2. A divine epithet is not a place name.
  3. The story offers a hidden history and cannot be checked.
  4. Unfalsifiability is the appeal, not the evidence.

What is genuinely there to read

A great deal. The Enuma Elish, the Sumerian King List, the Epic of Gilgamesh, Hittite and Akkadian ritual texts, the Sumerian and Akkadian hymn corpora. These are translated, published, excavated, dated and edited, and the scholarly literature on them is enormous.

The tablets are not hidden. Anyone with a library card can consult critical editions, and the interesting material is in the ritual instructions rather than in the cosmic narrative. A beginner who wants to engage with this material seriously will find more in a scholarly translation of a ritual text than in any popular account of what the gods were really doing.

Atlantis, and the same pattern

The case is nearly identical and is worth reading alongside. Atlantis appears in Plato's Timaeus and Critias as a literary device. No contemporary source mentions it, and it became a cultural invention after nineteenth century use.

Plato is not a reliable witness for a lost continent any more than the Enuma Elish is a reliable witness for interstellar travel. Both are valuable documents. Neither is a report, and both have been treated as reports for a couple of centuries, which tells you more about the appetite for lost origins than about either text.

The other corpora worth your time

The Epic of Gilgamesh, the Egyptian Book of the Dead, the Norse Eddas and the Mahabharata all contain large bodies of ritual and mythological material in translation, and all of them repay close reading. The Book of the Dead in particular is closer to a body of practical instruction than most people expect.

What none of them offers is a single concealed truth waiting to be recovered. They are traditions of practice, meaning and argument, and the argument is where the value is. A text that is internally contradictory in places is more interesting than a text that is simply true, because the contradiction is doing some work you can see.

How to read a translation without being fooled by it

Find out who translated it and when, because a translation is an interpretation with a date on it. Compare two. Notice where they diverge, because the divergences are where the argument lives. And find out what the manuscript tradition looks like, since many of these texts are known from a single late copy.

A single late copy is a normal condition of the field and not a reason for dismissal. It is a reason for calibrating your confidence. The tablets at Ebla and the Sumerian royal tombs are spectacular finds precisely because the record before them was so thin, and the same fragility applies to plenty of other material.

  1. Identify the translator and the date.
  2. Compare two translations of the same passage.
  3. Note the divergences rather than averaging them.
  4. Check how many manuscripts stand behind the text.

A month of study that produces something

Read one creation account in two translations. Read one ritual text in a scholarly edition. Read one modern historian of the period rather than a popular account. Write down every claim you encounter that you cannot source, and go and source it or drop it.

That is a month, it costs almost nothing, and it produces something the popular route does not: you end up knowing which questions in this field are open. A question you cannot answer is more interesting than a conclusion you were handed, because the first one has a next step.

Where the speculation gets its hold

Somewhere in every popular version of this story is an implied rescue: humanity is special, watched, seeded, and therefore not alone. That is a genuinely appealing structure, and it appears across a great many narratives that have nothing to do with ancient astronauts.

Naming the appeal does not dismiss the story. It just locates it, which means you can decide deliberately whether it is the history you want or the comfort you want. Those are both legitimate things to be looking for, and mixing them up is how people end up defending a claim they never chose to care about.

The material that is actually usable

If your interest is ritual rather than history, the Mesopotamian material is unusually rich and unusually practical. Incantations, offerings, the specific requirements of a house or a boundary, the correct order of a ceremony, the words said when a foundation is laid. It is technical, learnable and largely uninterested in what you believe.

That last property is the one that makes it attractive to people who cannot find that quality elsewhere. A tradition that hands you a procedure without demanding a commitment is rare, and Mesopotamian ritual is full of them. Nobody asks you to believe anything in order to build a door correctly.

Reading the tablets for their own sake

Set the astronauts aside and the material becomes what it always was. A creation poem about labour and boredom. A king list that suddenly interrupts its own chronology to record a flood and starts again afterwards, which is one of the most interesting structural features in ancient literature and has nothing extraterrestrial about it.

There is a pleasure in reading a text on its own terms that is missing entirely when you are hunting for a payload. The tablets were written to be recited, in a temple, by someone who knew what they meant. Reading them that way is slower and better.

Whether to pay for any of it

A good translation is worth paying for and is available inexpensively through libraries and second-hand bookshops, so the case for buying new is weak. A critical edition with commentary is worth buying if you intend to read it more than once, and that is the only version of this purchase that pays for itself.

A subscription library of ancient texts is worth it only if you would otherwise not read them. The rest of the market in this corner is aimed at people who want the conclusion rather than the tablets, and it is priced accordingly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where do the Annunaki actually appear?

In Sumerian and Akkadian compositions, most often the Enuma Elish, where deities fashion humans from clay so that they will not have to labour. The term is a class designation for those gods, not a term for visitors from another world. Everything built on the extraterrestrial reading has to be added on top of that line by a later interpreter.

Is the ancient astronaut reading defensible?

Not from the texts, because the argument requires treating a creation poem as a report and a divine epithet as a place name. It is internally coherent once you accept those two substitutions. It is also unfalsifiable, which is a property of the claim rather than evidence for it, and that is the part worth understanding.

How do I check a translation?

Compare two by different translators, find the year and the scholarly credentials of each, and read the passages where they disagree. Then check how many manuscripts stand behind the text, because much of this material survives in a single late copy. Where translators differ, the divergence is usually where the interesting philology is.

Is there anything genuinely hidden in this material?

In the sense of suppressed and available, very little, and the material is public. A great deal is genuinely obscure in the sense that it is difficult, technical and rarely studied in translation, and that kind of obscurity is where the real value sits. The nineteenth and twentieth century excavation record is excellent and anyone with a library can consult it, so the received story about a lost library does not hold up.

What am I paying for when I buy an ancient texts bundle?

Usually access to translations you could get from a library, plus a framing that tells you what they mean. The translations have real value, particularly a good critical edition you will read more than once. The interpretation is the part to be sceptical about, because it is where the conclusions that are not in the text get attached.
