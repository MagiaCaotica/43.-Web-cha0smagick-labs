History of the Occult

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

History written about this field is usually written badly, in one of two directions: either as a debunking or as a genealogy of sages. Neither is useful for somebody who wants to know what to read and why. This page is built as reference instead. It gives you the chain of transmission in order, names the books that actually sit at each link, and sets out the vocabulary you need in order to argue about any of it. The claim throughout is narrow and defensible: this is a European tradition built by copying, translation and printing, and knowing which parts are old and which are recent changes what you can responsibly claim. Where the evidence runs out, the page says so rather than filling the gap.

The word itself, which settles more than it looks

English took the word in the sixteenth century, from occultare, the Latin verb for hiding. That root is the same one behind the phrase occult science, which originally meant the hidden or secret arts rather than the hidden knowledge we now attach to it. The word has drifted a long way from its origin, and the drift is not neutral: a field called occult sciences sounds like a body of technique, while a field called hidden knowledge sounds like a body of facts.

That drift is worth noticing because most bad history in this area is a result of it. Practitioners who read occult science as hidden knowledge end up defending claims about facts, and the sources almost never supported claims about facts. They supported claims about procedure.

The early grimoires and what they were made of

Three books sit near the beginning of what we call grimoire literature: the Notoria, the Almadel and the Lemuria. All were compiled in Western Europe between the medieval and early-modern periods, and all draw on Arabic, Jewish and Latin sources rather than on any single tradition.

The word grimoire itself came into English as a borrowing of a word meaning grim, a black book, and it was originally a term of disparagement. A grimoire was what a cleric called a book he thought was nonsense. The genre is therefore named by its critics, which is a useful thing to hold onto when the books are described as authoritative.

  1. Ars Notoria: early, short, and mostly a set of requests rather than a system.
  2. Ars Almadel: associated with a particular grimoire compilation of the early-modern period.
  3. Lemuria: a ritual of household protection rather than a general-purpose book of operations.
  4. Their shared feature is composite authorship, which is typical of the whole tradition.

The fifteenth century, when the picture changed

the Hermetic corpus was turned into Latin in the fifteenth century, and the translators then fused it with Judaic and Christian material. That fusion is what produced the Renaissance magicians as we now understand them, and it is the single most consequential event in this history because it manufactured a lineage.

Before the translation there is no continuous European magical corpus. After it there appears to be one, running from antiquity to the present, and the appearance is an artefact of the translation. The Corpus is genuinely old. The line connecting it to modern practice was assembled, not inherited.

The 1888 order, and the part of it that lasted

Founded in 1888, the Golden Dawn dominated British occultism for the rest of that century. Most of the ceremonial practice that came afterwards was shaped by its initiatory material, so a great deal of what gets taught today carries a nineteenth-century British provenance however ancient its imagery.

That is not a criticism of the material. It is a fact about its transmission, and it matters when you are deciding which parts of a ritual are structural and which are ornamental. A structure invented in 1888 can be entirely effective. It just cannot be cited as ancient.

A table of the chain, link by link

The table below is the whole argument of this page compressed into rows. Read it left to right and the shape of the thing becomes obvious: every link is a copying event, and no link is a discovery.

  • Antiquity: Hermetic and Jewish apocalyptic material, much of it now lost, some preserved.
  • Translation, 15th century: Corpus rendered into Latin and fused with Christian material.
  • Early-modern grimoires: composite books assembled from several of those strands.
  • 1888: Golden Dawn formalises a initiatory structure that most later practice inherits.
  • Late 19th to 20th century: print, translation and revival consolidate the modern canon.
  • Now: internet distribution makes the canon look older and more uniform than it is.

Why this is a discipline rather than a betrayal

None of the above makes the material weak. A practice assembled from several traditions is not a degraded version of a pure one; it is what traditions actually do when they meet. The problem is only the claim, and the claim is usually made implicitly by treating a modern compilation as though it had been transmitted.

Knowing the shape of the chain also tells you what to expect from criticism. Someone who knows the material was assembled will not be impressed by antiquity, and someone who knows the assembly was coherent will not be impressed by mere age. Both of those positions are reasonable and you will meet both.

How to check a date claim cheaply

Most confident claims about age in this field are checkable in about ten minutes with a library catalogue and no specialist knowledge. The first printed edition of a text is a hard fact. A vague medieval attribution is not. The gap between the two is where most of the misrhetoric lives.

  1. Find the earliest printed edition in a catalogue. Note the year.
  2. Check whether the author names sources or presents the work as newly received.
  3. Distinguish a manuscript tradition from a print tradition; they are different histories.
  4. Treat any undated claim as a claim about the author, not about the material.

A reading order that holds together

The order below is deliberately short, because the most common way to fail at this is to read the field chronologically, which means reading five hundred years of commentary before reading anything operational. Each item below is chosen for a specific job.

  1. One honest general history, read front to back without skipping.
  2. One early grimoire in translation, for the texture of the actual material.
  3. One nineteenth or twentieth century ceremonial manual, for the structure most practice still uses.
  4. One short modern method text, for something you can act on this month.

The terms you will meet and what they actually distinguish

Most arguments in this field are terminological disputes conducted without either side noticing. The glossary below covers the terms that get confused most often, and each entry is short on purpose.

  • Grimoire: a practical book, named by its critics rather than its authors.
  • Ceremonial magic: the structured, initiatory branch that Golden Dawn consolidated.
  • Chaos magic: a late twentieth-century method-first reaction against that structure.
  • Occult science: the older sense, meaning the hidden arts rather than hidden facts.
  • Western tradition: a convenience label for a body of material with many origins.

What the printing press changed, in one paragraph

Before print, a practice survived if a manuscript was copied by somebody who cared enough to copy it. After print, a practice survived if it sold, and those are very different criteria. The nineteenth century is when the field's centre of gravity shifts from lineage to market, and almost everything that looks canonical about it follows from that shift rather than from any judgement about quality.

What to do when a source disagrees with another

Record the disagreement. Do not resolve it, because resolving it requires an authority you do not have, and the appearance of a resolution is usually a modern editor's preference being presented as tradition.

Where two sources disagree about a practice, try the version that is more specific. A text that says what to do on a particular day is more likely to transmit something real than a text that says the same thing generally.

A table of the texts, and what each is actually for

The most useful question about any book in this history is not whether it is true but what job it was doing. Most of them have a real function even when the claims inside them are unreliable, and knowing the function stops you asking a text to do work it was never built for.

  • Early grimoires: procedure in a composite voice. Read for texture, not for accuracy.
  • Nineteenth-century ceremonial manuals: structure that most later practice inherits.
  • General histories: orientation. One is enough and the second usually repeats it.
  • Modern method texts: something to do this month. Read once and then use.
  • Commentary: the most repetitive category and the least checked. Sample rather than finish.

Why the twentieth century produced the canon

The material most people recognise as the tradition was consolidated between the 1880s and the 1940s, and the consolidation was driven by revival, translation and print rather than by anything in the material itself. That period is short and it is responsible for most of the shape of what comes after it.

The practical consequence is a strange asymmetry. A practitioner in 1910 and a practitioner in 2026 may have used the same text for entirely different reasons, and the one working from it now is working from a twentieth-century interpretation of a medieval composite, which is three removes from any source. Not one of those removes is a fraud. All of them are gaps.

  1. Ask what a text was for when it was written, before asking what it is for now.
  2. Notice when a modern edition adds structure the original did not have.
  3. Prefer the oldest available text when two editions disagree about what was said.
  4. Treat any edition with a modern introduction as containing two separate arguments.

The three questions worth asking about any claim

Almost every confident claim in this field can be sorted with three questions, and sorting takes a minute. They are not a substitute for checking, but they will tell you whether checking is worth your time.

  1. What is the earliest source for this, and can I see it?
  2. Who assembled this, and what were they trying to do?
  3. Does the claim make a prediction that could fail, or only a description that cannot?

Frequently Asked Questions

Which grimoires matter most?

For the historical chain, the Lemuria, the Ars Notoria and the Ars Almadel, because they sit early and their composite authorship is visible. For the corpus most people actually work from, the Lesser Key of Solomon and its Ars Goetia section. For practice, two short modern texts are worth more than any of the old ones. Read one of each rather than all of one kind.

Is occult history mostly fraud?

Partly, and the proportion is higher than practitioners like. There is genuine scholarship in the field, and the test is whether a writer names their sources and dates them. Work that cites unnamed manuscripts as though they settled something, or that presents a nineteenth-century manual as ancient, is not scholarship in any useful sense. Read work that interrogates its own sources and you will not go far wrong.

What is a grimoire, given that the name is disparaging?

A practical book of instruction, and that is all the term ever meant. It came into English as a borrowing of a word for a grim or black book, and it was used by clerics to describe books they thought were nonsense. The genre is named by its critics. That is worth holding onto when such books are described as authoritative rather than as documents.

If the tradition is a modern invention, why does any of it work?

Because assembly and invention are not the same as fraud. A practice assembled from several traditions out of several sources can be entirely coherent and entirely effective, and it cannot also be ancient. The honest position is that the material is recent in its current form and that its structure is older than its most influential versions. Neither fact reduces what it can do.

Is a paid shelf of history books worth the money?

Only where it gives you something a library cannot, which in practice means a searchable primary-text corpus you can query. A pile of restatements of material that is already free is not worth paying for at any price. If you are starting, the short method texts are cheap or free and the history is available in print, so a paid library is a convenience rather than a prerequisite.
