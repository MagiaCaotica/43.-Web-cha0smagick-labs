How to Spot a Magic Scam Before You Pay

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

Esoteric communities are unusually easy to defraud, and the reason is structural rather than moral. The knowledge claims are hard to verify, the vocabulary is dense enough to hide circular reasoning, and the social pressure runs toward deference. That combination lowers the price a competent con artist has to pay. You do not need to develop supernatural perception to protect yourself. You need four questions, asked early, in writing, before any money moves. The pattern below is drawn from the ordinary research literature on high-control groups rather than from occult gossip, because the mechanism is identical whether the group is religious, financial or esoteric. What follows is a test you can apply to any teacher, any course and any community, including ones that are entirely sincere. Sincere people run con-shaped structures by accident all the time, and the structure is still worth identifying.

What a con looks like from the inside

Members rarely describe their experience as being trapped. The accounts that surface years later follow a recognisable shape: a period of genuine relief, an escalation in commitment, a slow narrowing of acceptable opinion, and a final stage where leaving feels like betraying something sacred rather than admitting a mistake.

The scholarly literature on charismatic groups attributes this to identity consolidation. Early on, the group supplies answers, community and a sense of direction. Those are real goods. Once the person's identity is partly organised around the group, criticism of the group becomes criticism of the self, which is a much more expensive thing to process.

Cognitive dissonance reduction is the standard mechanism cited for why commitment deepens after doubts appear rather than fading. Holding a claim you privately doubt is uncomfortable, so the cheaper repair is to reinterpret the doubt as evidence of your own weakness. A group that has taught this move to its members has taught them to argue themselves into anything.

The diagnosis that only the practitioner can resolve

One structure shows up often enough to name on its own: a condition that is diagnosed as severe, whose cure is available only from the healer, and whose treatment requires continued payment. In fraudulent healing this is textbook. In esoteric settings the same shape appears as a permanent block, a curse, a heavy karmic load or a suppressed gift.

The tell is the cure. If the diagnosis can only be resolved by the person who made it, and resolution is priced in sessions rather than demonstrated in method, you have a closed loop. The customer cannot verify, cannot compare, and cannot leave without losing the treatment. Every element of that is engineered.

A legitimate tradition will tell you what it does not treat. That boundary statement is nearly absent from con-shaped offers and is the single most useful question you can ask. Ask what the practice does not do, what it cannot promise, and what the conditions are under which you should stop.

Why esoteric fields are cheaper to con

The epistemic standards of Western esotericism are already loose. A claim like an astrological transit affecting your day is presented by serious practitioners in the same register as a claim about file permissions, which means the ordinary social cues that help people detect fraud are muted from the start.

None of that makes the field fraudulent. A tradition that studies symbolic systems without a settled mechanism is doing something legitimate, and demanding empirical proof of a symbolic practice is a category error. The problem is narrower: when standards are loose, a liar gains ground cheaply, because the honest practitioner is not claiming more than they can support and therefore has no visible defence.

The practical consequence is that you have to do the verification yourself. Nobody in the transaction has an incentive to do it for you, and the person selling is the least reliable source of information about whether you need what they are selling.

Four questions that resolve most cases

Ask whether the basic information is free. A teacher who will not explain the fundamentals without payment is selling gatekeeping rather than teaching, and gatekeeping has no teaching content. Every serious tradition gives away its entry material. If the entry is behind a price, the price is the product.

Ask what happens if you leave. A healthy group has an answer that does not involve consequences. A high-control group has no answer at all, which is itself the answer, because a structure that cannot tolerate departure is built around retention rather than results.

Ask for a falsifiable claim with a deadline. Say: I will test this by a specific date and record every attempt, including the failures. A sincere practitioner engages with that and often improves their own claims as a result. A con-shaped offer has to either refuse or pivot to a demand for belief.

Ask what the refund terms are, in writing, before you pay. Then actually read them. The refund policy is where the real business model is disclosed, and it is the last document a person in a hurry reads.

Courses, subscriptions and the pressure to ascend

Buying a course is not a scam. Teaching requires time, and charging for structured material is normal. What makes it a scam is the sales framing, and the framing is where you should read carefully.

Three framings are reliable warnings. First, fear of a curse as the reason to enrol now. Second, rank escalation, where the material is organised so that the next tier is always the fix for the dissatisfaction with the current one. Third, an arrangement in which you cannot easily leave, such as a subscription tied to a community identity, or an annual commitment presented as a founding member status.

Escalation ladders work because each rung resolves the previous complaint. That is a legitimate design for teaching, and a manipulative one when the complaint is manufactured. Test by buying a single level, doing the work, and seeing whether the result arrives without an upgrade. If the result requires the next tier, the tier was the point of the sale.

What legitimate teaching does and does not charge for

Teachers charge for time. That is the honest version of the transaction: an hour of structured attention has a cost, and someone who has spent twenty years developing material is not obliged to give it away. A rate card is honest. A rate card plus a narrative about your spiritual poverty is not.

The line I use is simple. If the cost is proportional to something real you receive, it is a purchase. If the cost is proportional to your insecurity, it is a donation you are being punished for not making larger.

Proportionate costs are also easy to compare. A flat fee for a fixed body of material is comparable, reviewable and bounded. A sliding scale tied to how much you can afford is a signal about the seller's relationship to money more than yours. Neither is illegal. One of them is a business.

Leaving: the frame comes off first

People who eventually leave high-control groups frequently describe the same sequence, which is the reverse of how it feels at the time. The material is dealt with first, then the relationships, then the finances, then the legal dimension if there was coercion.

The order matters because the interpretive frame is the mechanism. As long as you still hold the cosmology that explains your membership, leaving looks like abandoning something sacred and your anxiety will keep pulling you back. Disengaging from the claims, not from the people, is what makes departure survivable.

Coercion changes the register entirely. If there were threats, withheld money, or pressure against family, that is a legal matter and not a spiritual one, and it is worth getting outside help before you act. A support line or a solicitor will know more about your specific situation than any teacher you left, including one who claims to be helping you.

Keeping a practice that survives scrutiny

None of this requires you to give up the work. The entire tradition rests on self-directed experiment, and the discipline that makes experiment meaningful is exactly the discipline that detects a bad teacher. Pre-registering a prediction and a deadline is not scepticism about the practice. It is the only way to find out what the practice does.

Practise alone as the default and take guidance as a supplement. A teacher who can be checked, whose claims can be timed and whose results you can attribute partly to your own work is an asset. A teacher who needs to be the sole source is a dependency.

Keep a written record of what you attempted, what happened and what you did next. That record is what makes you immune to the specific manipulation this article is about, because a person with a year of dates in front of them cannot be moved by an anecdote. It also happens to be the only thing standing between you and the feeling that the whole thing was worthless, which is the emotion most often used to reopen the door.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I tell whether a teacher is legitimate?

Legitimate teachers do not require payment for basic information, do not ask you to cut contact with people outside, do not make claims that cannot be tested by any means including yours, and do not require you to feel broken in order to sell you a cure. Each of those is a behavioural test rather than a doctrinal one, which means you can run it without knowing anything about magic.

Is buying an occult course a scam?

Not by itself. A course is a scam when the sales framing depends on fear of a curse, on rank escalation that never resolves the complaint, or on a structure in which leaving is costly. A flat-priced course with real material, a real refund policy and no emotional leverage is an ordinary purchase from an ordinary seller.

What do I do if I think I have already been scammed?

Disengage from the interpretive material first, because the frame is what keeps the structure in place. Then deal with the financial side, and if there was coercion, with the legal side, using outside help. Do not go back to the group to ask whether it was all real, since that conversation is usually designed to end with you staying.

Can a sincere teacher still run a harmful structure?

Yes, and it is common, because the incentive to retain members is built into the model rather than into the character. Sincere people repeat what they were shown. A structure that only the leader can diagnose and only payment can resolve is harmful whether or not the leader knows it, and asking about it directly is a fair test of their self-awareness.

Does buying an app or a course from a sincere teacher put me at risk?

A flat cost you can see the end of is a different proposition from a standing arrangement that needs you to stay unhappy. One is a purchase of a fixed thing and you own the output. The other is a subscription whose value depends on your continued uncertainty, and that is the structure worth refusing. Buy the artefact, keep the practice, leave the dependency.
