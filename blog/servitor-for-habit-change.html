A Servitor for Habit Change: Repetitions, Not Resolutions

By Frater Alek0s • 7 November 2026 • 7 min read

A habit is a count of repetitions in a stable context. A resolution is a sentence with no count.

Behaviour, not identity

"Become a disciplined person" is an identity claim with no observable. "Walk for twenty minutes after lunch, five days a week" is a behaviour with a count.

A servitor can hold the second. The first will be fed by self-judgement, which is the opposite of a stable cue.

Write the behaviour in the smallest version you would still respect. A smaller version kept is worth more than a large version abandoned.

Counting repetitions

The task is a number of repetitions in a window: twenty walks in thirty days, thirty pages in two weeks, four practice sessions a week.

Choose the counting unit that is easiest to verify. A walk is a walk; "being healthy" is not.

Write the failure condition too. Twenty walks with twelve done is a partial success, and the log should say so without drama.

The environment clause

The operator's clause is environmental. Shoes by the door, book on the pillow, instrument out of the case. The cue does more work than the intention.

Decide what removes friction and write it next to the feeding rule. A servitor fed in a room set up for the behaviour is a different construction from one fed in a cluttered room.

Do not rely on motivation. It is a mood, and moods are not a schedule.

Feeding as a cue

Feed immediately before the behaviour, in the same place, with the same words. The feeding becomes the starting ritual.

Keep it short: mark, name, count, then start the behaviour. If the feeding takes longer than the behaviour, the ritual has become the point.

Do not add offerings that cost money or time. A habit servitor that requires a purchase is a habit you will drop when money is tight.

Streaks and rates

A streak is fragile: one miss and the number resets. A rate is robust: twelve of twenty is a rate.

Prefer the rate unless the streak itself is the skill. Even then, log the misses; they tell you which context is unstable.

At the end date, compare the rate to the contract. Then change one variable: the cue, the size or the window.

Safety and limits

This is behaviour design, not medicine. If the habit involves alcohol, drugs, self-harm, eating or sleep, work with a professional. A servitor is not a treatment.

Do not use the practice to override exhaustion or pain. A body sending signals is not a lack of discipline.

If the practice increases shame or compulsive checking, close it. A habit tool that produces guilt is a bad tool.

Checklist

One behaviour, one count, one window.

Write the cue and remove one source of friction.

Feed immediately before the behaviour.

Track the rate, not only the streak.

Stop if the practice produces shame or compulsion.

Missing days without a reset

The all-or-nothing rule is the most common reason a habit experiment fails. A missed day is a data point about the cue, not a verdict about the operator.

Decide in advance what happens after a miss: the next scheduled repetition happens as planned, with no extra session to compensate. Compensation turns a rate into a debt and makes the next miss more likely.

Changing the cue

If the misses cluster on the same days, the cue is wrong. Move the behaviour to a different anchor: after a different meal, before a different transition, in a different room.

Change one variable at a time and note the date in the log. Two changes at once tell you nothing about which one worked.

The social environment

Habits are easier to keep when the people around you expect them. Tell one person what you are doing and when; that is a second cue and a small accountability.

Do not turn the practice into a performance. One person is enough. A public streak adds an audience to the contract and makes a miss feel like a failure instead of data.

Quick rules

  • Tell one person the schedule.
  • Do not publish the streak.
  • Keep the cue visible.
  • Log the misses.
  • Change one variable at a time.

After a relapse

A relapse is not the end of the contract. It is the event that tells you which context is strongest. Write the trigger, the time and what happened before it.

Resume at the next scheduled repetition, not the next day and not after a punishment. Compensation sessions turn the rate into a debt and make the relapse more likely to repeat.

The two-week check

At day fourteen, look at the log before deciding anything. If the rate is above half, keep the contract unchanged. If it is below half, change the cue, not the count. The two-week check exists so the end date is not the first time you look at the data.

FAQ

Can a servitor break an addiction?

No. Addiction needs professional care. A servitor can support a plan designed with a clinician, not replace it.

How long should the experiment last?

Thirty days is a useful window: long enough to see a rate, short enough to revise.

Should I build one servitor per habit?

Yes. Two habits in one contract make the count ambiguous and the end unclear.

What if I miss three days?

Log it and continue. If the misses cluster, change the cue rather than adding willpower.

How many misses make the experiment a failure?

Write the threshold before you start. Twelve of twenty is a partial success; four of twenty is a failed contract.

Can the servitor itself be the cue?

Yes, if the feeding is short and always happens in the same place. A long feeding becomes the behaviour and the habit disappears.

Should I restart the count after a relapse?

No. Keep the same window and count the repetitions. A restart hides the data you need.

What if the habit depends on other people?

Then the count includes the coordination, not only the behaviour. Write the smallest version you can do alone, and treat the social part as a separate contract.
