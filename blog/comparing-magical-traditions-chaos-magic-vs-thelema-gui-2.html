Comparing Magical Traditions

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Choosing between traditions is usually framed as a matter of which one is true, and the framing makes the decision impossible. A workable comparison has to set that question aside and look at something more concrete: what each tradition asks of you day to day, what it costs in time and money, what it forbids, and what it leaves you free to do. This page walks through that comparison in a fixed order, so the same criteria get applied to each system rather than whichever one you happen to admire most. It is aimed at people who have looked at two or three traditions, liked more than one, and want a defensible reason to pick one for a period rather than forever. Nothing here requires you to leave anything, and the output is meant to be a season of work rather than a permanent allegiance.

Step one, drop the question about which one is true

Truth claims are the least useful thing you can compare, because you have no procedure for adjudicating them and any procedure you invent will confirm whatever you started with. If two traditions disagree about whether a given ritual does something, there is no shared instrument that can settle it, and the argument will be settled by social power rather than evidence. That is a description of how such arguments go, not an argument against either system.

Set the claim aside for the length of the comparison. You are not being asked to stop holding a metaphysical position. You are being asked to make a practical decision without pretending the practical decision is also a philosophical one, which is a mistake that damages both activities. People who keep the two separate find they can move between traditions without losing either their commitments or their critical faculties.

Step two, write down the daily demand of each one

A tradition that asks for an hour a day and one that asks for an hour a year are not the same commitment, and the difference is invisible until you write it down. Take a realistic week, subtract sleep, work, transport and family time, and see what is actually left. Most people are surprised by how little is left, and the amount that remains is the ceiling on whatever they choose.

Then look at what the remainder has to cover. This step is where most enthusiastic comparisons fail, because the enthusiasm is spent on reading about systems rather than measuring them. If a tradition needs a fixed weekly slot and you have three free hours total, that is a fact about your life and it outranks any claim about the tradition's depth or lineage.

  1. List the recurring commitments each system actually requires, not the ones its advocates admire
  2. Total them in hours per month, including study time and any expected gathering
  3. Subtract from the realistic free time you actually have, not the time you wish you had
  4. Flag anything that already exceeds the ceiling before you start, and treat it as a hard constraint

Step three, list what each one forbids

Prohibitions are the clearest expression of a tradition's actual priorities, and they are far more informative than its stated aims. A system that forbids something is telling you what it considers load-bearing. Read the prohibitions as design decisions rather than as obstacles, and the character of each system becomes much easier to see.

Then check them against your actual life rather than your imagined one. Some prohibitions concern diet, others concern sex, others concern money or the use of certain substances, and a few concern association with particular people. Most of them are not enforced and most people break some of them within a year. What matters is which ones you would find genuinely difficult, because those are the ones that will generate resentment, and resentful compliance is worse than honest non-participation.

Step four, notice the shape of the entry route

The most consequential structural difference between the systems people compare is how access works. One is initiatory: there is a sequence, there are gates, and there is a body of material you are not shown until you have done something to earn it. The other is largely self-directed: the books are printed, the methods are described, and nothing obliges you to prove anything to anybody before you begin.

Neither is better. They solve different problems. Initiatory structures control the distortion of methods, at the cost of making access depend on other people's availability and judgement. Self-directed structures are free and immediate, at the cost of providing no correction mechanism at all. Knowing which one you are optimising for is more useful than knowing which one is older, because age tells you nothing about the fit.

Step five, price the whole thing properly

Cost is not only the subscription or the initiatory fee. It is the travel, the materials that a given system treats as required, the time spent on its internal politics, and the opportunity cost of the alternative things you would otherwise be doing with the same hours. Some systems look free and are expensive, and some look expensive and are cheap, because the expensive-seeming one asks for less of your week.

Include what you would have to give up. A tradition that requires weekly travel to a distant lodge is making a real claim on a real resource, and the claim is usually not obvious because it is spread across dozens of small expenses. Write the total down monthly. Then check the same figure for the system you are comparing against, using the same method, and only then draw a conclusion.

Step six, test the correction mechanism

Ask a concrete question: if I am wrong, who tells me, and how quickly? A tradition with an active teacher, working groups and a method of disagreement has a real answer. A tradition practised alone with a private journal and no external input has, structurally, no answer at all, and the practitioner is the only check on their own drift.

This is the criterion most often skipped and it should not be. Many people select on the basis of what a system promises, and few on whether it can correct them when they are wrong. Over a few years, the correction mechanism determines more about the outcome than the content of the teachings, because content is inert without somebody noticing that you have misunderstood it.

Step seven, commit for a fixed season rather than forever

Permanent allegiance to a system is a decision almost nobody makes consciously and almost everybody makes by inertia. Choose instead a defined period, write the date down, and arrange to review it. A season of work is long enough to learn something real and short enough that changing your mind costs nothing, which removes the main reason people stay in systems they have quietly stopped believing in.

There is a practical benefit beyond flexibility. Fixed periods force you to notice what you learned, because there is an ending at which you have to account for the time. Open-ended commitment produces a growing pile of half-finished study and a vague sense that something is wrong, which is the most common reason people abandon practice entirely rather than changing systems.

Where the two genuinely overlap

The overlap is larger than the rhetoric suggests. Both of the systems most often compared use symbolic language, a initiatory or graded structure in some form, ritual as a container for attention, and a body of practice aimed at changing behaviour rather than at acquiring information. A practitioner who has spent time in either has most of the raw material already, and the apparent gulf between them is considerably smaller than the internet suggests.

The overlap also creates a real risk of a particular kind of confusion: taking the vocabulary of one and the practice of another and producing a hybrid that is coherent to nobody including its creator. Hybrid practice is not illegitimate, and plenty of it is effective. It is worth being aware that it gives you access to none of the corrective apparatus of either parent, so the log and the outside reader matter more, not less.

The most-quoted line, and what it is actually for

The phrase that gets repeated most in this area is the short injunction about doing what you will. It is almost always quoted as a permission slip, and it is almost never quoted as what it is, which is a demand for responsibility at the point of action. The instruction presupposes that you already know what your will is, and that the honest difficulty lies not in discovering a new permission but in declining the comfortable ones you have been granted by others.

Practitioners who read it that way tend to become noticeably more careful, because the standard it implies is applied to their own conduct first. Read as licence, it makes a person less responsible, since every consequence can be described as chosen. Read as an assignment, it makes them more so, because choosing well is now visibly the work. The systems in this comparison differ less in their prohibitions than in how hard they make that work.

A scoring sheet you can actually fill in

Assign each system a number on the six criteria above, using the notes you wrote rather than your impression. Resist weighting the criteria by affection. The value of the exercise is not the total, it is which single criterion produced the largest gap, because that is the one that will determine whether the choice holds up once the novelty has worn off.

If the totals are close, the decision is genuinely close and you should choose on the criterion that matters most to you rather than on the total. If one system wins decisively on every line, the more useful question becomes why you were considering the other one, because that answer usually contains the actual problem.

  • Realistic weekly hours available after everything else has been subtracted
  • Prohibitions you would find genuinely difficult rather than merely inconvenient
  • How access is granted, and whether that route will still exist in two years
  • Monthly cost including travel, materials and opportunity cost
  • Whether anyone will tell you when you are wrong, and how fast
  • Whether a fixed review date can be written down without it feeling artificial

What most people actually borrow from each other

In practice, borrowing is nearly universal and the borrowing is usually respectful. People take a daily structure from one system, a vocabulary from another and a seasonal framework from a third, and most of them do so without formally joining anything. This is the normal shape of contemporary practice and it is not a failure of commitment, though it is often treated as one by the most committed members of each tradition.

The only thing to be careful about is provenance. If you use a term, know where it came from and use it consistently, because inconsistent vocabulary is how practitioners end up convinced they have had an experience that their own system would have explained differently. Writing down what each borrowed element is doing in your practice takes five minutes and prevents a whole category of later confusion.

The question to ask before you commit

There is one question that resolves most of this faster than the scoring sheet: what do you want to be different in a year, and which system makes that outcome more likely for a person with your actual constraints? The answer is sometimes neither of the systems you have been comparing, and finding that out early saves a great deal of reading.

If the honest answer is that you want structured correction and accountability, look for a system that provides them even if its theology is not what you were hoping for. If the answer is that you want to understand a specific body of material deeply, choose the one whose sources you would still be reading in five years. Match the structure to the need, and let the metaphysics wait.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to pick one tradition?

No, and refusing to pick is a legitimate position rather than a failure of commitment. What is not sustainable is drifting between several without a written account of what you are taking from each and why, because that produces a practice nobody can correct, including you. A workable middle path is to designate one system as your primary practice and treat the others as reference material, then revisit the designation at the end of a fixed season rather than continuously.

What is the left-hand path?

It is a modern esoteric distinction rather than a historical one, and it sorts traditions roughly along the line of self-directed and public-access on one side and initiatory and initiatic on the other. It is useful as a rough map and misleading as a boundary, since many historical initiatory systems published their material openly and many modern self-directed movements reproduce initiation structures closely. Treat it as a description of social organisation rather than of doctrine, and be suspicious of anyone who uses it as a moral ranking.

Is chaos magic compatible with Wicca?

In practice, very often yes, because they ask different things of a practitioner. One supplies a flexible method for testing intentions, the other supplies a seasonal and calendrical framework with a developed symbolic vocabulary. Most people who combine them find the overlap is a small set of attitudes rather than a shared theology. The practical thing to watch is consistency: if the two systems assign different meanings to the same symbol, and you use both, you will eventually contradict yourself. Write the meanings down once and pick one.

Would joining a formal order make my practice better?

It would make your practice correctable, which is a different and more important thing. The specific benefits of structure are external feedback, someone who will notice drift you cannot notice yourself, and a method of disagreement. The costs are real: money, calendar, deference, and a set of commitments you did not choose. Whether the exchange is worth making depends entirely on whether you currently have anyone in your life who will read your log without being impressed by it.

Where does the money go in a paid membership or course?

It pays for the correction mechanism, not for privileged knowledge. Fees fund teaching, space, administration and the person whose judgement makes your practice reliable, which is why a modest fee can buy more than an expensive one. Be sceptical of high prices attached to access to secrets, because the scarcity is usually manufactured. Ask what happens if you leave, and what you would still own if you did, before you pay anything at all.
