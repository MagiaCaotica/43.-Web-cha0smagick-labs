Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Most descriptions of chaos magic are either a history lesson or a sales pitch, and neither of those is much help to somebody who wants to know what to do on a Tuesday evening. This guide takes the movement as it actually stands and walks through a complete working cycle from the first sentence to the final log entry, with the interpretive material kept to a minimum and the uncertainty stated where it exists. It assumes you have met the terminology and want a procedure you can repeat, not a defence of the philosophy behind it. The order of the steps matters more than the steps themselves, and the reason why is explained at the point where it becomes relevant rather than asserted at the start.

Where the method comes from and what it inherits

The movement dates itself to the early 1970s, and 1976 is conventionally marked as the beginning because that is when the Order of the Golden Dawn's successor organisations were wound up and the Ordo Templi Ouroborus was founded. That date is a piece of history rather than a metaphysical claim, and it is worth treating it as one. What the movement inherited was less a technique than an attitude: that inherited authority was less valuable than direct experiment.

The two texts that shaped it agree on almost nothing metaphysical and agree completely on method: a short pamphlet by Peter Carroll, Liber Null and psi, and a short book by John Crowley, Liber Kaos. Both treat the practitioner's own results as the primary evidence. The name itself echoes chaos magic and the Chaos enthroned motif in Crowley's Book of the Law, and the affiliation was taken deliberately rather than by accident.

The one idea that carries the whole method

The durable contribution of the movement is procedural rather than metaphysical. It made it acceptable to write down what you did, fix beforehand what evidence would count, and then change your mind if the evidence says you should. Almost every serious practice in every tradition does something equivalent, but few of them state it as a rule and none of them attach it to a metaphysical system, which is why practitioners in this lineage tend to be unusually careful about their own claims.

If you take one thing from this guide, take that. The tools are cheap and the sequence is short, and the whole apparatus is worth very little if the result never gets written down in a form that could embarrass you later.

Step one, deciding what you are actually testing

Before you write anything, decide what question this working answers. This sounds bureaucratic and it is the step that makes the rest of the process worth doing. A working aimed at a specific, checkable outcome in a fixed period is worth running. A working aimed at a general improvement in your life is not, because there is no state of the world that would settle it.

Write the question down in advance and attach a deadline. Not a hoped-for deadline. If you cannot say what evidence would count against the working, you are not testing anything, and the record you keep afterwards will confirm whatever you wanted to believe regardless of what happened.

Step two, the intent reduced to one sentence

Write a single imperative sentence naming a concrete outcome, with a number or a date in it if one is available. Imperative means a command, not a request. Requests presuppose a counterparty who may decline; commands describe a state of affairs to be produced. This is a linguistic choice with a practical consequence, and the practical consequence is that a command is much easier to evaluate at the deadline than a wish.

One sentence. If it needs two, it is describing two intentions, and two intentions in one working means you will not know which one to credit. If it needs a paragraph, the extra words are almost always about feelings rather than outcomes, and they are the part you can leave out.

Step three, the reduction to a glyph

Reduce the letters, drop the vowels, and combine the remaining strokes until you have something you can draw in one movement. Austin Osman Spare documented the letter-reduction method in 1904 in a work called A Book of Gates, and the technique has been in continuous use since. Whether the glyph carries meaning independently of your knowledge of what it encodes is a separate question that this method does not need to answer in order to work.

Draw it once. A drawing made in a single continuous gesture carries the state you were in when you made it, which is part of what the charge is for. A drawing assembled carefully from parts is a diagram, and diagrams are for planning rather than working.

Step four, choosing the moment and the instrument

Choose a moment when you are not interrupted and a physical object to carry the glyph: a piece of paper, a card, the back of your hand. Instrument matters less than most material suggests, and it matters considerably less than the tradition implies. What matters is that the object is yours, that it stays out of sight afterwards, and that putting it away is a deliberate act rather than an afterthought.

Time of day matters for practical reasons rather than astrological ones. Nobody has demonstrated that a particular hour improves outcomes, and the people who claim the most about timing are usually the people who accomplished the least. A quiet evening with no obligations afterwards will outperform an astronomically ideal morning followed by a difficult day.

Step five, the charge, and why it is short

The charge is a focused act of attention on the glyph, with the intent held in mind. Thirty seconds is enough. Two minutes is plenty. The tradition's own instructions favour brevity, and the reason is not mystical: a long charge invites the mind to start narrating, and narration turns a single act of attention into a rehearsal of the whole story you have attached to it.

There is a common confusion here worth naming. Repeating a charge over several days is not the same as a longer charge. A long charge is one continuous act. Repetition is a different operation entirely, and it is the operation most likely to produce fixation rather than effect.

Step six, dismissal and putting the object away

End the working deliberately. Blow out a candle, close a notebook, or say the word that means you are finished, and then put the object somewhere you will not see it. This last part is the one that gets skipped, and skipping it is the most common route to a working that becomes a worry.

The reason is behavioural and not supernatural. An object you look at repeatedly is a reminder, and a reminder is a prompt for a train of thought, and a train of thought about your intention is functionally indistinguishable from a repetition of the charge. Putting the object away ends the loop.

Step seven, the log entry, written the same day

Write the entry while it is still slightly uncomfortable, because the discomfort is a signal. Record the exact sentence, the reduction method, the date and time, how long the charge ran, and what you were doing in the twenty minutes before you started. That last item catches more than any other, because the state you arrive from is half of what the working consists of.

Keep it to a paragraph. A log you will not keep is not a log. What you are building is a series of comparable entries, and comparable entries need to be comparable in length, which means the habit has to survive contact with a week when you would rather not do it.

Step eight, the deadline and the pre-registered rule

Set the review date when you write the entry, not later, and write down in advance what result would count and what result would not. A deadline without a decision rule produces a comfortable fiction, because by the time you arrive you will be able to construct a story either way. The decision rule is the part that makes the deadline worth setting.

Arrive on the date, read the entry, and update. If the outcome matches, record what was different about the run. If it does not, record that too, without editing the earlier entry to make the miss interpretable. A log you have revised after the fact is a diary, and a diary is a document nobody can use.

  1. The sentence, written in the imperative with a number or a date
  2. The reduction, drawn once in a single gesture
  3. The charge, thirty seconds to two minutes, once
  4. The dismissal, followed by putting the object out of sight
  5. The log entry, written the same day in under a paragraph
  6. The deadline and decision rule, fixed before the working started

The part of the sequence that most often collapses

Four places, in order of frequency. The intent is written too vaguely, which is the most common and the least visible, because a vague intention produces no clear moment of disappointment. The charge gets repeated across days. The object stays visible. The decision rule is never written down, so the deadline arrives and becomes an opportunity for reinterpretation rather than a test.

None of these are spiritual problems. They are ordinary procedural failures that would undermine any project tracking, and the reason they persist in this particular field is that nothing external enforces them. There is no one checking your log. The structure only exists if you build it and then keep it.

What differs when you do it again

One variable, and preferably the boring one. Change the day of the week, or the time of day, or the physical object, or the sentence wording by a word. Do not change everything, because a run that differs in five dimensions teaches you nothing, and changing the sentence is the change that muddies the result rather than clarifying it.

If a first run produced nothing at all, the most likely explanations are an intent that was not checkable and a decision rule that was not fixed. Both are visible in the log you already have, so read it before writing a second one. A second run that repeats the same ambiguity will produce the same uninformative outcome, and it will take twice as long to get there.

When to stop, and what to write when you do

Stop when the practice starts costing you something you did not agree to spend, or when you notice you are running workings to obtain reassurance rather than to test something. Both are the same problem wearing different clothes. The record is supposed to reduce uncertainty about your own practice, and a working run for reassurance increases it every time.

Write the final entry honestly, including the parts that look like failure. A log with a run of nulls in it is more useful to you than a log of successes, because it is the only one that can correct you. If you end the practice, leave the record where you will find it in a year. That is when it will be worth having.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where does the 1976 date actually come from?

It comes from the founding of the Ordo Templi Ouroborus, which happened when several organisations associated with the Golden Dawn were dissolved and their remaining members formed a new body. Nobody has demonstrated that the movement began on that day or that the date carries any significance beyond being a convenient administrative marker. Treat it as a landmark in the record, not a cause.

Must I accept anything supernatural to do this?

No. The method is a set of procedures for stating an intent, acting on it, and recording what happened. A sceptic can run every step and keep every result, and a sincere practitioner can run the identical steps. The lineage is unusually accommodating on this point because the founders were explicit that experiment came first and doctrine second, which leaves the beliefs optional.

What is the single most common mistake?

Writing an intent that could not fail. Aiming to be happier, or more successful, or less stuck leaves the deadline without anything to test, and a deadline without a test is just a date to feel bad on. The second most common is repeating the charge over several days, which turns a single act of attention into a worry. Both are visible in the log afterwards, which is the argument for writing it.

Would a paid tool outperform a notebook page?

The tracking does. Software that stores entries, sorts them by date and makes the pre-registered decision rule easy to check at the review is genuinely worth having, because the bookkeeping is the part people abandon. Software that generates the glyph is saving you four minutes and introducing a screen into a moment that used to be private. Judge the tool on the log, not on the drawing.

How long should I wait before judging a working?

Long enough for the deadline you set, and no longer. The thirty-to-ninety day window that gets quoted for money work is arbitrary, and the arbitrariness is the point: any fixed interval works as long as it is fixed in advance. What fails is the endless deferral in which a working is never judged because judging it might mean declaring it unsuccessful.
