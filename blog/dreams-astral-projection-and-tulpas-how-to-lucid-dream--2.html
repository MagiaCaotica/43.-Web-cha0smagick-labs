Dreams, Astral Projection and Tulpas

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 12 min read

Dreams, out of body experiences and tulpas get bundled together in most introductory material, and bundling them hides the fact that they have wildly different evidence bases and wildly different reliabilities. Lucid dreaming is well documented, has a large measurable population, and has induction techniques that work often enough to be worth learning. Out of body experiences are reported by a small minority and usually arrive uninvited. Remote viewing is genuinely disputed. Tulpas come from a specific Tibetan Buddhist practice with a specific purpose, and reading them as a technique for creating a separate being inverts what the source tradition does. Treating these four as one subject is the reason so much advice about them is unreliable. This is a step-by-step sequence for the one that is most tractable, with the other three placed honestly around it, including what the evidence does and does not support. It is written for someone who wants to start tonight with something that has a realistic chance of working.

Which of the four is actually reproducible

Lucid dreaming, by a wide margin. It has a large measured base of people who do it, it can be induced by techniques with a decent success rate, and the phenomenon is well characterised in the sleep literature. Out of body experiences sit in a different category: a small percentage report them, they cluster near sleep onset, and they often accompany illness, meditation or sleep disruption rather than occurring at will. That pattern is important. If you are trying to force one, the forcing is part of what is happening.

Remote viewing is the one people most often want to believe and least often test. The prospective results from the research programmes, including Stargate, have not replicated reliably. What the training does appear to improve is the ability to describe visual material precisely, which is a real and transferable skill that has nothing to do with seeing at distance. Tulpas are a category error in most popular writing. The Tibetan practice is a visualisation and deity yoga method, not a technology for manufacturing a companion.

Why the morning decides everything

REM periods lengthen as the night goes on, so the final third of a normal sleep contains the longest REM periods and the highest odds of a lucid episode. Waking in that window is what turns an occasional spontaneous lucid dream into a reliable practice, and it is the single change that produces the largest return for the smallest effort. The practical consequence is that the technique is less mystical than it looks: you are managing a wake-up time, not a mental state.

This also explains why people who report never having a lucid dream usually describe good dream recall. Recall and lucidity are different capacities, and the one you can practise most cheaply is recall. Write your dreams down the moment you are awake enough to hold a pen. Do it for two weeks before you change anything else, because you need a baseline and because the writing itself changes what you remember three nights later.

Step one, set the conditions before you try

Fix a consistent wake time rather than a consistent bedtime. Bedtime varies with your day; wake time can be held to fifteen minutes across a week and that is enough to stabilise the cycle. Then set an alarm for the last third of your typical sleep and decide in advance what you will do when it goes off. The decision matters more than the method, because the failure mode is waking up, feeling briefly alert, thinking about your day, and drifting back. You cannot improvise in that window.

Write the plan on paper and put it where you will see it. It should say: on waking, do not move, look at the ceiling or at the back of your eyelids, hold the memory of where you were, and look for the sense that you are watching. That last part is the actual technique, and it is easier when you have rehearsed it. Expect a period of several weeks where nothing happens. That period is normal, it is not evidence that you cannot do it, and treating it as a verdict is the most common reason people quit a method that works.

Step two, wake without moving

The single most important physical action is doing nothing. Movement shifts you into full waking sleep architecture and the window closes. Most people fail here by rolling over, or by reaching for a phone, or by standing up to use a bathroom, and they then report having been nearly lucid. Nearly is the whole problem. The state is fragile, and the first physical act is usually what ends it.

When you do find yourself lucid, resist the urge to test it by looking at your hands. That is a reliable way to lose it. Instead, hold the state and look for text. Reading in a lucid dream is possible for a good proportion of people and it is a strong reinforcement, because confirming text is far more persuasive than confirming that you have hands, which you can half-imagine. If you want one question answered, write it down before you sleep and read it while lucid.

Step three, the reality test that actually works

Time is the reliable test. Clocks in dreams are unstable, and a clock that is running while you watch it will usually do something impossible within a minute or two. A stable clock over a longer period suggests you are awake, or in a rare and unusually coherent dream. This is worth knowing because the alternative is relying on how it feels, and how it feels is precisely the thing that is unreliable. A lucid dream is generally indistinguishable from a vivid dream by subjective quality alone.

Text and light switches are the other two that hold up. Light switches usually fail to operate or operate inconsistently, and the failure is informative rather than conclusive. If you are using a reality test, use two of them rather than one, and write in your log which one you used and what happened. The log is what converts this from a hobby into something you can improve, and it is the part that nearly everyone skips.

  1. Read a clock and watch it for at least a minute
  2. Try a light switch and note whether the light responds
  3. Look for written text and see whether it stays stable
  4. Ask yourself a question you wrote down before sleeping

Step four, log it while it is still present

Dream logs are the least glamorous and most effective part of the whole practice. The moment you are awake, write three things: where you were, what happened, and how you became lucid. Almost everyone omits that third field, and it is exactly the field that reveals your actual trigger. Some people find it is a written reminder. Some find it is a physical sensation. Some find it is simply having nowhere to be. The log will tell you which, and you cannot guess it in advance.

Keep the log boring and dated. Three months of entries will show you patterns that no amount of introspection will produce, including the nights that produce nothing and the sleep changes that precede a bad run. If you notice the log itself becoming distressing, that is a signal to reduce the practice rather than to intensify it, and the reduction is the correct move for the same reasons it is the correct move for over-breathwork or over-meditation. Nothing in this field rewards escalation, and the literature is unusually consistent on that point.

Step five, out of body experiences need a different approach

Forcing an out of body experience is a bad use of a good evening. The reports cluster near sleep onset and during illness, meditation and sleep debt, which means the condition most people try to create is a condition most people are already accidentally in. If it has happened to you, it is worth writing down. If it has not, the standard techniques have a modest hit rate and a real cost, because they all involve either sleep disruption or a version of dissociation that is unpleasant at best.

The safety literature on this is specific and worth reading before you start. The genuine documented risks are sleep disruption, anxiety, and in a small number of cases a persistent sense of derealisation that lasts after the practice stops. The way to manage all three is the same way: short sessions, regular hours, and a stop rule you write down in advance. If you already have a history of dissociation or psychotic episodes, the honest advice is to talk to a clinician first and treat this as a technique to be cleared, not a self-directed experiment.

Step six, what tulpas are actually for

The practice comes from Tibetan Buddhist traditions, where the method belongs to visualisation and deity yoga. The purpose is a disciplined capacity to hold a form in mind, not the creation of a separate being. Read that way it is a real and demanding contemplative discipline with a long history, and it is nothing like the companion-creating exercise that dominates the popular writing.

The distortion matters for practical reasons. If you treat the form as a being with its own wants, you are building an ambiguity into a practice that was designed to be unambiguous, and the ambiguity tends to resolve in the direction of projection. If you treat it as a skill, you get what the tradition actually offered: a way to hold a complicated mental image steady while you work with it. Most people who want the second thing can get it more easily from a structured visualisation practice with a defined duration and a defined end, and they will get it without any risk of the first.

Step seven, remote viewing and the description skill

The prospective results have not replicated, and anyone telling you otherwise is relying on a body of work that has been examined and found wanting. The part that survives scrutiny is duller and more useful. Training in describing visual material precisely, under time pressure, without seeing it first, improves your ability to describe what you have seen. That is a genuine perceptual skill and it is one of the better exercises available for anyone who writes, draws, investigates, or needs to look at a scene and say what is in it.

It is worth taking on those terms. A structured description exercise, where you look at an unfamiliar image for sixty seconds and then write everything you can still see, will improve your observational reporting and costs nothing. It will not let you see through walls, and the people who claim otherwise are making a claim about physics rather than about training. If you enjoy the framing, keep the framing and drop the claim. That habit is the single most transferable skill in this entire area, and it applies to every marginal claim in the field.

What belongs in the log and what does not

Belong in the log: date, wake time, time to sleep, whether you woke during the night, whether you were lucid, what triggered it, and one sentence about the content. Optionally the reality tests used and their results. That is the whole record and it is more than most people keep for years. Not in the log: interpretation, significance, what the dream was about, and any theory of what the experience means. Interpretation goes in a separate column if you insist, and you mark it as interpretation, because the moment the meaning gets logged as fact the log stops being able to argue with you.

This split is what makes the practice honest rather than merely interesting. A log of events will show you that your lucidity clusters around a particular wake time or a particular mood, and both of those are useful. A log of interpretations will show you that the dreams are about whatever you are worried about, which is true of everybody and tells you nothing. Keep the first. Discard the second. If you want the interpretation to be the object of study, take it to a practice with a built-in check, such as a divinatory method where the record is the draw and the meaning is separate.

The sleep debt trap

Every technique here becomes easier when you are sleep deprived and becomes worse in the sense of more vivid and less stable. That combination is exactly what makes it appealing and exactly why it is a trap. Sleep onset is when most reported out of body experiences occur, and sleep disruption reliably produces dreams vivid enough to be mistaken for waking. Chasing the vividness by cutting sleep produces a worse practice and a worse life, and the improvement feels real for a few weeks before it stops feeling real.

The rule that holds up is unglamorous: keep your sleep schedule stable before you change anything else, and treat any technique that requires you to be tired as a technique you are misusing. If you find yourself using an alarm to wake yourself into a dream state earlier and earlier, that is the trajectory and it does not have a good destination. Reduce, restore the schedule, and let the practice rebuild from the foundation. The people who get good results with these techniques are almost all the people who are sleeping properly, which is the least interesting sentence in this entire field and the most predictive.

A four-week structure that works

Week one is baseline: keep your schedule normal, do nothing else, and write down every dream you can recall. You are establishing what recall looks like for you before you add an induction. Week two is technique: fixed wake time, alarm in the last third, stillness on waking, one written question. Expect little. Week three is testing: two or three lucids with a full log, including the reality test used. Week four is review, and the only three questions are how often, how reliably, and whether it changed anything outside the dream.

The reason for the ordering is that it separates the three things people conflate: recall, which is trainable immediately; lucidity, which takes weeks; and meaning, which you are not measuring at all in the first month. If you skip week one, every subsequent result is uninterpretable, because you do not know what your baseline was. If you start interpreting in week two, you will find the dream confirms whatever you expected, which is the finding the research literature is mostly about. Four weeks is enough to know whether the technique does anything for you and nowhere near enough to support any claim about what dreams are.

The question worth asking before you continue

Ask what you are hoping the practice will give you, and answer it in a sentence you could say out loud. If the answer is about remembering dreams, that is a fully legitimate goal with a real technique and a measurable result, and you can have it in a fortnight. If the answer is about leaving your body, you are signing up for a technique with a low hit rate and a documented capacity to make you feel worse, and you should read the safety material before your first session rather than after your worst one. If the answer is that you want to know whether any of this is real, then none of these methods will settle it, and the ones that appear to settle it quickly are the ones most likely to be delivering something other than an answer.

That framing is the whole of the practical content. The techniques are genuinely good, the literature is unusually honest about their limits, and the failure mode in this area is not a bad technique. It is a person who never decided what they wanted and so accepted whatever showed up. Decide first. The methods are easier to practise well when you know what success looks like, and much easier to abandon correctly when you know what failure looks like.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which of these is actually reproducible at home?

Lucid dreaming, by a wide margin. It has a large measured population, documented induction techniques, and the clearest characterisation in the sleep literature. Out of body experiences are real for a small minority and usually arrive uninvited. Remote viewing has not replicated. Tulpas belong to a Tibetan practice with a different purpose than the popular framing suggests.

How long before I have a lucid dream?

Weeks, not nights. The realistic sequence is two weeks of dream logging to establish recall, then two to four weeks of fixed wake time with a stillness technique. A period of nothing happening is the normal middle of the curve rather than evidence of failure, and treating it as a verdict is the most common reason people quit a method that works.

Why morning waking instead of a dream journal?

REM periods lengthen through the night, so the final third of sleep carries the longest REM and the highest odds of lucidity. Waking in that window is a scheduling decision rather than a mystical one, and it produces a much larger return than any technique performed at bedtime. Keep the journal as well, but it serves recall rather than induction.

Is it safe to practise this without supervision?

Short, regular practice with stable sleep hours is low risk. The documented problems cluster around sleep disruption, breath work, and prolonged or forced attempts, and all three are managed by reduction rather than by pushing harder. If you have a history of dissociation or psychotic episodes, talk to a clinician first and treat this as something to be cleared rather than a self-directed experiment.

Does a paid dream app help more than a paper log?

It helps in one specific way: an app can log the timestamp the moment you wake, which removes the five minutes of drift that makes handwritten logs inaccurate. Beyond that it is a notebook with a nicer interface, and the notebook works. A one-time purchase is reasonable if you will actually use the alarm and timestamp features; a subscription for a log you keep on paper is a waste of the same money in a more confusing way.
