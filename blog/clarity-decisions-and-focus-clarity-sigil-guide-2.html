Clarity, Decisions and Focus

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

A reference for clarity work is not a set of methods, it is a set of questions, and the difference is the whole design decision. A method tells you what to do, which is comfortable and produces the appearance of progress. A question tells you what to look at, which is uncomfortable and occasionally produces an answer. This article assembles the terms and tables you need to run a clarity working without pretending the system is doing the thinking. Four ideas carry it. Attentional control is among the most studied faculties in psychology, with training methods that replicate. Decision paralysis is nearly always a conflict between criteria rather than a shortage of data. Projective methods reach material that self-report does not, which is the honest reason cards function in this kind of work and not as prediction. And gnosis, in the sense the contemplative traditions use it and which is considerably older than the movement that borrowed the word, is a moment of sustained contact rather than an intellectual position, and it supplies a standard rather than a concept.

What the reference is for

This reference exists to be consulted in the ten minutes before you draft a working, not studied in advance. That is a deliberate design choice, and it reflects how this kind of work actually fails. Clarity problems are rarely solved by knowing more about clarity. They are solved by finding the specific question you are actually asking, and the finding takes ninety seconds with a written list and about three hours with a book.

So the contents are narrow on purpose. Terms that get confused, one framework, one drafting procedure, one logging method, and a short list of things the system cannot tell you. If you find yourself reading the reference for reassurance about your situation, that is the moment to stop reading and go write the criteria column, because the reading is functioning as avoidance and the column is the only part that moves.

Attentional control, and the training that works

Attentional control is trainable, the methods are replicated, and the transfer to unpractised tasks is real but partial. Nobody becomes able to concentrate indefinitely by intending to, and the interventions that produce measurable gains are unglamorous: sustained attention on a single task for a fixed period, with a graduated difficulty, practised regularly.

For clarity work this is not background knowledge, it is the actual input. The decision you are trying to reach requires you to hold several criteria in mind at once and not be captured by the loudest one, and that is an attentional task before it is a mystical one. The practical instruction is to place the decision in a window where your attention is already trained rather than wherever the decision happens to surface, and to record the time of day in the log so the placement can be checked instead of assumed.

Criterion conflict instead of information deficit

The dominant error in clarity work is treating a decision as a research problem. It is not. Once the information is in, the remaining difficulty is that two or more things you want are in tension, and tension has no evidence to find. Safety against income, honesty against protection, speed against being right. No quantity of further reading resolves those, because the question is what to prioritise rather than what is true.

Write them in a column, one per line, with nothing about solutions underneath. The moment you can see them side by side the fog clears, and the decision becomes a choice between named items rather than a search for a missing fact. A sigil built from a chosen priority is then aimed at something that exists and can be checked later, which is the difference between a working and a mood.

The imperative, and why writing it changes things

The diagnostic move is grammatical. Write the aim as an imperative sentence and test whether it can be completed, can be falsified, and would be done today. I will decide between the two offers passes all three. I will be clear about what I want fails all three, because clarity is a state and states are not completable.

This single test catches more unusable aims than anything else in the practice, and it works because the failure is grammatical before it is psychological. Sentences describing states are the ones you cannot date, cannot check, and cannot fail, which means a sigil charged for them can go on producing a feeling of having done something indefinitely without ever producing an outcome. Keep the sentence visible for the whole working, written in the imperative, on paper, in the front of your attention.

Projective methods and the limit of self-report

Projective techniques, the Rorschach among them, were built on a sound observation and are now largely discredited as measurement, which is a separate thing from being useless. The sound observation is that self-report has limits, and that some material is more accessible when the person responding is not being asked directly about themselves.

A drawn card is a good instrument for that purpose for a specific reason: it has no relationship to your situation that you did not supply. That absence is a feature. Draw, write three or four sentences about what you see, and check whether any of it names a criterion you had not listed. When it does, you have gained something. When it does not, log it as nothing rather than digging, because a surface that produces nothing has done its job by producing nothing quickly.

Gnosis, defined operationally

Operationally, gnosis is a moment of sustained contact, and the operative word is sustained. A flash of insight that lasts ninety seconds and ends is a mood, and moods are not controllable. The same moment sustained, with attention returning each time it wanders, is a practice, and the returning is the discipline rather than an optional addition to it.

Applied to clarity, this reframes what a sigil is for. It is not for the insight, which arrives unasked and cannot be aimed. It is for the repeated return to a decision after the insight has faded and the criteria have resumed arguing, which is the state in which most decisions are actually abandoned. A working that holds the priority in place across several of those returns is doing something that the insight itself cannot.

The five-question framework

One, what decision is this, in one noun phrase, not a situation. Two, what are the competing criteria, listed. Three, which one am I prioritising, chosen and dated. Four, what action do I take that makes it visible, in the imperative, completable in under ten minutes. Five, what would tell me in a month that this was the wrong priority, written before, not decided later.

The fifth question is the one that makes the framework usable rather than decorative, and it is also the one most often skipped. Answering it in advance converts the review from a matter of interpretation into a matter of comparison, and a comparison can be made from a page of notes by anyone, including a sceptic, including you in six weeks when you would prefer not to look.

A first working, from the framework

An hour. Fifteen minutes for the criteria column, in your own handwriting, no digital list. Five to ground, meaning name the room, the hour of the day, and the next physical thing you will do. Ten to charge the sigil, five repetitions, then stop. Twenty to sit with the aim visible, timed. Ten for notes, and nothing interpretive.

The proportions are not arbitrary. The criteria column is the method and the twenty minutes is where it operates, so if you are short of time, cut the charge rather than the sitting. Cutting the sitting produces a charged sigil and no engagement with the decision, which is the most common way a clarity working produces nothing and feels like it did something.

What the framework cannot tell you

It cannot tell you which priority is correct. It cannot tell you whether the decision will turn out well. It cannot tell you whether the outcome is evidence the method works, because a decision that goes well and a decision that goes badly are both compatible with a well-run process. And it cannot tell you anything about the world; it is a procedure for noticing what you already want and then committing to it visibly.

This limit is not a defect and pretending otherwise is how clarity work becomes a way of feeling informed. What the framework provides is a guarantee that a decision was made deliberately, with its competing criteria written down and its failure condition recorded. That is genuinely more than most decisions get, and it is worth having even though it is unremarkable as a metaphysical claim.

Adapting the aim to the decision at hand

Decisions differ in shape, and the aim should follow the shape rather than follow a template. A choice between two options gets a commitment sentence. A decision about an ongoing situation gets a first action, because the situation has not resolved and nothing can be committed to yet. A decision you have already made and are avoiding gets the earliest observable step, usually a conversation.

The common error is using a commitment sentence for a situation. It cannot be completed, so it fails the grammatical test, so the sigil encodes a state and the work drifts. Naming which of the three shapes you are in takes ten seconds and prevents the most common structural error in the entire method.

Where the record and the framework disagree

They will disagree, and the disagreement is the useful part. A log showing that a commitment held for two weeks and then quietly stopped has told you something the framework could not: the priority was not strong enough, or the criteria were incompletely listed, or the situation changed and the new contender was never written down.

Read for that specifically rather than for confirmation. The five questions exist partly so that when the record disagrees with the plan, you can find out which of the five was wrong. Usually it is question two, because an incompletely listed criterion is invisible from inside the decision and obvious from two months of notes. Re-run question two, rewrite the sentence, and charge again.

Keeping the reference while the aim changes

The reference is the stable part and the aim is not. Decisions change, situations acquire new contenders, and a sentence written six weeks ago refers to a world that no longer exists in that form. Keeping the reference and revising the aim is the intended pattern, not an exception to it.

Version the aim, not the reference. Keep dated copies of each aim sentence with a line on what changed and why, and let the reference stay fixed for a season. A reference that changes with every aim is a record rather than a tool, and a record cannot be consulted under pressure. After a year you will have a set of dated sentences and a short list of the criteria that kept reappearing, and the second is a considerably more useful object than the first.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my clarity problem is a criterion conflict?

Write each thing you want in a column, one per line, with nothing about solutions. If the remaining difficulty is which of them to prioritise, it is a criterion conflict and no amount of research will resolve it. If you cannot list two or more, you may genuinely have an information deficit, and the next step is research rather than ritual.

Why write the aim as an imperative sentence?

Because the test is grammatical and catches more than a psychological test would. An imperative sentence can be completed, can be falsified, and can be dated. Sentences describing states fail all three, which means a sigil charged for them can go on feeling productive indefinitely without ever being checkable. The imperative form is the cheapest diagnostic in the practice.

Are cards in a clarity working prediction or projection?

Projection, and the distinction matters. A drawn card has no information about your situation that you did not supply, which is exactly why it reaches material self-report does not. Draw, write a few sentences, and check whether any of it names a criterion you had not listed. If it does not, log that and stop rather than digging further into a surface that has nothing to give.

How often should I revisit a clarity working?

Monthly is enough and weekly is defensible for a live decision. The review is short: read the dated aim sentence, read the entries, and check the failure condition you wrote in advance. What you are looking for is the moment the record stopped agreeing with the plan, since that date is almost always the real end of the working and it is rarely the date you would have guessed.

Does the framework tell me which option is right?

No, and it is not designed to. It forces the competing criteria into view, forces a written priority, and records what would count as having been wrong. Whether the priority was correct is settled by outcomes over months, if it is ever settled clearly. A process that reliably produces deliberate, documented decisions is worth a great deal without claiming more than that.
