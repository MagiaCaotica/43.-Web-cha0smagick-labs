Grant Morrison and the Chaos Magic Revolution in Comics

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

Almost everything written about chaos magic in comics is wrong about a detail that matters: the first person to use it structurally was Alan Moore, and the first person to use it as autobiography was Grant Morrison, and they did not do it the same way. A decade of Vertigo superheroes that borrowed the vocabulary without the mechanism sits between them and every reader who arrives today. Getting the sequence right changes what the material is for.

The 1980s synthesis was British, and it was not a single author

Chaos magic entered mainstream comics in a specific place during a specific decade. Alan Moore and Grant Morrison were both working in British superhero comics in the mid-1980s, both were reading Spare and Crowley, and both were dissatisfied with a genre whose entire apparatus assumed a supervising intelligence behind the page. Moore came at it in 1985 with Swamp Thing, where he treated the story as a structural object rather than a window. Morrison came at it with the British new wave, Zenith among them, arriving 1987.

The reason this matters is that almost nobody else in the industry had read the sources. A working comic in 1990 assumed the reader already knew what a sigil was and why Austin Osman Spare mattered, and that assumption is why the whole edifice collapsed into superhero slang within five years. A practice that only gets borrowed by people who skipped the primary texts does not survive the borrowing. The Americans who arrived next had read the surface of it and none of the argument underneath, which is exactly what you would predict from the outcome.

What Moore actually did, in Swamp Thing and Promethea

The Promethea volume, known to collectors as The Chymical Wedding, ran across 2002 to 2003 and is the most sustained use of the idea in comics. It is a story about a fictional 16th-century doctor discovering that the world is a series of nested symbolic systems and that every layer can be read from the one beneath it. The plot is a ritual, the argument is an epistemology, and the villain is a man who wants to collapse the layers for the sake of clarity.

What Moore is doing structurally is Spare's reduction applied to narrative form rather than to a phrase. A symbol stands for something, it is loaded and then released, and what you get back is the thing rather than the description of the thing. Moore's version of the working is that a reader who has followed the argument for two hundred pages does not have a description of a world, they have the world, and that is a different thing. If you want to see a working described accurately in a medium with no precedent for it, that volume is the closest thing available.

Morrison used it as autobiography, which is the opposite move

Batman Arkham Asylum, written across 1988 and 1989, is a long argument about a book called Journey into the Whirlwind that features a version of Batman who has no fourth wall, and the book is doing something specific when Batman reads it: it takes a person apart and shows them the mechanism. A very long section breaks the story down into panels and narrates the process by which a reader is recruited. Then it is over and the narrative asks you to do something.

The later, better example is Batman: The Maze, which ran through 1990 to 1991. Batman is in an institution that is not Arkham, three months have passed, and the story has no author in it. The structure is the point. Morrison built a work that cannot be completed by reading it, and the reader's frustration is the instrument. It is a gnosis technique, and it is one of the cleanest instances of a gnosis technique in any medium: the material is built so that the attempt to solve it is what changes the person attempting it.

The serial is the part almost nobody copies

Of Morrison's late-1980s work, the thing with the most direct bearing on practice is the New Age serial, published across 1991 in 2000 AD, and it is about a fictional magickal group doing exactly what a practitioner does. There is a person who wants to free his wife from a nightmare, and the group treats his intention as a specification with a completion condition and a review date, and the group is wrong about the method, and the story is about how the specification fails and what is left after it fails.

Read it as a training manual and it will disappoint you, because it does not present a technique that works. Read it as a description of a community's method, including the parts that are unsound, and it is more useful than any grimoire chapter on the subject, because the failure is where the information is. That is the standard this channel uses for every entity article, and it comes from the same place: a system specification is more valuable with its failure conditions attached than without them.

What Vertigo did with the vocabulary

Vertigo, the DC imprint that ran from 1993 to 2019, was where the transformation happened, and it is worth being precise about what it produced. Sandman, Preacher, Transmetropolitan, Fables and The Invisibles all used a real or near-real occult substrate, and roughly half of them had read the sources. The other half used chaos magick as a register, a way of writing wizards who talked like their characters talk.

Kyle Rayner, introduced 1993, is the clearest example of the collapse. The character was created as a horned-headed metaphysical construct and by the end of his first series the implications had been fully domesticated into a green energy battery, a chosen weapon, and a lantern corps with recruitment criteria. None of that is a misunderstanding of chaos magick. It is a character arc, and the arc required the metaphysics to be a lie. This is the ordinary fate of a borrowed system with a commercial owner, and it is worth naming as a risk rather than treating it as a failure of taste.

The honest case that fiction changes belief is documented

There is a real claim buried in all of this and it deserves to be stated without embarrassment. Reading a piece of fiction that argues for a position, and finding yourself believing it, is not a trick. It is the normal operation of language, and the structural version of it, where a work is built so that completing it requires a change in the reader, is doing the same work in a more targeted way. Morrison has said plainly that Promethea changed readers, and there is no reason to treat that as a claim about magic rather than a claim about persuasion.

This is where the honest overlap with your own practice sits, and it is also where the cheap version sits. You can adopt a fictional technique as a working. The tests are the same ones you would apply to a ritual drawn from a real source. Does it change a specific behaviour, can someone else observe that change, does it survive you putting it away for the whole interval, and are you running the practice or running the interpretation of the practice. A story that persuades you of a belief without giving you a behaviour has changed nothing, and a story that gives you a behaviour has changed something, and the difference is measurable.

Where Morrison's later work leaves the practice behind

Morrison's 2000s and 2010s work runs deliberately away from ritual. All-Star Superman makes a superhero out of a concept rather than a power. New X-Men builds a resurrection without any theology attached. The Invisibles, despite the name, is not a chaos magick book in the sense that matters here: it is about people refusing to accept the terms under which reality is offered. That refusal is a political move and it is a good one, and it is not a working.

If you are looking for a fiction practice to borrow, take the 1988 to 1991 work, not the later work. The later work shows a writer who absorbed a set of commitments in youth and then spent thirty years examining them without ceremony. That examination is valuable and it will not make anything happen in your life. What makes something happen is still the specification, the refusal clause, and the review date, and none of the three requires a comic book to supply them.

Five ways this material gets taken out of context

The first and most common is treating Vertigo superheroes as an accurate depiction of the tradition. They are not, and the character arcs required the metaphysics to be untrue. The second is claiming lineage. Moore and Morrison read the sources, which is a documented fact about two people. A writer in 2020 using chaos magick as slang has no relationship to either of them beyond the published pages, and calling that a lineage is the error the whole history of this literature is an example of.

The third is using a story's persuasion as evidence for the story's claims. A novel that convinces you of a proposition has demonstrated nothing except that the novel was well built. The fourth is reading the cosmic material as practice. There is a difference between a writer enjoying big ideas and a writer doing work, and the second one has a log. The fifth, and the most expensive, is the costume. People arrive wearing a trench coat and a pentagram, which is an aesthetic, and an aesthetic contains no method, so there is nothing there to fail and therefore nothing there to learn.

  1. Judge by description and reading, not by era: what did the author actually have open on the desk?
  2. Separate a structural use from a decorative one. A structural use is built into the form; a decorative one is dialogue about the form.
  3. Treat persuasion as persuasion. A work that changes your belief has demonstrated nothing about the proposition.
  4. Ask whether a behaviour followed. Belief without a behaviour is a mood, and moods are not outcomes.
  5. Copy the specification, never the aesthetic.

Frequently Asked Questions

Did Grant Morrison really practise chaos magick?

There is no reliable evidence that he did, and it does not matter. What is documented is that he read the sources closely enough to use them structurally, which is a real and demanding intellectual achievement. Whether belief accompanied the technique is a private matter, and the comics work stands either way.

What is the closest comics equivalent to a working?

Batman: The Maze, 1990 to 1991. It is built so that the reader's attempt to complete it is the mechanism, and that is a structural description of what a gnosis technique is supposed to do. It fails for most readers, and the failure is part of what it is.

Is Vertigo the same tradition as chaos magick?

No, and the difference is not a matter of degree. A tradition has sources, a method, and a failure record. Vertigo has source material and a house style, and the two were combined by editors looking for a register. The combination was commercially successful and intellectually thinner than either half.

Can I use a fictional technique as a ritual?

Yes, and the tests are the same you would apply to any working. Does it change a specific behaviour, can someone else observe that, does it survive the interval with the material put away, and are you running it or your account of running it. A technique that produces only a feeling has produced nothing you can review.

Should I start with Promethea or the Batman run?

The Batman run, if you want the working. The Chymical Wedding if you want the argument. Promethea rewards a reader who already has the historical background and frustrates one who does not, and the frustration is not a flaw in it, it is the cost of the form.
