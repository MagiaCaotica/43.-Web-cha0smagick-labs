Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 11 min read

Almost nobody writes down the part of starting that is genuinely difficult, because the part that is genuinely difficult has no content. There is no technique to learn in week one, no method to acquire, nothing to do except continue doing a small thing for a fortnight while nothing observable happens. Every practical guide to this material spends its first pages on definitions and its middle on results, and the two weeks in the middle get skipped. That gap is where most people stop, and the reason they stop is not that the material is difficult. It is that the material is easy, and there is nothing yet to hold on to. A first working in this tradition is deliberately boring. A written aim, a glyph, a short charge, and a fortnight of repeating an action you were already going to take. If the fortnight is uncomfortable, that discomfort is the method working, not evidence that you have picked the wrong path, and knowing that in advance is most of what makes the first month survivable.

The first fortnight, and what it feels like

Week one is administrative. You write something down, you make a mark, you sit with it briefly, and then you have nothing to do for six days. This is genuinely uncomfortable and nobody warns you, because the whole of the practical advice is written by people who are describing the part that comes after the boredom has passed.

Week two is worse, in a way that is hard to predict and easy to misread. You begin to wonder whether you did it right. You go back to check the aim. You find small differences between what you have written and what you remember writing, and the differences matter enormously and mean nothing. This is not a sign that something is wrong. It is what a fortnight with no feedback looks like from the inside, and every tradition that does not promise quick results has a version of this week and none of them enjoy it.

The two documents, and the order

The movement that came out of the early 1970s and formalised itself in 1976 has two short documents at its foundation, and they are not equally approachable. One is a practical manual that happens to be ideological, roughly a hundred pages, and usable immediately. The other is an argument, more polemical, that contains instructions inside the argument and assumes you already want to argue.

Read the manual first and the argument second, and read both in full rather than selectively. The manual is where your first working will come from, including most of the procedural vocabulary, and it does not require agreement with anything. The argument is where the reasoning comes from, and it is worth reading early precisely because it is the more frustrating of the two and you will be in a better mood to read it once you have done something small.

Building nothing on day one

The most common first week is spent acquiring. People buy things. They download things. They assemble a toolkit for a practice that at this stage requires paper and a pen, and the acquisition is a way of not doing the boring part while feeling that the practice has begun.

I did this, and I want to describe it accurately rather than mock it, because the motivation is legitimate. You cannot begin, and the acquisition is available, and it is a form of preparation that a person who has read enough books to reach this point finds extremely easy to justify. It also has a property that makes it a poor substitute: it produces no record, and the record is the only thing that will tell you in a month whether you did anything. Buying something produces a receipt. That is the entire difference, and the receipt is not a record.

The first aim, small enough to be dull

Your first aim should be embarrassing. Not a relationship, not a body, not a career, not money. Something you could do anyway on a bad day, written in the imperative, with a number and a date in it. The purpose is not the outcome. The purpose is to have an aim whose execution you cannot confuse with coincidence.

This looks like a retreat and it is the opposite. A large aim gives you nothing to attribute, because you cannot tell whether a partial result came from the practice or from the other eight things you were doing. A dull aim gives you a fortnight in which the answer is unambiguous, which is worth an enormous amount when you are trying to find out whether the method works. Most people pick a large aim for their first working and then spend the month unable to conclude anything at all. Pick a dull one. Be ambitious in month four.

The fortnight of nothing happening

This is the fortnight, and I want to give it its proper name. Nothing happens. The aim is dull, so even the small win you were hoping for is not there, and what you have instead is the experience of repeating an action with no evidence that the repetition is doing anything. This is genuinely the worst two weeks of the whole practice and also the only part that is load-bearing.

The reason it is load-bearing is that everything in this tradition is downstream of the willingness to do it. The claims about sigils and intention and belief are all downstream of one thing, which is whether you do the small boring thing when the small boring thing produces nothing. A person who will do that has a practice. A person who will not, does not, and no amount of doctrine or symbolism substitutes for it. The fortnight is not a test of your commitment to the method. It is a description of the method, which is mostly this.

Where the method becomes uncomfortable

There is a specific week where the discomfort changes character. Up to then you are bored. Then, usually around day ten or eleven, you notice that you are constructing arguments about whether the practice works, and that the arguments are more elaborate than anything you would have thought up before starting. You are doing epistemology in your head while trying to complete a form.

This is the movement's own method arriving at you rather than being applied by you, and it is the most valuable single thing in the whole first month. The founding documents insist on experiment over doctrine, and the experiment is supposed to be run on claims you already hold, which is uncomfortable precisely because you are being asked to be the instrument. The discomfort means the examination has started. A practice that never became uncomfortable was not examining anything.

The discomfort is the method working

The argument you were constructing has a name, which is a sign that the method is working, and the name is worth knowing because it will recur for as long as you do this. The mechanism is structural and not a matter of willpower. Any practice that records successes and does not record failures is a practice that will confirm whatever you believed when you started, and it will feel effective the whole time, because it will find support. Nothing about sincerity prevents this.

So the recommendation is not to try harder at neutralism. It is to build the record before you need it. Open the page before the aim, not after, and include a column for what you would have done anyway and a column for anything you noticed and cannot attribute. Do this once, in the first fortnight, while it still feels pedantic, and the argument you construct in week two has somewhere to go that is not your memory. My memory is not a neutral instrument. Neither is yours, and the whole apparatus of this tradition is an attempt to build one that is slightly less partial than memory.

The question nobody asks beginners

Whether you would still do it if the answer were no. This is asked by nobody, because the field is almost entirely composed of people who are enthusiasts, and the beginner reading it assumes the question is rhetorical. It is not.

It matters because a practice that needs the answer to be yes will end, and the ending will be disguised as a reassessment. You will find a flaw in the method, or a better system, or a more suitable aim, and none of those will be false. The reason people leave a practice is nearly always that the result did not arrive on the timescale they had implicitly agreed to, and they nearly always describe it as a methodological conclusion. Asking the question in the first month, when there is nothing at stake, is the only cheap time to ask it.

Recording the first month

A page. Dated entries, a fixed layout, written at the same time each day rather than whenever something happened to occur. Three columns: what you did, anything else you noticed, and anything you cannot attribute. The second column is the one that gets skipped and it is the one that protects you, because it is where a coincidence goes when you would otherwise claim it.

The discipline that matters is not writing a lot. It is writing on the flat days. The record is only honest if the boring fortnight is in it, and a record that starts on the day something went well is not a record of the practice, it is a record of the practice on its good days, which is a thing that can be made to support anything. Thirty short entries in a month is enough. Twenty is enough. Zero is a category of its own.

Deciding whether to continue

There is no correct answer and there is a correct way to arrive at one, which is to decide before the fortnight is over rather than after. Write down, on day one, what result would be interesting if it went the other way. Not encouraging, which is a different thing. Interesting. This is the practice's own method applied to the question of whether to do the practice, and it costs ten minutes and removes most of the ability to construct a comfortable answer later.

Most people who continue did not get a result in the first month. They got a result, if any, much later, and what kept them in was the small dull aim they completed over and over. The first aim is not supposed to be the one that convinces you. It is supposed to be the one that teaches you what the method feels like from the inside, which is information you cannot get any other way and cannot reason your way to before you have experienced it.

What continuing actually commits you to

Continuing commits you to a practice whose results are slow, ambiguous, and mostly indistinguishable from ordinary variation, in a field with almost no gatekeeping, almost no sequencing, and a great deal of confidently wrong material. That is the honest cost. It also commits you to a specific way of relating to your own claims, which is to hold them loosely while acting on them firmly, and most people find that harder than the magic.

The version of continuing that does not work is the one where you accumulate a practice of asserting without recording, and the outcome after two years is a system of beliefs that cannot be wrong, maintained by a person who never wrote anything down. The version that works is unglamorous and requires a page. If you keep a page, you will be in a small group of people who can tell what they are doing, and that is a more valuable position than it sounds.

The permission to stop

You are allowed to stop after one month and nothing is forfeited. This is said in the second paragraph of most books in this field and is not believed by anybody reading them, and I would like to make it slightly more concrete than that. If you do the fortnight, keep the record honestly, and decide in month two that this is not for you, then you have completed the experiment properly. That is a better outcome than continuing for two years on momentum.

The permission is also worth taking seriously in the other direction, because the people who most need to hear it are the ones who will not. If you are in the first month and the discomfort in week two has become the thing that is holding the practice together, stop. That discomfort is valuable once, as information about how you react to being examined. Sustained by it, it is a different thing entirely, and continuing on that basis is how people end up defending a practice rather than doing one. The boring fortnight is enough to give you the information. Take it and go if you want to.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do I need to start?

Paper and a pen, and a fixed half hour somewhere you can be left alone. That is the whole requirement. The tradition is unusually clear that the apparatus is optional, and the acquisition impulse in the first week is a way of avoiding the boring part rather than a way of preparing. If you buy something, buy paper. Everything else in this field is available later, after you have a record showing that you did the small thing for a fortnight, which is the only credential that has ever meant anything here.

How do I know my first working was done right?

You probably cannot, and the honest answer is that nobody can at that stage. The tradition has no initiatory authority to tell you, and anyone who claims to is selling something. What you can check is procedural: was the aim written in the imperative, was it small enough to complete on a bad day, was the record opened before the aim, and are the flat days in it. Those four are checkable by you. The rest is a matter of reading the instructions carefully, and the instructions are short because the procedure is short.

What if nothing happens in the first month?

Then nothing happens, which is the normal result and the reason the first aim is supposed to be small. A month with no observable change is consistent with a working that works, a working that does nothing, and an aim that was too vague to have been tested. The first month cannot distinguish those three. What it can do is establish whether you kept the record, and that is the variable that determines what the second month is worth.

Should I buy books before I start?

Two, at most, and only the practical one to begin with. The temptation to assemble a reading list in month one is difficult to resist and mostly a way of not doing the fortnight. Read the practical manual, do the fortnight, and then read the argumentative one, which is more frustrating and more valuable and will land differently once you have done something small with it. Everything else can wait until you know what you are trying to answer.

Why does the founding text say experiment rather than doctrine?

Because the method is the contribution and the doctrine is disposable, and because a movement that supplied a doctrine would have been over in a decade. The instruction has a cost that is easy to understate: it means you have to run the test on claims you hold, which is the uncomfortable week in the first fortnight and the thing most people are not actually doing. It also means there is no authority to appeal to, since the whole point is that you run it yourself.
