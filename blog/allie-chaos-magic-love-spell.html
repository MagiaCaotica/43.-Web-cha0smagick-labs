ALLIE

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 13 min read

Love is the most requested working in this material and the one most likely to produce a result you did not want. That is not a warning about the technique being weak. It is a warning about the aim being aimed at the wrong target. What follows is the beginner route, including the version of this working that is defensible and the two versions that are not.

Why This One Has Rules Attached

Practitioners who work with sigils for years will tell you the same thing: almost nothing in this material is as dangerous as a sigil aimed at another person's feelings. Not because sigils are unusually potent, but because this is the only common aim whose whole mechanism depends on overriding somebody else's stated preferences.

The core objection is not mystical, it is structural. A sigil aimed at a job, a car or a passing examination is aimed at a system that has no opinion. A sigil aimed at a person's love is aimed at a person, and that person has preferences, and the working works by outvoting them.

The defensive argument usually offered is that the practitioner only wants them to be happy, and that this is therefore a benevolent act. It does not survive contact with the question: would you still run the same working if the outcome were that they become deeply unhappy with you? If the answer is no, the benevolence was conditional, and a conditional wish is still a wish imposed on someone else.

The good news is that the useful version of this working does not require the objection to be ignored. It requires the target to be changed. Everything below is built that way, deliberately, because the self-directed version is both defensible and far more likely to produce something you can live with.

What a Sigil Cannot Do For You

Before any technique, it is worth being blunt about the ceiling, because most disappointment in this area comes from expecting something the method cannot deliver regardless of belief.

A sigil cannot make a specific person want you. It can influence your own attention, your own availability and your own behaviour, and those have a documented effect on how other people respond to you. That is a weaker claim than the one people are sold, and it is the one that survives contact with evidence.

The reason the stronger claim fails so often is visible in practice rather than theory. Working regularly on attraction reliably produces obsessive rumination about the target. You check their status more often. You rehearse conversations. You read their messages as meaningful. This feels intense and it is easily mistaken for progress, and it is actually the opposite: rumination narrows your attention, damages your availability, and makes you less attractive as a person to be around.

So the honest diagnosis of a failed love working is frequently that the working was too successful at focusing you. The intervention is to change the aim, not to increase the dose. Anyone who tells you to escalate an attraction working in response to a lack of results is describing a mechanism that makes the result less likely.

The Version Worth Building

The defensible working keeps the sigil and moves the target. Instead of a statement about what someone else should feel, you write one about what you will do. The shift sounds cosmetic and is not, because the statement is what the sigil encodes, so the target is genuinely different.

A workable statement has three parts: an availability you can actually sustain, a communication habit you can actually keep, and one condition you can meet without pretending. For example, that you will be present and warm in conversations without performing, that you will let a good connection develop at its own pace rather than escalating it, and that you will not pursue anyone who has made it clear they are not interested.

That third clause is the one that makes the whole thing honest, and it is the one most people delete. Including a self-limiting condition in your own aim is what distinguishes a working you can trust from a working you are running against your own better judgement, and it costs nothing in effectiveness.

The mechanical shape of the practice is then ordinary. Reduce the statement to a sigil, charge it in a short private session, and then leave it alone for the duration of the cycle you committed to. The sigil is a commitment device, which is a genuinely well-supported technique in behavioural science, and the commitment is to your own conduct rather than to an outcome you do not control.

In practice this version is duller to describe and much better to live with. Nobody is going to be able to tell you it worked in a week. What you will notice is that you are more available, less rehearsing, and more willing to be told no, which is a measurable improvement in most people's actual dating lives.

Reconciliation Is a Different Operation

Restoring contact with someone you previously had a relationship with is treated as a separate operation from creating attraction, and the distinction is worth keeping because the risk profile genuinely differs.

The argument for treating it as lower risk is that a bond already existed. You are not manufacturing something out of nothing, you are addressing a channel that was open before. That is a real difference and it is why most ethical frameworks in this material permit reconciliation more readily than attraction.

The counterargument is that the previous relationship ended for reasons, and those reasons have not changed. The older sources in this area are fairly consistent about what repeated working against one person does, and it is not described as a neutral repetition.

So the defensible version of a reconciliation working is also self-directed. It aims at your own capacity to leave the door open and to be someone worth reopening it to, rather than at the other person's willingness. A statement about your own conduct, your own honesty about what happened, and your own acceptance of their answer is a working you can live with, including in the likely case that the answer is no.

The version to avoid is one that aims at removing the other person's resistance. That phrase is doing a lot of work in how such workings get described, and unpacking it shows the problem immediately: it is an operation whose success condition is the removal of someone's judgement, which is the part of a person you are least entitled to overwrite.

Your First Hour, Step by Step

This is the route for someone doing their first work in this area. It is short on purpose, because a first attempt should be easy to repeat and easy to abandon if it turns out not to fit how you actually think.

The first fifteen minutes are for choosing the aim, and the choice is the whole exercise. Write three candidate statements, all of them about your own conduct. If you cannot write three, you are not ready to build one, and the correct next step is reading the ethics material rather than pushing harder on this.

The next fifteen are for reducing the chosen statement to a sigil. Do it by hand. The handwriting is not a mystical ingredient, but the slowness of it is useful, because it forces you to read the statement as you encode it, and most people notice at least one clause they do not actually want to keep.

Then charge it. Ten minutes, in a private room, with the sigil in front of you, and a stated start and end. What you are doing is committing to a behaviour for a fixed period, and the ceremony exists to make that commitment feel like a commitment rather than a thought.

Whether you learn anything is settled in the last twenty minutes. Write down what you expect to be different in three weeks, in terms someone else could observe. Then write the date. Then stop, and do not revisit it until the date, which is the hardest instruction in this entire piece and the one that most improves the result.

Escalation Is the Documented Failure

The most consistent warning in the older material on this subject concerns repetition. Working repeatedly against one person is described as escalating, and that description is worth respecting even if you reject the metaphysics entirely, because it accurately names a real pattern in human behaviour.

The mechanism is straightforward. Each session that produces nothing increases the urgency of the next one. Urgency increases the intensity of the aiming. Intensity increases rumination. Rumination makes the situation worse, and the worsening is read as evidence that the working needs more force. The loop closes on itself and the practical cost is that you have damaged your own attention and often a relationship you still valued.

The same pattern has a documented non-magical twin in obsessive checking, in gambling, and in repeated unwanted contact. All of them share a structure: a behaviour with an unreliable payoff schedule, pursued at increasing intensity because the payoff is not arriving. Recognising that shape is more useful than any ritual disclaimer, because it lets you apply the ordinary behavioural tools that work, such as a hard stop written down in advance.

The practical rule I would give a first-timer: decide the number of sessions before you start, write it down, and stop when you reach it regardless of how it feels. Six sessions over a month is plenty. If the aim is well-formed and self-directed, you will not need more. If it seems to demand more, the aim is badly formed and the extra sessions are not the remedy.

Reading the Result Honestly

Pre-registration is the mechanism that separates a practice from a wish. It is unglamorous and it is the only part of this that reliably protects you from the most common failure, which is constructing a story after the fact.

Write the prediction before the charge, in observable terms. Not that you will feel more confident, because feeling is exactly what you cannot trust here. Instead: that you will have made one more genuinely warm first conversation than in the previous month, or that you will have gone four weeks without checking a particular status, or that you will have declined to pursue someone who was not interested.

Then count attempts including the ones that produced nothing at all, and keep the failures in the same file as the successes. A practice that only records its hits cannot be evaluated, and the person doing the recording is the person most motivated to record generously.

Two controls are worth running. One session with the same structure and no aim tells you how much of the effect is the structure itself, which is usually more than expected. One session with the aim stated only to yourself tells you how much of it is the sigil, which is also usually less than expected. Both are cheap and both make the remaining results interpretable.

And accept the null. Most of the time nothing in your circumstances will change because you encoded a sentence about your own conduct. The working's value on the null-result days is that it got you to state plainly what you were going to do differently, which is the part that sometimes survives contact with a real life.

What the Historical Sources Thought They Were Doing

The written tradition on attraction workings is extensive and it is worth understanding on its own terms, because most of it is not doing what the modern descriptions claim it is doing.

Ceremonial love workings overwhelmingly treat attraction as a symmetrical question. The concern is not how to make one specific person want you, it is how to become the sort of person who is available to being wanted, and the ritual objects are the person's own state. Heat, availability, reputation among peers and the removal of obstacles to being approached are the recurring elements, and only occasionally does a source turn to influencing a named third party.

Where the historical material does address influence over another person, it is usually in service of reconciliation rather than of creating something new, and the preparations described are often more about the operator's own state than about the target. That asymmetry is a clue about what was actually considered controllable.

The framing has shifted considerably. Modern practice split from the ceremonial models during the twentieth century, partly by declaring the practitioner's will to be the operative ingredient. That is a clean and useful simplification. Partly, too, by loosening the ethics that had come bundled with the elaborate apparatus. The loosening made the practice widely available and made the consent question sharper, since nothing in the simplified method stops you aiming at somebody.

Reading the older material is therefore a form of ethics rather than nostalgia. It shows you what a serious version of this looks like, and the serious versions were overwhelmingly about your own conduct.

Where People Actually Get Hurt Here

The most common serious harm in this area is not spiritual and not magical. It is that people spend months of their attention on a person they are not speaking to, and the ritual makes the fixation feel productive rather than painful.

That harm is easy to misread, because the working is genuinely effortful. Building a sigil, setting a time, sitting down, writing predictions: none of that is idle, and effortful attention to a problem is easy to confuse with progress on the problem. The tell is that none of the effort has changed any contact with the other person, and none of it was aimed at your own conduct.

The second harm is social. Repeatedly attempting influence on a named person who has declined, or repeatedly trying to remove their judgement, damages your standing with the people around you, and the damage is entirely deserved.

The third is that the practice gets a reputation it does not deserve. Nothing here justifies a sigil aimed at someone who has said no, and no framework in this material claims that it does. The word love doing the work in the name is what makes the abuse possible, and naming the aim accurately is the cheapest protection available.

If you take one habit from this piece, make it writing the aim out in plain language and reading it back to yourself before encoding. Aimed at your own conduct, it reads as a commitment. Aimed at someone else's feelings, it reads exactly like what it is, which is usually enough to make you set it down.

A Reasonable Place to Stop

This is a beginner route, and beginner routes should have an exit. The natural place to stop is when you find yourself treating a sigil as a strategy for a specific person rather than as a commitment about your own behaviour. That switch is easy to miss and it is the most important thing on this page.

You have also reached the point of diminishing returns if the working has run its course of six sessions without a change you can point to. Repeating it is not the answer at that stage. The honest move is to write down what the aim was, what you learned about yourself while doing it, and to stop.

What comes after stopping is usually more useful than what came before. The self-directed version of this working tends to produce a shift that does not announce itself, and the noticing takes months rather than weeks. Most people who do this well end up describing it as having become more available rather than as having become more successful, and the second of those would not have been a legitimate aim anyway.

If you continue past the exit, keep the log, keep the hard stop, and keep the aim aimed at yourself. Those three constraints are what make the practice honest, and dropping any one of them is how a beginner route turns into the thing this article exists to warn against.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do sigils aimed at attraction produce any real effect?

What they reliably produce is obsessive rumination about the target, and that is not the same as attraction. It narrows your attention, rehearses conversations you are not having, and makes you read ordinary messages as significant. The one version with a defensible mechanism is the reciprocal one, where the sigil commits you to changing your own availability, communication and willingness to be declined. That works, slowly, and by changing the conditions rather than the other person's mind.

Is it manipulative to cast one at a specific person?

By most ethical frameworks, including most frameworks in the occult literature, yes. It works by removing the other person's ability to choose, and the fact that your intention is benevolent does not change what the operation is. Ask yourself whether you would run it if the outcome were that they become miserable, and if the answer is no, the wish was conditional and conditional wishes are still imposed on someone.

What does a reconciliation working actually aim at?

At restoring contact with someone you previously had a relationship with, which is treated as lower risk than creating attraction because a bond already existed. It is still an influence operation and the older sources are consistent that repeating it escalates. The defensible version aims at your own conduct, your own honesty and your own acceptance of their answer, which includes the answer being no.

How long should I run one of these before deciding?

Six sessions over about a month, decided in advance and written down, is enough. If the aim is well-formed and directed at your own behaviour, you will see a difference inside that window. If it seems to need more, the aim is the problem and additional sessions will not fix it. The instruction that matters most is the stop date, because wanting more sessions is the clearest sign that the aim was never really about your conduct.

Does the ritual part matter at all, or is it the intention?

Reducing a statement to a glyph and formally committing to it appears to work mainly as a commitment device, and commitment devices are well supported in behavioural research. That is a modest but real effect, and it is enough to justify the practice without any claim about influence on other people. The elements people argue about most, blood, particular materials, particular timings, matter much less than the written aim and the fixed stop date.
