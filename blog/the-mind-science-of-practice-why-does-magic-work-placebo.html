Why Does Magic Work? The Placebo Question Answered Properly

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

The placebo objection is not stupid, and treating it as stupid is the single most common way practitioners lose an argument they should win. Placebo accounts a real part of what happens when you perform a ritual and then experience a change. What it cannot account for is the autonomic shift that happens during the ritual itself, before any outcome, and before any belief about the outcome has had time to form. Once you separate the ritual from the result you get a much cleaner picture. The ritual has measurable physiological effects that are well documented. The result is a separate question with a much worse evidence base, and the honest position is that most of the strong claims fail while some of the modest ones survive. This article walks through what the placebo account actually explains, where it breaks, and how to run your own practice so that the question of whether it works becomes answerable rather than rhetorical.

What the Placebo Account Gets Right

Expectation changes experience. This is not mysticism, it is a large and well-replicated body of findings covering pain, nausea, mood and even some endocrine responses. A person who expects a treatment to work reports more improvement than one who expects nothing, and the difference shows up in trials. If your practice produces a feeling of having received something, the placebo account predicts that you will feel better, and it is usually right.

It also explains the timing pattern that practitioners find so convincing. Results cluster in the days after the operation, they feel meaningful, and they decay. That is exactly the shape of an expectancy effect, and no amount of sincerity in the practitioner prevents the pattern from looking like one.

The part people hear as an accusation is usually an implication rather than a claim: that because a real mechanism has not been identified, the effect is imaginary. That is a non sequitur, and it is worth naming as one. We call a placebo effect real and effective while having no idea what mediates it, and the identical objection is rarely made in medicine.

Open-Label Studies That Broke the Simple Story

In an open-label placebo, participants are told plainly that they are receiving an inert pill, and many of them still show a measurable physiological response. That is a genuinely awkward result for the clean version of the placebo account, because it removes conscious belief from the chain and leaves the response standing.

No one has a satisfying mechanistic account of why that works, and it is not proof of anything supernatural. It is proof that the tidy story, in which belief in a treatment is the necessary ingredient, is too simple. Something about the ritual of receiving, taking and expecting a response appears to matter independently of the belief about what is inside the pill.

For practitioners the useful move is to stop arguing about which single ingredient is the active one. The ingredients are dosage, timing, gesture, attention and expectation, they are entangled in every tradition, and nobody has isolated them cleanly. Arguing about the isolated active ingredient is a twentieth-century habit that does not fit the evidence.

Ritual and the Autonomic Nervous System

This is the best-evidenced part of the whole question, and it is routinely left out of the argument because it is boring. Structured ritual reliably produces measurable autonomic change: lowered heart-rate variability, slower and more regular respiration, altered skin conductance. These are the standard markers of a shift out of stress reactivity.

The timing is what makes it relevant. The shift happens during the ritual, before any outcome exists, and it is strongest in rituals with fixed sequence, a defined beginning and end, and a sustained attention requirement. A practitioner who performs a rite of known duration has used a stress-regulation technique, whether or not they know the vocabulary for it.

There is solid evidence that structured ritual reduces anxiety, and the mechanism is mostly predictability combined with behavioural commitment rather than expectancy of a medical outcome. A sequence you have performed forty times is calming because the body has learned it, not because you believe in the candles. That is enough to be worth having, and it is enough to explain why ritual out-performs an equivalent amount of sitting quietly.

Expectation as Hypothesis Management

Predictive-processing accounts treat perception as the continuous management of hypotheses rather than as a passive intake of signal. On that account, the brain is not lying to you when expectation alters experience. It is doing what it does with an underspecified input, applying the best available prediction.

For a practitioner this has a practical consequence that is easy to miss. If perception is hypothesis management, then the way to see more clearly is to change the hypothesis, not to concentrate harder. Attention spent on a fixed expectation narrows it. Attention spent on a well-formed, falsifiable question broadens the hypothesis space and lets more actual information in.

That is an argument for writing down what you expect before an operation, and for choosing targets phrased so that you could be wrong. A question with an open outcome produces more usable information than one whose answer you have already decided, which is the same reason a musician practices scales they have already mastered less than they practise the passage that defeats them.

The Zener Deck and Why 20% Matters

The Zener deck has five symbols and a hit rate of 20% by pure chance. It is a beautifully designed instrument for the statistics of psychic claims, and almost every psychic research programme of the twentieth century eventually found its way to it.

The arithmetic is worth doing once, by hand. Over a thousand guesses, a random guesser scores about two hundred. Over a hundred thousand, about twenty thousand. A high-scoring run of a few hundred guesses is exactly what you would expect from luck, and it will feel like a result, and it usually does. A genuinely gifted performer shows a divergence that grows with sample size, and it shows up in prospective testing where the target has not been seen before.

The design has a second property that makes it useful pedagogically: the target can be sealed before the session. That single feature converts a joke about psychic claims into a rigorous experiment, and the programmes that used it honestly found mostly nothing. That is a genuinely useful data point for a practitioner, because the same five-symbol logic applies to any success rate someone offers you without a control condition.

  1. Five symbols, so chance performance is exactly one in five.
  2. A thousand random guesses score about two hundred hits.
  3. A short high-scoring run is the expected output of chance.
  4. Sealing the target before the session is what makes it an experiment.

Counting Failures Instead of Remembering Them

Nearly every claim of a working method is a claim about a remembered set of attempts, and memory is not a neutral ledger. Failures are harder to recall than successes, ambiguous outcomes get recategorised after the fact, and the flattering reading is always available. Nobody has to be lying for the record to be unreliable.

The fix is unglamorous. Count every attempt in a written log, including the ones you did badly enough that you would like to discount them, including the ones where nothing happened, including the ones where something happened and you cannot tell whether it mattered. This is the only mechanism anyone has found for separating a working practice from a lucky one, and it takes ten minutes a week.

Practitioners who do this consistently tend to reach an uncomfortable and useful conclusion: a small number of their techniques carry nearly all of their effect, and the technique they were most attached to does almost nothing. That finding is uncomfortable once and then permanently valuable, because it converts a belief system into a short list of methods worth repeating.

Writing Down a Prediction Before You Start

A prediction is falsifiable only if it is written before the fact and specific enough to fail. Both halves matter. Write the expected outcome, the way you would recognise it, the date by which you expect it, and what counts as no result. Then stop looking at it until the date arrives.

The deadline does more work than the prediction. A specific outcome with no deadline is a permanent maybe, and a permanent maybe is unfalsifiable by construction. Setting a date converts an open question into a closed one, and a closed question can be answered badly, which is the entire point.

For a beginner the practical version is small. Pick one technique, write down the kind of result you expect and the date thirty days out, log every attempt in between, and read your own notes on the day. Do this three times with the same technique before you decide anything about whether it works for you. Most people find that the method they thought was the weak one was simply never tested.

What Any Honest Practitioner Can Still Claim

Strip out the untestable claims and a defensible position remains. Ritual reliably produces measurable autonomic change, and structured sequences reduce anxiety through predictability and commitment. Expectation alters experience, which is a property of ordinary perception rather than a property of magic. A practitioner who keeps records can identify which of their own methods produce results for them.

What does not survive is the claim that a sigil moves money, that a spell changes another person's behaviour, or that a rite produces effects at a distance. Those claims have no supporting evidence and the burden of proof sits with the person making them, not with the sceptic.

Holding both positions at once is the whole point. You can use ritual as a genuine stress-regulation and decision-structuring technique, take the autonomic effect seriously, and hold the extraordinary claims loosely enough to be honest about them. Practitioners who insist on all-or-nothing positions end up defending claims they cannot support, and lose the arguments they were actually winning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is magic just placebo?

It explains part of the effect and misses the ritual component. Expectation does alter experience, and that accounts for a real share of what practitioners report. What it does not account for is the autonomic shift that occurs during the ritual itself, before any outcome exists and before any belief about that outcome has formed. Ritual works through predictability, sequence and behavioural commitment, which is a different mechanism from expecting a medical outcome.

How do I know whether a result I got is real?

Usually you cannot, from a single instance, and anyone who claims. Otherwise it is a sales pitch, and what you can actually do is pre-register a falsifiable prediction with a deadline, count every attempt including the failures and the sloppy ones, and read your own notes on the date rather than whenever the mood improves. Repeated across three cycles, that process reliably separates the one or two methods that work for you from the many that only feel like they do.

Does ritual help with anxiety?

There is reasonable evidence that it does, and the mechanism is not mysterious. A fixed sequence with a defined beginning and end, performed repeatedly, trains the body to move out of stress reactivity. That shows up as lower heart-rate variability and slower, more regular breathing, and it happens during the ritual rather than after it. This is why ritual outperforms an equal amount of quiet sitting: the structure is doing the work.

What do open-label placebo studies actually show?

That the clean version of the story is too simple. When participants are told plainly that their pill is inert, a substantial subset still show a measurable physiological response, which removes conscious belief from the causal chain and leaves the response standing. That is not proof of anything supernatural. It is evidence that the ritual of receiving and taking something has an effect apart from belief in its contents, and that the search for one isolated active ingredient is a habit that no longer fits the data.

Should I pay for training software or just keep a notebook?

The method is free and so is the discipline; the entire cost is attention and ten minutes a week of writing. A paid purchase buys the bookkeeping that most people fail at: a timestamped log that cannot be quietly rewritten after a bad week, a fixed schedule for the training you are actually doing, and a pre-registration slot for predictions so the deadline exists before you start wanting it to succeed. That is record-keeping, not a technique, and it is the part that decides whether you learn anything. Paying once suits something you plan to keep for years; a recurring charge would tax the very habit you are trying to build.
