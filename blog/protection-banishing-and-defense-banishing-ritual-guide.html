Protection, Banishing and Defence: What the Ritual Does

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

Banishing is the most misdescribed operation in Western esotericism. The popular version makes it sound like combat, and it has never been anything of the kind. The Lesser Banishing of Ritual, sitting in the Golden Dawn initiatory material and used as the default banishing in ceremonial practice since the nineteenth century, is a short piece of spoken language that declares a boundary. The language is the mechanism, not a decoration on one. The reason the same eleven lines appear in so many traditions is that they are phrased so that any intelligence, sympathetic or not, would recognise the declaration. What follows separates banishing from cleansing from grounding, explains the rising, and deals honestly with the question most people are actually asking when they search for a banishing guide.

Banishing Is Language, Not Combat

The Lesser Banishing of Ritual takes under a minute to speak and contains no combat imagery. It states who is present, names the space, names the authorities, declares the boundary, identifies the operator, and closes. Nothing is fought, defeated or destroyed.

The design assumption is worth taking seriously. The wording is drafted to be legible to any hostile intelligence, which means it works on a cooperative one too. If the operation depends on the target understanding the declaration, then a being that reads English has a far bigger problem than a ghost in the walls.

This is also why improvised banishing mostly does not work as banishing. A cry for help, however sincere, is a request. A boundary has to be stated in a form that could be quoted back to whoever is on the other side of it, and the traditional wording does that work for free.

Three Jobs That Get Confused

Banishing removes. Cleansing purifies. Grounding re-establishes your own footing. People frequently need all three and they are routinely collapsed into a single vague practice, which is why so many practitioners feel the ritual did not do the job they wanted.

Grounding is the one most often missing. After a disorienting session, a difficult dream or an hour of reading disturbing material, the useful operation is not to push something out. It is to come back into your own body, your own room and your own schedule, and to write down what happened.

Cleansing sits in between and is the vaguest of the three. People use it when something feels off and cannot say what. A sequence that purifies a space, a window and a person, with a stated intention, is a reasonable thing to do. A cleansing that is expected to remove an external condition is not testable, and the part that does work is the clean-slate effect it produces in your own behaviour.

Why a Rising Is Specified

Many traditions end the banishing with a physical movement toward the door while speaking the closing words. The function is obvious once you see it, and it applies whether or not anybody is in the room.

A boundary that is only declared has not yet been left. Rising gives the ritual an unambiguous exit, which converts a spoken intention into a change of position. The body ends up somewhere different from where it started, and that is the part the nervous system notices.

It also sets a hard ending. A ritual without a defined close tends to run on, and running on is the opposite of what a closing operation is for. The rise is the moment the work stops, stated in the only language you cannot argue with.

How Often, and After What

The traditional schedule is daily, at a fixed threshold time, usually at the end of a working session. Many contemporary practitioners use it only after a disturbing session, which is more sustainable and just as traditional, since nothing in the sources requires the daily version.

What matters is that it is not ad hoc. A banishing run the first time something feels wrong is a response to a feeling, and feelings about boundaries are not reliable evidence. A banishing run at the end of every session is a procedure, and it is much harder to talk yourself out of.

The escalation rule is worth stating. If you have started doing more protective work than actual work, the protective work has become the practice. The signal is not that something is wrong with your life. It is the ratio.

Am I Cursed

Usually you are not. Curses are self-diagnosed far more often than they are externally confirmed, and the reason is structural: a curse claim has to have a source, and the source is almost always another person whose intentions you cannot check.

The honest way to handle the question is to change the subject. If something has been wrong for weeks, it is worth examining sleep, work, money and relationships before assigning it a spiritual cause. Persistent anxiety, paranoia or intrusive thoughts are a mental health matter first and a magical one a distant second, and the order of those two clauses is not negotiable.

Banishing will still help. It reliably produces a sense of a clean slate, and a clean slate changes what you do next. What it will not do is explain a condition that has an ordinary and treatable explanation, and using it instead of addressing that leaves the actual problem in place.

Building Your Own Version

Editing a traditional banishing down is worthwhile and rewriting it from nothing usually is not. The declarations carry the structure, and the structure is the part that does the work. What you can safely add is specificity.

The written note in the last line is the piece most people skip. It moves the operation from a state you were in to a thing you did, which is the difference between an experience you can look up later and one you can only relive.

  1. Keep the traditional wording if you use it. The declarations are what make it legible as a boundary.
  2. Cut it to the version you will actually say daily. Brevity beats completeness when the choice is daily repetition.
  3. End with the rise. Move before the last line rather than after it, so the exit is part of the speech.
  4. Add one line of your own naming what is leaving. Specific beats general and takes five seconds to write.
  5. Pair it with grounding: food, water, daylight, and the written note of what you did.

Boundaries Outside the Ritual

A boundary that is only declared in a spoken formula is a claim. A boundary that shows up in your schedule is a fact, and the spoken version is at best the marker that you built the other one on purpose.

Deciding in advance that you will not answer messages after a certain hour, will not take a call you have been avoiding, and will not agree to something on the spot because saying no in the moment is hard, are all banishings. They are boring, they are effective, and they are what the ceremony is imitating.

Frequency and Cost

Every protective operation costs a little attention, and attention spent on boundaries is attention not spent on whatever the session was for. A practice that ends with twenty minutes of protection every day has quietly changed what the practice is.

Watch the ratio, and treat a rising count of protective work as a signal to reduce the practice rather than to increase the protection. The traditional form is brief by design, and a version that has grown to dominate the session has stopped being a closing and started being the main event.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if I am cursed?

You usually do not, because curses are self-diagnosed far more often than they are externally confirmed, and a curse claim needs a source, which is almost always somebody else's intentions. Change the subject instead. Look at sleep, work, money and relationships first, because those explain most persistent difficulties. Persistent anxiety, paranoia or intrusive thoughts are a matter for a professional, and the ordering of those two steps is not something to improvise on.

How often should I perform a banishing?

Traditionally daily at a fixed threshold time, at the end of a working session. Many modern practitioners run it only after a disturbing session, which is more sustainable and equally traditional, since nothing in the sources obliges the daily form. What to avoid is running it the first time something feels wrong, because that makes it a reaction to a feeling and feelings about boundaries are unreliable. If protective work starts outnumbering actual work, the ratio is the signal.

Does a cleansing ritual remove bad luck?

It reliably produces a sense of a clean slate, and that reliably changes behaviour, which is the part you can observe. Whether it removes a condition in the external world is not testable, so treat the behavioural change as the effect and the rest as framing. Grounding matters too, since what usually follows a difficult practice is not something in the room but a loss of footing in your own schedule, and no banishing addresses that.

Should I learn the Lesser Banishing or write my own?

Learn the traditional one first, because the declarations are what make the boundary legible and they are doing the work. Once you can say it without reading, cutting it to a version you will actually say every day is an improvement, since daily repetition is where the practice lives. What you can add safely is one specific line naming what is leaving. Rewriting it from nothing usually produces something with the shape of a banishing and none of the structure.

Is a paid app worth it for protection work?

The traditional text costs nothing and has not changed since the nineteenth century, so there is no case for renting access to it. The only real cost in protection practice is forgetting, and forgetting is what happens when a boundary, a date and an outcome live only in your head. If a purchase keeps a dated log of what you closed, when, and what changed afterwards, that is worth having, and it is the part I would actually pay for. Buying access to an old formula rather than owning somewhere to keep the record feels backwards to me.
