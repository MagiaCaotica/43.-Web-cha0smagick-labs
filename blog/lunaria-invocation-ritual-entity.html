LUNARIA

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

There is no established catalogue entry for LUNARIA, which means the name is yours to define before it is anything else. That is not a defect to work around. A name with a thin documentary trail gives you complete freedom over the office, and freedom over the office is the part that actually determines whether a working does anything. This article runs the full operational sequence for a first entity working, in the order you would actually perform it, and it is written for someone who has not done this before. The four ideas underneath it are standard and worth stating once each. Evocation means the spirit appears; invocation means its influence operates through the operator. Most modern usage collapses the distinction, and collapsing it is why first workings feel vaguer than they should. The Ars Goetia supplies rank, form, office and powers for each of seventy-two spirits, which makes it the most structured entity catalogue in Western grimoire literature and a reasonable place to borrow vocabulary from. Modern chaos practice treats entities as independent pattern fragments, and Carroll held they are aspects of the magician's own mind. The last of those is worth taking seriously: it dissolves the risk of the working without requiring scepticism.

A name you will have to define yourself

LUNARIA does not appear in the standard seventy-two, and a name outside the catalogue arrives without a job description. You will be inventing the office, and the invention is the work. Write the definition down before anything else, because the first definition you think of will be the one you inherit every time you return to it.

Three questions produce a usable office. What does it do, stated as a verb rather than an adjective. Who or what is it for, including the possibility that the answer is you. What would count as it having done that, phrased so you could recognise it without feeling guilty. If the third answer comes out as a feeling rather than an event, keep asking. Offices that cannot be recognised from outside are the ones that inflate.

Evocation and invocation, before anything else

Evocation asks for appearance: the spirit presents itself, traditionally in a visible form, and the practitioner sees or perceives something. Invocation asks for influence: the entity's power acts through the practitioner without necessarily appearing. Classical grimoires distinguish these carefully, because an evocation that goes badly is frightening and an invocation that goes badly is subtle.

For a first working, invocation is the better choice. It produces less to interpret, which means less room for self-flattery, and it does not ask you to hold a visual form in mind while also trying to judge what you are seeing. If you choose evocation anyway, decide in advance how long you will look and what you will write down afterwards, because the state produced by a strong evocation is exactly the state in which people report most and record least.

Reading an office without inflating it

An office is a description of a function, not a personality. That distinction is the whole defence against inflation, and inflation is the standard failure of entity practice: the practitioner comes to believe they are special to the entity, or the entity becomes a mood that validates whatever the practitioner already felt.

Write the office as a job description someone could hold. Then ask what the job requires of you, and write that down too. A workable entity office produces obligations, usually inconvenient ones, which is a better test than any amount of checking whether you feel chosen. If the job description generates no requirements, it is not an office yet.

Entities as pattern, and the licence that gives

Modern chaos practice treats an entity as a fragment of pattern rather than a being with a biography. On this view there is no entity to offend and no entity to deceive, and the practitioner is free to define, invoke, charge and retire a name as a matter of technique. Carroll's position, that entities are aspects of the magician's own mind, is the strongest version of the same claim.

This is a practical licence, not just a philosophical one. It means you can work with a name you invented without any ritual risk attached, and it means a working that does nothing has a clear diagnosis: the pattern was too vague to be noticed. That diagnosis is more useful than a failed evocation interpreted as a refusal, because it tells you what to change before you try again.

A first hour, in order

Ten minutes to write the office and the three questions' answers. Five minutes to clear the space and remove anything you would not want in the room during the working, which in practice means electronics and a second screen. Three minutes to set the intention in one sentence. Then the working itself, for a fixed twenty minutes, timed. Five minutes of notes before you get up.

The order matters more than the content. Clearing the space before setting the intention prevents you from changing the intention once you have seen what the space contains. Timing the working prevents the common failure of extending it while something interesting is happening, which turns a measurement into a highlight reel. Notes come last because anything you write during the working is narration, not record.

Narrow offices and the ones to refuse

A narrow office is one that can be described in a sentence and checked against a list. Answers to a small number of questions, on a schedule, for a person who is you, are narrow. Anything asking you to change someone else, to obtain something you have no legitimate route to, or to keep a state going indefinitely is too wide to audit.

Refuse a specific set of things rather than trusting your judgement in the moment. Anything aimed at a person who has not asked. Anything requiring sustained daily attention beyond what you can realistically sustain for a year. Anything that asks for secrecy from people close to you. These three cover most of the failure cases, and writing them down before the charge is what stops you from renegotiating at 2am when the state is good and the judgement is not.

The charge, and the paperwork kept

The charge is short. Read the office sentence aloud or silently, five times for a short charge, and stop. What makes it a charge rather than an intention is the repetition with a fixed count and an agreed end. There is no technique here that other practitioners do not know, and the absence of mystery is deliberate: the sigil is a compression of the office, the charging is attention applied to it, and the forgetting is what keeps it from having to be defended.

The paperwork is the part people skip. Keep a single page with the name, the office sentence, the date of the charge, the count used, and one line on what you expect to try. That page is what makes the working auditable later, and it is also what stops you from telling a story after the fact about what you were actually doing.

Month two, when nothing happens

Nothing visible happening is the most common outcome and it is not evidence of failure. The pattern-fragment view predicts this precisely: a pattern that is too generic does not get noticed, and a noticed-but-unattributed change does not accumulate into anything you would call a result.

At the end of the second month, read your own page back and do one of three things. Narrow the office, keeping the name. Retire it and write down why. Or extend the trial, which is only defensible if the office is already narrow and you have been inconsistent rather than unsuccessful. Choosing from a page of notes is much harder to talk yourself out of than choosing from memory, which is the entire reason the page exists.

Instruments, and which ones are decoration

You need a surface to write on and something to write with. A pentacle drawn on paper works, a candle is optional, a specific moon phase is a preference rather than a requirement, and a talisman carried afterwards does nothing that the notebook does not do better.

The instruments that earn their place are the ones that change what you record. A timer, because it converts a session into a measurable length. A separate notebook, because a working written in the same page as your day is read as part of the day. And a ritual-safety checklist, which is not a charm but a two-minute inventory of what is in the room, what you are taking, and who you have told you are doing this.

A record you could hand to somebody else

Test the record the way you would test a research claim: could another person look at it and reach the same conclusion you would. That means dates, counts, stated intentions before the fact, and a place for the things that did not work. It also means no editing after the fact, which is easier if you write notes the same evening rather than reconstructing later.

Keep the log boring. A table with a date, a count, a one-line description of anything notable, and a yes or no on whether the working felt engaged is enough. Anything more elaborate will attract the parts of your attention that enjoy producing records, and the writing will outrun the practice without you noticing that the practice has stopped.

The consent line you do not cross

Entity practice is a relationship-shaped activity conducted with a name rather than a person, and that asymmetry is easy to forget. Someone who has not asked you to work on them has not consented, and neither has the people around them who will notice a change in your behaviour without understanding where it came from.

The line is simple: your own attention, your own schedule, your own habits, and your own creative work are in scope. Other people's decisions, other people's relationships, and anything that depends on their not noticing are out. A working that requires concealment from someone close to you is a working you should retire rather than refine, and the reason is not ethics in the abstract but that concealment is itself evidence the working is doing something you would not sign.

Closing a working you will not finish

Most entity workings end without a climax, and closing one properly is a skill of its own. A dismissal, stated once and in the past tense, followed by a written note naming what you were hoping for and what you got, is enough. Ritual closure is useful because it is external and takes ten seconds, and internal closure is useful because the writing is the part that stops the loop.

Then put the notebook somewhere you will not see it for a month. Entity workings that fail tend to be recharged rather than reassessed, and the reason is that recharging is easier than deciding. If a month passes and you want to run the same name again with a narrower office, that is a new working with the same name, and the paperwork for the first one should be complete before you start.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a formal Goetia entry to work with a name like LUNARIA?

No. LUNARIA is not in the standard catalogue, which means you define its office yourself, and that freedom is the point. The Goetia is useful as a source of vocabulary and structure rather than as a permission list. Define the office in a sentence, decide what would count as it having functioned, and refuse anything that cannot be checked.

Should a first working be an evocation or an invocation?

Invocation, for most people starting out. It produces less to interpret, which means less opportunity for self-flattery, and it does not require holding a visual form in mind while judging what you are perceiving. Evocation is available when you have a stable record-keeping habit, because the state it produces is one in which people report far more than they record.

What if the working produces nothing at all?

At the end of two months, read your written page and choose between three options: narrow the office, retire the name, or extend the trial. Extending is only defensible if the office was already narrow and you were inconsistent rather than unsuccessful. A pattern that is too generic does not get noticed, which is the most likely cause and the easiest to fix.

How long should each session be?

A fixed twenty minutes for the working itself, preceded by about eighteen minutes of writing, clearing and setting, and followed by five minutes of notes. The fixed length is the important part, because extending a session while something interesting is happening converts a measurement into a highlight reel. Consistency across many sessions is worth considerably more than length in any single one.

Is it safe to work with a name I invented?

Under the pattern-fragment view that most modern practice uses, yes, which is one reason that view is useful. There is no entity to offend and nothing to deceive, so a self-defined name carries no ritual risk. What it does carry is the ordinary risk of a vague aim, which is why the office needs to be written as a sentence with a recognisable success condition.
