Community Blog | President Vine of Goetia: King of the West

Introduction

President Vine is the second of the four Goetian kings, presiding over the western cabinet. He appears in the Ars Goetia as the 45th spirit, a King who rides a black horse with two heads—one equine, one leonine—while clutching a viper in his hand. The imagery is deliberate: the lion-head speaks to solar authority and raw power, the horse to swift movement across boundaries, the viper to venomous knowledge that kills ignorance. In my own work, Vine has proven less a "demon" in the medieval sense and more a specific cognitive-energetic current that excels at three things: cutting through deception, commanding water and storm, and revealing what someone has gone to great lengths to hide.

The Lesser Key of Solomon describes him as "a King and a President," which signals dual cabinet membership—he sits in both the royal and presidential ranks. Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1583) lists him as "Vine, a Great King and President," noting his 36 legions and his office of discovering hidden things, witches, and things past, present, and to come. Reginald Scot in The Discoverie of Witchcraft (1584) translates the entry nearly verbatim, which tells us the core attributes were stable across the early manuscript tradition. The consistency matters: when a spirit's description survives Weyer, Scot, and the multiple Goetia manuscripts (Sloane, Harley, Lansdowne) with minimal drift, you're looking at a well-defined operative current, not a scribal invention.

Why does Vine matter for a chaos magick practitioner in 2026? Because the problems he solves—finding lost objects, exposing deception, commanding weather, navigating the liminal space between salt water and fresh—are exactly the problems that resist purely psychological approaches. You can journal your way to insight, but you cannot journal your way into a storm breaking at the right moment, or a thief confessing because the pressure became unbearable. Vine operates at the intersection of information and energy, which is precisely where chaos magick's "meta-belief" technology shines.

Vine's Rank and Authority

As a King, Vine commands 36 legions and serves as a powerful ally in uncovering hidden truths. But "King" in the Goetia isn't a feudal title—it's a functional classification. The four Kings (Bael, Paimon, Beleth, Vine/Balam depending on manuscript) each govern a cardinal direction and a cabinet of spirits. Vine holds the West, the quarter of water, twilight, endings, and the emotional unconscious. His presidential rank means he also administers a specific bureaucratic function: the revelation of secrets. This dual-hat arrangement is unique; most Goetian spirits are either King or President, not both. The implication for practice is that Vine can be approached either as a sovereign (formal evocation, full ceremonial container) or as an office-holder (petition, sigil gaze, dream incubation), and the results differ in texture.

In the Solomonic hierarchy, the 36 legions under Vine break down into approximately 3,600 individual spirits (assuming the standard 100 per legion). Each legion likely corresponds to a specific domain of hidden knowledge: lost maritime cargo, buried treasure, family secrets, corporate fraud, magical deception, and so on. When I've worked with Vine for treasure-hunting operations—both literal and metaphorical—the legion structure manifests as specialized "teams" that present themselves in trance. One legion handles salt-water workings; another handles the viper's venom (poisonous truth-telling); a third manages the two-headed horse (rapid transit between worlds). You don't command 3,600 spirits individually; you address the King, and he deploys the relevant legion.

The western cabinet association brings specific elemental correspondences. West equals water, autumn, sunset, the cup in tarot, the suit of chalices. Vine's storms are not generic—they are salt-water storms, the kind that rise off the ocean and rewrite coastlines. This connects to his historical reputation among sailors and coastal communities. In 17th-century Basque and Cornish folklore, spirits resembling Vine were petitioned before fishing voyages and to recover wrecked cargo. The "hidden things" he reveals often have a maritime flavor: things lost at sea, things washed ashore, things the tide refuses to keep. If your working involves freshwater (lakes, rivers), Vine still operates but the texture shifts—less storm, more current; less violent revelation, more patient uncovering.

His Sigil and Symbolism

Vine's sigil depicts a lion's head with a snake's tail, symbolizing the duality of revelation and concealment. The actual seal from the Goetia manuscripts shows a more complex figure: a composite beast, lion-headed, serpent-tailed, often with the two-headed horse implied in the line work. The viper in his hand is sometimes rendered as a staff topped with a snake, sometimes as a live serpent coiled around his fist. The lion head represents the solar intellect—clear, hot, authoritative. The viper represents chthonic knowledge—cold, venomous, striking from concealment. The two-headed horse (equine and leonine) represents transport that crosses categories: land and spirit, known and unknown, waking and dreaming.

In practice, the sigil functions as a tuning fork. I've found that drawing it by hand—rather than printing or projecting it—activates a stronger link. The act of tracing the lion's mane, the horse's dual necks, the viper's coils, creates a somatic memory that the trance state can access faster than visual recognition alone. The traditional color for Vine's seal is black on white (or gold on black for the royal aspect), but chaos magick frees us to experiment. I've had success with a version rendered in sea-green and rust-red: the green for the salt-water domain, the rust for the viper's blood-knowledge. The sigil also responds well to being etched into copper or lead—metals associated with Venus and Saturn respectively, bridging the "hidden treasure" (Venus) and "boundary enforcement" (Saturn) aspects of his office.

The symbolism extends to his enn (vocalization). The traditional enn "Kaymen vefa Vine" or "Vine kaymen vefa" varies by manuscript. I use a simplified vibration: "VINE-AH-VEH-FAH" on a low D, sustained for three breaths, then whispered. The vibration should feel like it originates in the solar plexus (lion) and terminates in the throat (viper's strike). Some practitioners add a gesture: right hand forming a viper's head (index and middle finger extended, thumb tucked), left hand a lion's claw (fingers curled). This mudra, held during the enn, creates a physical anchor for the visualization of his mounted form.

Powers and Abilities

Vine reveals hidden things, uncovers witches and sorcerers, and transports souls between worlds. The Goetia lists three core powers: (1) discovering things hidden, lost, or secret; (2) revealing witches, sorcerers, and those practicing magic in secret; (3) building towers, overthrowing stone walls, and making waters rough or calm. The third power is the most physically dramatic—storm magic and structural manipulation—and the most overlooked in modern practice. Historical grimoires emphasize Vine's control over "rough waters" and "tempests," which aligns with his western/watery cabinet. He doesn't just "know" where the treasure is; he can clear the path to it by calming seas or, conversely, raise a storm to conceal an operation or destroy a rival's work.

The "uncovering witches" power has a specific historical valence. In 1583–1584, when Weyer and Scot wrote, "witch" meant someone practicing maleficium—harmful magic—often in secret. Vine's function was judicial: he exposed the hidden practitioner so justice (or persecution) could follow. In modern chaos magick terms, this translates to counter-magic detection. If you suspect someone is working against you—whether through psychic attack, sigil interference, or mundane sabotage—Vine is the specialist for identifying the source. I've used this in two contexts: corporate competitive intelligence (finding the leak) and magical hygiene (identifying a parasitic construct). The method is consistent: open Vine's sigil, state the query "Show me the hidden hand," and record dreams, synchronicities, and intrusive thoughts over 72 hours. The answer rarely comes as a name; it comes as a pattern—a repeated symbol, a specific location, a time stamp—that correlates with the suspected activity.

"Transports souls between worlds" is the most esoteric of his listed powers. The medieval interpretation was necromantic: Vine could fetch the dead for interrogation. The chaos magick interpretation is broader: he moves information across the boundary between consensus reality and the subcognitive realms. This includes past-life data, ancestral memory, and the "akashic" record of a location. In a 2021 working for a client trying to locate a family burial site lost since the 1930s, Vine provided not coordinates but a sequence of sensory impressions: the smell of pine resin, the sound of a train whistle at 3 AM, the texture of red clay. Cross-referenced with historical railway maps, this pinpointed the cemetery within 200 meters. The "soul transport" was the information crossing the veil, not a literal ghost ride.

One power the Goetia mentions only in passing but practitioners consistently report: Vine teaches the "secret arts" to those who earn his trust. This isn't spoon-feeding; it's initiatory. He reveals techniques for weather-working, for finding lost objects through dowsing-augmented trance, for constructing "viper wards" that poison hostile magic on contact. The price is always the same: you must be willing to have your own secrets exposed first. Vine does not work for hypocrites. If you hide your motives from yourself, he will amplify that self-deception until the working backfires. I've seen this happen—practitioners who petitioned Vine for "hidden treasure" while secretly motivated by greed received exactly what they asked for: the treasure remained hidden, and their greed was exposed to everyone around them.

The Two-Headed Horse and the Viper

The iconography of Vine's mount—a black horse with two heads, one horse, one lion—is unique in the Goetia. No other spirit rides a bicephalic beast. The horse head governs speed, endurance, and the physical plane; the lion head governs authority, ferocity, and the solar plane. Together, they represent transport that does not merely move from A to B but transforms the nature of the journey. When you ride Vine's horse in trance, the transition between worlds is not a fade-to-black; it's a violent shift, like crossing a storm front. The temperature drops, the air pressure changes, and you arrive changed. This mirrors the phenomenology of actual storm magic: the practitioner who calls a storm does not control it from safety; they stand in the wind and become the lightning rod.

The viper in Vine's hand is not decorative. In Mediterranean magical tradition, the viper (specifically Vipera aspis or Vipera ammodytes) carries the "evil eye" reversal—its venom, properly prepared, becomes the antidote to poisoning. Vine's viper represents venomous truth: knowledge that kills the lie it strikes. In practical work, this manifests as the "Viper Ward"—a sigilized construct charged with Vine's current that sits on a property or person and activates only when hostile magic crosses its threshold. The ward doesn't reflect or deflect; it poisons the source, sending back a concentrated dose of the attacker's own energy, amplified. I've deployed these for clients dealing with sustained psychic harassment; the pattern is consistent: the attacks stop within a week, and the harasser experiences a sudden, inexplicable run of bad luck—mechanical failures, financial errors, social humiliation. The viper strikes where the lion cannot reach.

The two-headed horse also encodes a temporal duality. The horse head faces the present (the immediate journey); the lion head faces the past (the ancestral power behind the journey). In past-life or ancestral workings, Vine's mount serves as a bridge: the horse carries you to the memory, the lion ensures you survive the encounter. I've used this in regression work where the client needed to confront a traumatic ancestral pattern—the horse got them there fast, the lion prevented dissolution. The viper, in this context, extracts the venom from the memory so it can be transmuted. This three-part mechanism (horse = access, lion = survival, viper = transmutation) is a complete therapeutic protocol disguised as demonology.

Storm and Water Workings: What Practitioners Report

Vine's dominion over "rough waters" and tempests is his most physically verifiable power. In the chaos magick community, storm workings with Vine fall into two categories: invocation (calling the storm) and evocation (directing the storm). The invocation method is simpler: open the sigil, charge with the intent "Storm come / Storm break / Storm serve," and release into a body of water—ocean, lake, even a bathtub if that's what you have. The storm manifests within 6–48 hours, typically as a localized squall rather than a regional event. I've logged 17 such workings since 2019; 14 produced measurable precipitation or wind shifts within the predicted window. The three failures correlated with the practitioner's internal resistance—fear of the storm's power, or unconscious desire for the storm not to work as proof of control.

Evocation is more structured. The classical method: stand at the water's edge at sunset (Vine's hour), face west, hold a piece of driftwood or sea-worn stone, vibrate the enn 36 times (one per legion), and speak the command. The command must be specific: "Raise wind from the southwest at 15 knots by midnight" works; "Make it stormy" produces chaotic, often counterproductive results. The driftwood/stone serves as a physical anchor—the storm's energy binds to it, and you can later "discharge" the working by returning the object to deep water. One practitioner I know uses a lead weight inscribed with Vine's sigil; he casts it from his boat, works the storm, then retrieves the weight. The lead takes the residual charge, preventing the storm from following him home.

Salt water versus fresh water matters. Vine's native element is salt water—the ocean, the tide, the brine. Workings performed at the coast carry an authority that inland workings lack. If you're landlocked, you can simulate the connection by adding sea salt to your working water, or by using a vial of actual ocean water as a "witness." The difference is palpable: coastal workings feel like commanding a general; inland workings feel like phoning a general's aide. Both work, but the latency and precision differ. For treasure-hunting (Vine's other maritime specialty), the salt-water connection is non-negotiable. He finds things lost in the sea or to the sea. Lake wrecks, river gold, swamp caches—these fall under other spirits (Focalor for lakes, Vepar for seas generally). Vine is specifically the King of the West, the sunset coast, the shore where the tide deposits secrets.

Vine in the Ars Goetia vs. Modern Practice

The textual Vine—Weyer's "Great King and President," Scot's "King Vine," the Lemegeton's "Vine, a King"—is a stable but skeletal figure. The manuscripts give us name, rank, legions, appearance, and three powers. They do not give us methodology, safety protocols, or the nuance of how he reveals hidden things. Modern practice fills these gaps through shared gnosis (UPG—Unverified Personal Gnosis that converges across practitioners). The consensus from the last two decades of chaos magick forums, private journals, and my own logs: Vine is not a "beginner spirit." He demands clarity of intent, tolerates no vague petitions, and enforces a strict reciprocity—you get the secret, but you must surrender a secret of your own.

One major divergence: the grimoires frame Vine as a servant of the magician (bound by Solomon's seal). Modern chaos magick frames him as a partner (bound by mutual interest). This shift changes the operational posture. In the Solomonic model, you threaten and constrain; in the partnership model, you negotiate and align. I've tested both. The Solomonic approach yields faster initial contact but brittle results—the working holds only as long as the ceremonial container holds. The partnership approach takes longer to establish (3–7 sessions of sigil gazing, dream incubation, and small favors exchanged) but produces durable, self-sustaining effects. A Vine partnership established in 2018 is still active in my practice; the Solomonic evocations from 2015 produced results that decayed within months.

Another divergence: the grimoires emphasize Vine's role in exposing witches. Modern practice has expanded this to "exposing any hidden agency"—corporate espionage, government surveillance, algorithmic manipulation, parasitic entities, self-deception. The mechanism is the same (viper's strike, lion's roar, horse's speed), but the targets have evolved. Vine doesn't care about the category; he cares about the concealment. Anything deliberately hidden from you—whether by a rival, a system, or your own shadow—falls under his office. This makes him uniquely valuable in the information-saturated 2020s, where the problem isn't scarcity of data but the deliberate burial of signal beneath noise.

The 36 Legions

Vine commands 36 legions of spirits, each with specific domains related to secrets and hidden knowledge. The number 36 is not arbitrary—it's 6×6, the square of the solar number, suggesting complete solar manifestation across the six directions (four cardinals + zenith + nadir). In the Ars Paulina and related hour-based angelologies, 36 also corresponds to the 36 decans of the zodiac, each ruling 10 degrees. Vine's legions may map to these decanic divisions, each specialized for a specific type of hidden knowledge within a specific astrological window. I've experimented with timing Vine workings to the decan rulers (using the Chaldean order), and the correlation is strong: workings for financial secrets succeed in the decans of Venus/Jupiter; workings for magical secrets succeed in Mercury/Saturn decans; storm workings succeed in Mars decans.

In trance, the legions present as distinct "voices" or "currents" rather than individual entities. Legion 1–6 (the solar hexad) handle revelation of cosmic law and divine will. Legion 7–12 (the lunar hexad) handle emotional secrets, family mysteries, and dream-content. Legion 13–18 (the martial hexad) handle conflict, enemy plans, and protective counter-measures. Legion 19–24 (the mercurial hexad) handle communication intercepts, contractual deception, and technological secrets. Legion 25–30 (the jovial hexad) handle treasure, abundance, and hidden resources. Legion 31–36 (the saturnine hexad) handle boundaries, endings, karmic debts, and the secrets of the dead. This mapping is my own working model, derived from 40+ trance sessions; other practitioners report similar but not identical schemas. The point is not that this mapping is "true" but that it's functional—it lets you address the relevant legion directly, reducing latency and increasing precision.

Practical tip: you don't need to evoke all 36 legions. You address Vine as King, specify the domain (e.g., "Legion of the Jovial Hexad—reveal the hidden asset"), and he delegates. The exception is when you're building a long-term construct—a servitor, a ward, a standing storm-anchor. Then you may petition a specific legion to "assign a detachment" to the construct. I have a standing arrangement with Legion 27 (jovial, treasure-finding) to monitor a specific geographic area for "newly deposited value"—lost jewelry, buried caches, abandoned property. The arrangement is renewed quarterly with an offering of rum, copper coins, and a written report of findings. It works like a magical subscription service.

Working with Vine: A Chaos Magick Protocol

For practitioners wanting to engage Vine without the full Solomonic apparatus, here's a stripped-down protocol I've refined over six years. Phase 1: Research. Read the Goetia entries (Weyer, Scot, Lemegeton, Mathers/Crowley). Draw the sigil by hand 9 times. Memorize the enn. Phase 2: Contact. For 7 consecutive nights, gaze at the sigil for 10 minutes before sleep. Record dreams. No petition yet—just introduction. Phase 3: Test. On the 8th day, make a small, verifiable request: "Show me something I've lost in this house." Give a 48-hour window. If the object appears (or the memory of its location surfaces), the link is live. Phase 4: Contract. Write a clear agreement: what you offer (weekly sigil gaze, monthly offering of salt water and copper, public acknowledgment of his aid), what you request (specific domains of revelation, storm access, treasure finding). Sign in blood or semen or ink—your choice, but it must cost you something. Phase 5: Maintain. Weekly check-in. Quarterly renewal. Annual review.

The offerings matter. Vine accepts: sea water (collected at high tide), copper (coins, wire, filings), rum (dark, aged), red meat (raw), and written secrets (your own, burned after offering). He rejects: gold (too solar, belongs to Bael/Paimon), frankincense (too ecclesiastical), vague "energy" (lazy). The salt water is non-negotiable for any working involving his water/storm powers. For pure information-revelation workings, copper and a written secret suffice. The written secret is the key reciprocity mechanism: you write a genuine secret you've never told anyone, fold it toward you, burn it in his candle flame. This "feeds" the viper. Practitioners who skip this step find their workings go silent after 2–3 successes. The viper demands venom.

Safety considerations. Vine is not "safe" in the New Age sense. He will expose your secrets if you use him to expose others'. He will raise storms that damage your property if your command was imprecise. He will lead you to treasure that carries a curse if you didn't specify "clean treasure." The chaos magick advantage is that we can build in fail-safes: a "kill switch" servitor that severs the link if predefined boundaries are crossed; a "truth filter" construct that screens Vine's revelations for actionable relevance vs. destructive noise; a "grounding anchor" (a piece of hematite or lodestone worn during workings) that prevents possession-type phenomena. I use all three. The kill switch has triggered twice in six years—both times when I petitioned Vine while in a manic state and the request was genuinely dangerous. The link severed cleanly; no backlash.
