Occult Culture and Modern Practice

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Finding your way into contemporary occult practice is now mostly a question of sequence rather than of initiation. There is no gate, no waiting list, and a great deal of material of wildly uneven quality available within seconds of deciding to look. That is a genuine improvement on the situation thirty years ago, and it is also the reason that so many people end up with a shelf of books and no practice. This page sets out a workable order of operations for someone starting now, from finding the small amount of material worth reading through to building a routine that survives a bad month. It is written for a person with no contacts, no teacher and no money to spend, and it assumes you have access to a search engine and roughly an hour a week. The order matters more than any individual recommendation in it.

Step one, work out what you are actually looking for

Most people arrive at this material through a specific problem rather than an interest in the whole field. Something did not happen, something happened that should not have, or a set of questions became urgent. Naming the problem precisely changes what you should read and stops you from assembling a shelf of books that collectively answer a question you do not have.

Write it as a single sentence with a verb in it. The sentence will not be grammatical and should not be, because what you want is a description of a situation rather than a statement of theory. Keep that sentence somewhere you will see it, because within a month you will have begun reading things that appear to answer a different question, and the sentence is the only reliable check on whether they do.

Step two, read two primary texts rather than ten commentaries

The single most common mistake in self-directed study is starting with interpretation. Commentaries are written by people who already have a position, and after four of them you have a strong position and no idea what the underlying material says. Two short primary texts will take less time than one commentary and will hold up better.

The obvious pairing for someone starting in this field is the two foundational short works that argue for experimentation rather than doctrine. Both are inexpensive, both are available in translation, and both are written in a tone that assumes you will do the work yourself. Read them with a notebook, disagree where you disagree, and resist the urge to read further until you can act on one of them.

Step three, learn the history badly on purpose

Not knowing the history produces specific, predictable errors, and they are worth avoiding even at a crude level. Most confusion in this field comes from reading a modern practice as though it were ancient, or an ancient source as though it described a modern one. A few hours with one honest history book removes most of them at a stroke.

Deliberately badly means accepting a simplified account. The simplification is the point. You are not trying to become an expert, you are trying to stop making arguments that can be refuted in a paragraph by anyone who knows the subject. Once you can tell roughly where a claim comes from, you can start deciding which claims are worth taking seriously on their own merits.

Step four, get one thing working before you gather tools

The temptation with this material is to assemble a system first: tools, journals, candles, decks, software, a wall chart. Each acquisition feels like progress and none of them are. The correct order is the reverse: get one small operation working, notice what it actually requires, and then acquire only what that operation demanded.

In practice this means the shortest possible version of something. One target, one hour, one record. If the record does not happen the practice has not started, and no amount of equipment substitutes for it. Everything else in this field, including the applications, is an accessory to the record, and acquiring accessories before the record exists is the standard reason people abandon a practice they never actually began.

  1. Write the sentence describing your situation, and keep it visible for the first month
  2. Read the two short foundational primary texts and take notes, disagreeing in writing
  3. Spend an afternoon in one honest history, accepting the crude version of it
  4. Run one small operation end to end and write the record the same day you did it

Step five, decide what you are willing to be public about

Obscurity used to be a genuine protection, because the material circulated quietly and the consequences of being visible were real. That has changed. The audience for this material is now overwhelmingly online, and being public carries a very different set of risks, mostly social rather than material. The main one is that visibility attracts confident people with no practice and something to sell.

The workable position is to be public about reading and private about working. There is no reason to conceal that you are interested in this material, and considerable practical value in the honesty, because it is the main defence against being drawn into somebody else's drama. Keep the details of your own practice to yourself, not out of secrecy but because nobody has any business in it.

Step six, treat a teacher as a skill rather than a destination

The structural risk in this field is not the charlatan, who is easy to identify. It is a single figure who becomes the centre of a practice for a decade. This happens gradually, feels like depth rather than dependency, and is almost invisible from the inside. A person can lose years this way and only notice when the figure stops publishing and the entire architecture they built round one voice turns out to have no other support.

A teacher is genuinely valuable for particular things: a physical skill, a diagnostic judgement, a correction they have earned the right to give. None of those require you to reorganise your life around one person. Look for somebody who teaches a specific thing, takes payment for a specific thing, and does not ask you to treat them as the centre of your practice. Those three conditions exclude most of the failure cases at once.

Step seven, build a practice that survives contact with a bad month

Every practice in this field fails the same way, which is not that it is wrong but that it requires a state of mind that is not reliably available. Enthusiasm is a resource, and it depletes. Any structure that depends on feeling ready will stop, and the version of you who stops will be embarrassed and will not restart.

Design for the worst week rather than the best. Decide in advance what the minimum is, put it at a level you would do on a day when nothing is calling you, and make that the actual target. Fifteen minutes of note-taking on a bad week sustains the habit; forty minutes of ceremony does not, and skipping it because the full version is impossible is how people conclude the practice does not work for them.

Where the subcultures have always been the real laboratory

Informal scenes have been a consistent site of improvised magical practice since the late nineteen-seventies, and the continuity is not accidental. They supply something orders and traditions structurally cannot: a peer group with no hierarchy, in which methods get invented, tried and discarded in public. Most of what survives in the current literature was tried by somebody in a rehearsal space before it was written up.

The mechanism is worth understanding even if you never attend anything, because it explains why certain practices feel alive and others feel inherited. A practice that has been through a scene has been stress-tested by people with no stake in defending it. A practice that has only been repeated is untested in the only sense that matters, regardless of how old it is.

How shared symbols actually spread

Memes in this field are not jokes. A shared image or archetype functions as a genuinely common reference object, and its spread follows patterns that are well documented in cultural research: repetition with slight variation, context collapse, and a slow shift in meaning that nobody votes on. The result is a symbol that means something specific to a community and something different to an outsider.

This is worth knowing because it cuts both ways. It means an archetype that resonates with you may carry a great deal of accumulated meaning that you did not choose, and it means the version you find online is a late copy of something that was different when it started. Neither of these makes the symbol useless. It does make it worth tracing, if you find yourself responding to one more strongly than you would expect.

What to do when nothing is working

This is the question every beginner eventually needs, and the answer is unglamorous. Go back to the record rather than the enthusiasm. Look at the last twenty entries, count how many were acted on, and ask whether the pattern you are complaining about is a pattern in the world or a pattern in the practice. Usually it is the second, and it is nearly always traceable to targets that were too vague to record or to a ritual that had become more elaborate than the action it was supposed to support.

The second-order response is to reduce. Cut the practice back to the shortest version that still counts, run it for a fortnight, and see whether it regains traction. Traditions in this field have done this repeatedly and it works repeatedly, which suggests the elaborate versions are mostly maintaining something other than the effect they are claimed to produce.

A first-year sequence, in the order that works

This is the whole plan compressed into a year. It is deliberately light in the early months and heavier later, because the early months are for finding out whether the practice suits you and the later ones are for getting something out of it. Most people who quit do so in the first six weeks, and the structure below is built to survive that period rather than to impress.

Nothing in it requires a purchase, an initiation, a group or a particular belief. If you complete the year and want to do more, the second year has obvious candidates. If you complete it and do not, you have lost a small amount of time and avoided the much larger risk of spending years on a practice you never actually tested.

  1. Months one and two: two primary texts, one history, one written sentence, no workings at all
  2. Months three and four: one small operation a week with a same-day record, kept deliberately plain
  3. Months five and six: the record only, then a second operation in an unfamiliar area for comparison
  4. Months seven to nine: three workings a week, one short reading thread, and a monthly review of misses
  5. Months ten to twelve: a defined project, a fixed ending date, and a written account of what you would tell a beginner

Frequently Asked Questions

What books are worth reading first?

Two short primary texts that argue for experimentation over doctrine, followed by one honest history of the field, in that order and no more. Resist the urge to collect commentary, because a shelf of interpretations of works you have not read produces the feeling of knowledge without the substance of it. Everything else can wait six months, by which point you will know which of the many available directions actually interests you, and you will be able to tell the difference between a source worth your time and one that is simply long.

Is it ok to be public about it?

Yes, and there is a practical advantage to it, provided you separate reading from working. Saying publicly that you read about this material costs nothing and makes you markedly harder to recruit into somebody's private system. Keeping your actual practice to yourself is sensible, not because secrecy confers power but because your workings are yours and nobody in the comments needs access to them. The old advice to stay hidden protected people from consequences that no longer exist while costing them the correction that conversation would have provided.

Should I follow a single teacher?

Follow somebody for a specific skill, not as a centre of your practice. The conditions worth insisting on are that they teach one thing, charge for it, and do not require you to reorganise your life around them. The failure case is not the obvious fraud, it is a sincere and skilled person whose methods quietly become the only ones you can access, which takes years to notice and is expensive by then. Read widely enough that you could learn the same thing elsewhere, and your practice is considerably harder to capture.

Would a paid tracker make this practice better or just busier?

Only if the tracking is the part you skip. The record is what converts a series of impressions into something you can learn from, and a tool is a way of keeping a record, not a substitute for one. What a paid product reliably adds is persistence through bad weeks and a dated history stored somewhere other than your memory, which is exactly the thing a self-directed practitioner loses. Write it by hand if you will actually keep doing it, and you have most of what is on offer. Purchase nothing until a hand-written record has failed twice for the same reason.

How do I know when to stop a practice?

Stop when the record has stopped being interesting rather than when it stops being encouraging, because the interesting period is the one that produces learning and it reliably comes first. Two signals are worth taking seriously: you have started doing the practice while resenting it, or you have started doing it to manage an emotional state that the practice is now also worsening. Neither is a failure of will, and both are reversible by reducing frequency or stopping cleanly. Ending a practice deliberately, with a written note about what you learned, is better practice than drifting out of it.
