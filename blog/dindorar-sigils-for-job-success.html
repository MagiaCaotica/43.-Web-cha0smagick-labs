DINDORAR

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

A career is a bad target for a sigil in its general form and an excellent one in a particular form, and the difference is structural. Professional outcomes are mostly decided by other people, by markets, and by conditions nobody in the room controls, which rules out the vague aims entirely. But every career also contains a set of decisions that are genuinely yours, made repeatedly, currently going unmade, and measurable. Attaching the practice to that set turns an unworkable ambition into a workable one.

A name for a career aim

The name comes first and it constrains everything else. A career aim phrased as a role, a salary, or a company is an aim about outcomes you do not control, and it will fail in the way that all such aims fail: not immediately, but gradually, through a series of reinterpretations that keep it alive while removing the parts you could have acted on.

A useful name is short, arbitrary, and attached to a verb. The verb is the whole point, because it tells you at the start whether the aim is in your column or somebody else's. If the verb names something you did on Tuesday, the aim is usable. If it names something that happens to you, you have written a preference and not an aim.

The three parts of a career, and only one is yours

Split a professional outcome into three parts: your decisions, other people's decisions, and conditions outside both. The first part includes what you charge, what you accept, what you decline, who you contact, what you write down, when you follow up, and what you learn. The second includes whether a client hires you, whether a panel says yes, whether a manager promotes you. The third includes the economy, the season, and your health.

The first part is large enough to build a practice on. People consistently underestimate it, because none of those decisions is visible in the outcome, and because the second part, when it goes badly, absorbs all of the attention. A practice aimed at the first part is not a consolation prize. It is the only part of a career that responds to being worked on at all.

Why partially controllable is the ideal target

This sounds obvious once stated and it is the most useful principle in the whole area. An aim that is wholly within your control is usually too small to be worth a ritual: you are already doing it. An aim that is wholly outside your control is untestable, because you cannot distinguish skill, luck, and the intervention of something you do not control. A partially controllable aim sits in the only region where effort and outcome are visibly connected.

Practitioners who adopt this criterion find that their aims get smaller and their results get better, which is an uncomfortable trade to recommend. Ask for the meeting you have been avoiding and see whether the aim still works. If it does not, it was an aim about other people's decisions, and it will now be producing interpretations rather than actions.

The servitor pattern, described exactly

A servitor for a task is a thoughtform assigned to one repeated action, kept in the background of attention, and invoked at the moment the action is due. It is a commitment device with a symbolic layer, and the symbolic layer is optional. You can run one entirely as a rule, a fixed hour, and a line in a notebook, and it will do most of the work that elaborate accounts of the pattern describe.

The pattern works because the task is repetitive and the failure mode is forgetting, not unwillingness. A task done once does not need a servitor. A task done forty times a month, where each instance is small and easy to skip, is exactly what a persistent assignment is for. The assignment does not make the task easier. It removes the daily decision, which is where the actual failures were happening.

If-then plans and what the glyph adds

Implementation intentions, in the form if this situation arises, then I will do this specific thing, are among the more reliably replicated findings in behavioural research. They work because they move the decision from the moment of difficulty, when you are tired and the task is unpleasant, to a moment when you were calm and could decide properly.

The glyph adds a small amount to that. It compresses the intention into a form that is hard to forget and easy to recognise, and it makes the commitment feel like a thing rather than a plan, which matters for a person whose difficulty is not planning but starting. Treat the if-then as the mechanism and the glyph as the handle. If you have one, keep the other; if you have neither, the habit of writing the plan down is the greater part of the benefit.

Aiming at a schedule instead of a feeling

The most common career aim in this field is some version of a state of mind: more confident, more visible, better at networking, less anxious about money. All of these are feelings, and feelings cannot be scheduled, which is why those aims produce nothing beyond the initial lift.

Rewrite the aim as a schedule. Three outreach messages before ten. One conversation with a former colleague each Thursday. Quotes followed up within forty-eight hours, every time. These are dull and checkable, and they have the further advantage that doing them tends to produce the confidence the original aim was trying to conjure. Aim at the schedule and the feeling arrives as a side effect, which is the only reliable way it arrives.

The study version, and why memory is the wrong aim

Study aims are where the general form fails most visibly, because every study aim in the popular material is a wish for an improved mental faculty. Better memory, sharper focus, deeper understanding, and so on. None of these can be scheduled and none can be checked from outside.

The research on learning gives you something much better. Performance responds to distributed practice, which means spreading the work across days, and to retrieval, which means testing yourself rather than rereading. A sigil for better memory will not beat a sigil for a specific slot in a specific evening spent testing yourself on the material. The second is countable, and the first is not. Where you can choose between a mental-faculty aim and a schedule aim, the schedule is the stronger working every time.

Choosing the task worth repeating

A good servitor target is small, repeated, and currently underdone. It should take under an hour, it should recur at least weekly, and it should be something you are not currently doing at all. People usually pick something large and occasional, which is exactly backwards, because a large occasional task does not need a persistent assignment and will not benefit from one.

Look at your own last month and count what you did repeatedly and avoided. The answer is usually one or two items, and they are frequently unglamorous: the follow-up email, the invoice, the twenty minutes of work on the thing you keep postponing, the second draft. A servitor on any of these outperforms a servitor on your career, and it will do so within a month.

The first fortnight

Build the sigil in one sitting, charge it by forgetting the sentence, and run the same short session at the same time for two weeks. Keep the count low enough that missing one is an irritation rather than a catastrophe. The fortnight is not for results; it is for finding out whether you will actually do the thing, because that is the variable everything else depends on and the one nobody can predict in advance.

At the end of the fortnight, answer one question with a number. How many times did you do the assigned task? Not how productive the fortnight felt, not how the conversations went, not whether anyone replied. The number is the result, and if it is under half of what was possible, the aim or the schedule needs changing before anything else does.

Reading a month against the two columns

Keep two columns and never merge them. The first is what you did: tasks assigned, tasks completed, times the outreach block was worked. The second is what happened: replies, hires, invitations, money, silence. The temptation to read the second column as evidence about the first is the single most reliable source of bad conclusions in this area, and the two-column format exists to prevent it.

After a month, look at the first column alone and ask whether the behaviour has become automatic. If it has, the practice has succeeded on its own terms and there is a reasonable case for continuing. If it has not, the second column is uninterpretable, because you do not know what your conduct would have produced without the sigil and the schedule, and any reading of the results assumes the answer you were hoping for.

A no, and what to do with it

Rejection is a normal output and it is the point at which most career practices are abandoned. The usual reasoning is that a sigil should work, and a no suggests something was wrong with it. The alternative reading is that the assignment produced an approach, the approach produced a no, and both of those are outcomes of your column rather than of anyone else's.

Write the no down on the same day, with the date, the approach, and what you said. Most people find that the pattern in their rejections is legible: the same hour, the same formulation, the same missing follow-up. It is far more actionable than any conclusion about a sigil, and it costs one line. A servitor that never gets tested against outcomes is a superstition, and the test is cheap.

When a servitor should retire

Write the retirement condition into the charge, while you are still building it and before the habit has made the decision feel like a loss. A good condition is factual: when the task has been done automatically for two months, or when the role it belonged to has changed, or when the count has been met for a defined number of months. A feeling of readiness is not a condition, because it arrives at exactly the wrong time.

Retiring a servitor is not an abandonment. It means the assignment has been discharged, which is the successful outcome, and the honest thing to do with a completed assignment is to say so and stop. People keep these running for years because the notion of retiring them feels like withdrawing from a practice, and the practice has by then become the thing maintained rather than the behaviour it was built to support.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a sigil get me a job or a promotion?

No, and any account that says otherwise is selling something. What a practice can affect is the part of the process you control: what you send, when you follow up, what you ask for, how many conversations you have. Whether that is enough to change an outcome is an ordinary professional question, and it depends on the market and on the people making the decision, neither of which responds to a symbol.

What is a servitor for a task?

A thoughtform assigned to one repeated action, held in the background of attention and invoked when the action is due. Functionally it is a commitment device: it removes the daily decision about whether to do the thing. The symbolic layer is optional, and most of the reported benefit survives if you keep only a fixed hour and a line in a notebook recording whether you did it.

Should I aim a study sigil at better memory?

Aim it at a schedule instead. Learning responds to distributed practice and to self-testing, and both can be scheduled; memory as a faculty cannot. A sigil for a specific evening of retrieval practice will outperform a sigil for better memory within a month, and you will be able to tell the difference, because one of them produces a countable record and the other does not.

How do I know whether the working is helping?

Read the behaviour column, not the results column, and do it after a month. Did the assigned task happen, at the assigned rate? That number is the only thing the practice can be evaluated on, because the results column cannot be attributed without knowing what you would have done anyway. If the behaviour has become automatic, the practice is doing its job regardless of what the outcomes say.

When should I retire a servitor?

Write the condition down while you build it, because after two months of automatic behaviour every decision about stopping will feel like a loss. Factual conditions work: the task has been automatic for two months, the role has changed, the count has been met for a defined period. A feeling of readiness is not a condition, because it appears at the point where the practice has become the thing you maintain rather than the thing you do.
