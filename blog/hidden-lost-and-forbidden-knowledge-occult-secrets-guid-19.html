Hidden, Lost and Forbidden Knowledge

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

A reference sheet is a different kind of document from a reading list, and the difference is worth stating before anything else, because most of the trouble in this area comes from treating the two as interchangeable. A reading list tells you what to read. A reference sheet tells you what a thing is, what it will carry, and which word for it is wrong. Those are three different operations and only the second one survives contact with a difficult source. What I have assembled here is three entries and three terms, arranged so that you can hold the whole thing in your head and use it without a library. The entries are a fragmentary text, a short modern pamphlet, and an anonymously published book. The terms are hidden, forbidden, and lost. Each entry carries a note on what it can support and a note on the term most people reach for. I have kept the last column strict, because a reference sheet that lets any claim through is not a reference, it is a mood. If you find yourself wanting the sheet to license something, that is the moment to check which entry you have misread, because in all three cases the answer is that it does not license it.

What belongs on a reference like this

Three things, in this order. What the object is, stated in a form that could be checked by somebody who has never heard of it. What it can be used to support, which will be narrower than you would like. And the term that gets used for it when the real answer is inconvenient. That third column is the reason the sheet is worth building, because the first two are available anywhere and the third is what actually saves you an argument.

The reason to keep it short is that a reference gets used under pressure. Nobody consults a fourteen-page dossier at eleven at night when something has gone wrong and they are trying to work out whether a text can be trusted. A short sheet gets read. Everything on it has to be true, which means each line has to be defensible to somebody who knows the subject and is looking for the mistake.

Entry one, and the term people get wrong

The fragmentary Jewish apocalyptic text known by the Greek name Enoch. It survives in fragmentary form, including in material recovered from the Dead Sea Scrolls, and church writers in the first centuries knew it, which is why it appears in older Bibles and not in most modern ones.

The term that gets used for it is forbidden, and the term is wrong in a specific way. It was not forbidden. It was excluded, which is a decision that leaves a paper trail: the exclusions are documented, the reasons were argued at the time, and the fact that it circulated for centuries before the canon settled is knowable. Calling it forbidden collapses a documented editorial decision into a romantic one. Calling it excluded lets you ask the useful question, which is who decided, when, and on what grounds.

What the fragmentary material can support

It supports a claim about a body of Jewish apocalyptic writing that predates the surviving canon, and it supports the point that canon formation is a process with identifiable stages. It supports work on the comparison between this material and early Christian literature, because the parallels are real and numerous and have been studied since the nineteenth century.

It does not support claims about a hidden teaching, a withheld tradition, or an unbroken line of transmission from antiquity. The text does not say it is secret. Texts that claim concealment without evidence are making an argument about themselves, and the argument is not evidence. This distinction is the one the sheet exists to keep, because the same passage gets used to support both positions depending on who is reading.

Exclusion as data rather than as mystery

Exclusion is one of the better-documented categories in the history of religious text. The Book of Enoch is a reasonably good specimen, and the Kybalion is a reasonably good counterexample, and holding both in the same sheet is what keeps the category honest.

Treated as data, exclusion tells you something specific: a group of people with authority over which texts counted decided, argued, and wrote down their reasoning. That is a historical event with a date range and participants. Treated as mystery, it becomes a property of the text itself, which is unfalsifiable and therefore uninteresting. The material is far more useful the first way, and it is also the version that survives a conversation with somebody who knows the subject.

Entry two, and what short and cheap implies

A pamphlet published in 1977, a little over a hundred pages, costing almost nothing, and still the commonest first book in the field. It is a practical manual that happens to be ideological, and it is written by the person who later founded the organisation that most practitioners in this area belong to or descend from.

What short and cheap implies is worth pausing on. It implies that the barrier to entry is money and time, and that the person who set the barrier deliberately. It also implies that the book cannot be a hidden document, because it was printed, sold, and left the copyright page blank on purpose as a joke that many people have never noticed. A text that is openly available, inexpensive, and not attributed to anyone is the opposite of concealed. The sheet should say so, because the mismatch between how it is used and what it is, is the whole reason the entry is worth including.

Why a modern instruction is a modern document

Something published fifty years ago and still in print is not a relic. It is a working document, written in a specific context, by a person with a project, in a moment when certain things were newly thinkable. Reading it as timeless is a way of not reading it at all, because the historical layer is where the interesting content is.

The corollary is that its own claims are open to criticism in a way an older text's are not. Nobody can use age to defend a sentence written in 1977. So when the sheet says what the pamphlet can support, the answer is unusually generous: it can support methodological instructions, because those are what it is, and those are still usable. What it cannot support is authority, and it does not claim to. It is a manual, not an authority, and the difference is the reason it has held up while a great many other things from the same decade have not.

Entry three, and the permanent category

A book published in 1908, anonymously, not attributed to any known author, with claims about its own provenance that are not supported by anything. The seven principles it sets out are widely used in this field and in adjacent commercial ones.

Anonymous authorship is a permanent category rather than a temporary mystery. Texts in that category are grouped together, and the group has a known shape: revival and esotericism movements around 1880, books that attach themselves to a lineage they cannot document, and a characteristic rhetorical habit of making the claim of transmission do the work that evidence would do in any other document. A competent practitioner does not need to identify who wrote a particular anonymous text. They need to know that anonymity here is a genre, and that the genre carries a presumption of failure that has to be actively overcome rather than assumed away.

What anonymity licenses

It licenses a great deal of flexibility, and most of the flexibility is legitimate. You may use a text without knowing who wrote it. You may find a procedure in it and follow the procedure. You may take a definition and apply it elsewhere. The genre is a genre, and genres have conventions that can be used once you know what they are.

It does not license treating the transmission claim as a finding. The most common misuse in the modern application of this material is exactly that: the claim that the principles come from a source older than the book is repeated as though it were a fact about the source, by people who have not read the provenance claim closely enough to notice that nothing supports it. The sheet should carry this in bold if it could. Using the material while holding the claim at arm's length is not a compromise. It is the position a competent reader has been occupying for a century.

Three terms and what each commits you to

Hidden means somebody chose not to show you. That commits you to a claim about an intention, and intentions are hard to evidence but not impossible: a deliberate omission can be demonstrated from the document's structure, and the fragmentary material is a reasonable case where the evidence is documentary. The word is usable in a narrow set of circumstances.

Forbidden means somebody stopped you. That commits you to an enforcement claim, which is stronger than an intention claim, and the evidence is usually a legal or institutional record. Where that record exists, forbidden is accurate. Where it does not, forbidden is a romance. Lost means nobody copied it any further. That commits you to almost nothing, and of the three it is the only claim that is straightforwardly true of a large amount of material. Most of what gets called hidden or forbidden in this field is merely lost, and the difference between the two is a difference in how much you are claiming.

Building your own entries

Three columns, three or four rows, written in an evening. That is the entire method and it scales to any subject, which is the useful part. The discipline is not the research. It is refusing to write a row until you can fill the third column, and the third column is the term that gets used for it wrongly. If you cannot name the misreading, you do not understand the entry yet, and the entry will fail in exactly the way you were trying to prevent.

The test of a finished sheet is that somebody else could use it to make a decision you would endorse. If your sheet is only usable by you, it is a set of personal associations and the third column is doing no work. If it is usable by somebody who disagrees with you, then the entries are about the objects rather than about your relationship to them, which is the property that makes it a reference.

The one question to ask of any source

Who wrote it, and what did they want it to do. Everything else in the sheet follows from answering that, and it is a question that can be answered badly in a way that is still informative. A text with a documented author and a stated purpose is a source. A text with no author and a stated purpose is a document of use. A text with no author and no stated purpose is a text you are probably better off describing than interpreting.

The reason to put it in this position is that the rest of the sheet is bookkeeping, and this is the part that does the work. Provenance, exclusion, publication, genre, the misreading term. All five columns in every row are downstream of the author-and-purpose pair. That is why the sheet is short. Two questions, applied to a handful of things, produce most of what a person needs to know in order to use them well.

The sheet worth keeping

Three entries, three terms, one question, on a single page, and kept where you will find it during a difficult evening rather than in a drawer for later. The reason it is worth the trouble is not that it settles anything. It does not, and nothing of this length settles anything. It is worth it because the third column catches the specific error that this material invites, and the error is not a small one.

The claim that a tradition was deliberately concealed is attractive, and it is the load-bearing claim of a great deal of the material in circulation, and it is the one the sources do not support. Holding the material and dropping the claim costs nothing. It leaves you with a pamphlet that gives real instructions, a fragmentary text that shows a documented exclusion, and an anonymous book that is usable once you stop treating its provenance as a finding. That is a workable position, and it is more interesting than the alternative, which is holding the claim and being wrong about it repeatedly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a hidden and a forbidden text?

Hidden means somebody chose not to show you, which is a claim about intention and is hard but not impossible to evidence from the document's own structure. Forbidden means somebody stopped you, which is a claim about enforcement and usually leaves a legal or institutional record. Lost means nobody copied it further, which claims almost nothing and is the honest description of a large amount of the material in circulation. Most material called hidden or forbidden is simply lost, and the difference is how much you are claiming.

Is the Kybalion worth reading at all?

The material is usable and the seven principles are widely applied, so reading it is reasonable. What should not be carried forward is the book's claim about its own provenance, which nothing supports and which is repeated so often that it functions as a fact. Anonymous authorship there is a genre with a known shape rather than an open mystery, and a competent reader has been holding that position for a century. Use the content, set the claim down.

Why do most modern Bibles leave the Book of Enoch out?

Because canon formation was a process, with identifiable stages, participants, and documented arguments in the first centuries. The text circulated widely before the canon settled, which is why some church writers knew it. Calling that exclusion forbidden inverts the direction of the evidence: there is a record of a decision being made, and the decision is not secrecy. It is a boundary being drawn, and boundaries leave paperwork.

How do I build a reference sheet for a subject I know nothing about?

Pick four to six objects. For each, write what it is, what it can carry, and the term that gets used for it wrongly. Leave the last column blank until you can fill it, because the misreading term is the part that shows whether you have understood the object rather than just filed it. Two questions, author and purpose, will produce most of the columns. Anyone can do it in an evening.

What if I cannot verify a source's provenance?

Then the third column is your answer and you keep it in front of you. An unverifiable source can be used and cited, with the limitation stated in the same sentence, which is what a competent practitioner does with a text that cannot be attributed. What does not work is dropping the limitation because it is inconvenient in a conversation. If the source matters enough to argue about, it matters enough to state the limit plainly.
