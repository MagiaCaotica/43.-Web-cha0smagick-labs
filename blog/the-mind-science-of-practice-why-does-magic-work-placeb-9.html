The Mind Science of Practice

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 12 min read

The honest question behind every attempt to explain why a practice works is not whether it works. It is what, exactly, is doing the work, and which parts of the popular explanation survive contact with the research. This is an attempt to answer that properly. The material below looks at four claims that circulate constantly in this field: that ritual changes the body, that belief is the active ingredient, that attention works like a prediction machine, and that a deck of five cards can tell you something about the world. The first two have partial support. The third has a useful name and an overstated reputation. The fourth is a very good arithmetic lesson. Written for practitioners who want a defensible position rather than a flattering one, and who suspect that a sincere commitment is compatible with refusing to overstate the mechanism.

The instrument in every experiment is the person conducting it

This is the part of the literature that gets left out when results are quoted, and it is the part that governs how much any result can be asked to carry. In every study of ritual, expectation, imagery or intention, the person running the experiment is also the person who designed it, decided what counted as a hit, and wrote the notes. That is not a reason to throw the studies out. It is a reason to read every number as a number produced by a particular arrangement of people and incentives.

The distinction matters because the popular version of this material usually presents a finding and then strips away the conditions under which it was obtained. A result observed under a specific protocol, by a specific observer, with a specific definition of success, is not a fact about ritual in general. It is a fact about ritual under those conditions, and extending it beyond them is the most common error in the genre.

None of this makes the material useless. It makes it legible. Once you know that the observer is part of the apparatus, you can ask better questions of any given study: who was counting, what were they counting, and what would they have had to see for a null result to count as a null result rather than as a failed experiment.

The open-label finding and what it does not license

In open-label placebo studies the participants are informed openly that the substance they have been given does nothing, and measurable effects are sometimes reported anyway. This complicates the clean claim that nothing happens without belief. It does not show that belief is unnecessary in general, and it certainly does not show that any belief about any outcome will produce it.

What the studies actually suggest is narrower and more interesting. Being told the truth, being treated with respect, and being given a reason to expect a change are each capable of producing measurable change on their own. That is a real mechanism, and it is a mechanism that operates on the body through ordinary social and attentional pathways rather than through anything supernatural.

The part to resist is the leap from that finding to the claim that expectation creates reality, or that a sufficiently vivid performance of conviction substitutes for the thing itself. A participant believing a pill is inert and reporting less pain is a fact about pain and expectation. It is not a fact about whether sigils move money, whether spirits appear, or whether the dead can be reached. Those are different questions and they need their own evidence.

Attention as hypothesis management, and where the metaphor breaks

Predictive processing treats perception as the management of hypotheses rather than the reception of data. On this account, what you see is the output of a system that has committed to a model of the world and then corrects it in light of incoming evidence. That gives a clean account of why expectation changes experience without anyone consciously lying to themselves.

It is a genuinely useful frame for understanding why a ritual changes what you notice. Once you have spent a fortnight looking for a sign, you will find one. The machinery that does this is ordinary and is not evidence of anything beyond ordinary perception. Understanding the mechanism does not remove the value of the practice. It relocates the value, from the outside world to your own attention.

Where the analogy breaks is the moment it is used to explain the world rather than the observer. A model of how expectation shapes experience is not a model of how intention shapes events. The first is well supported within the brain. The second makes a claim about the world, and no theory of perception settles it.

The autonomic signature, which is the part that holds up

Of everything in this field, the most solid finding is also the least dramatic. Ritual reliably produces measurable autonomic change. Heart-rate variability drops. Respiration patterns shift. Muscle tension changes. These are not subtle inferences from a story someone told afterwards. They are instruments, they are reproducible, and they are as reliable as anything else in psychology.

That is a real answer to a narrow version of the question. Yes, a structured practice that involves breath, posture, repetition and expectation reliably alters the body. People have been exploiting that fact deliberately for far longer than any laboratory has measured it, and a considerable amount of traditional practice amounts to a technology for producing a specific physiological state on demand.

What it does not establish is that the change has any content. A calmer nervous system is a real and worthwhile outcome. It is not the same outcome as a defended border, a reconciled relationship, or a client who signed the contract. When the two are conflated, the honest finding is stretched past the point where it can carry weight.

Conditioning, and the mechanism nobody wants to name

Some of what looks like result is simply training. A person who repeats an action for several weeks will get better at it. If the action involves noticing, they will notice more, because noticing is a trainable capacity and they have been training it. This is true of divination, of dream recall, of remote viewing description, and of the ability to tell whether a working produced something.

This is a serious confound rather than a disproof. The question was never whether practice produces change. It was whether practice produces change for reasons connected to what the practitioner claims. Better noticing is a real improvement and it is also completely ordinary, and it will be mistaken for evidence of the extraordinary by anyone who does not measure it.

The control that handles this is simple. Use a task you have no practice at, and keep the record blind. Cast something before you learn a method, write the result down, and only later compare. Genuine skill grows. The uncomfortable part is that almost nobody does this, and almost everyone finds a way to interpret the data once they already know the answer.

  • Practise a task you have no training in at all, and keep the record.
  • Do the recording before you know how you will interpret it later.
  • Keep at least one method you never learn, as a control condition.
  • Write the nulls in the same hand as the hits, in the same week.

The Zener deck and the arithmetic it teaches

The Zener deck used in the classic extrasensory perception work has five symbols. On a forced-choice trial with five options, chance produces a correct rate of one in five. That single fact is the most useful thing in this whole area, and almost nobody internalises it before starting.

Worked through, it makes the reasoning visible. A person hitting at thirty per cent on five cards, run a thousand times, is not showing a subtle effect. At a thousand trials, a twenty per cent rate is what you expect roughly half the time, and thirty per cent is detectable. A person hitting at ninety per cent is showing something, and the question is what, because most of them are not showing that.

The lesson transfers directly to the whole genre. Any forced-choice system with a known chance rate can be reasoned about exactly this way, and most systems in occult practice are forced-choice systems with a known chance rate. The arithmetic is not hostile. It is simply the only tool available, and using it is what separates a record from a feeling.

What expectancy research does and does not reach

Expectancy effects measurably shift reported pain and affect a small number of physiological outcomes. That is a genuine finding, and it is limited. It concerns experience and some bodily measures. It does not concern the healing of disease, and reading it as though it did has done real damage to the reputation of the entire field.

The distinction worth keeping is between outcomes that are largely subjective and outcomes that are largely not. Pain reports move with expectation because reporting is partly a sensory act and partly an interpretive one. A tumour, a signature, a transferred payment and a conviction are not in the same category, and no amount of citing pain research connects them.

There is a further wrinkle that nobody in this field wants to mention. Expectancy effects are largest when the outcome is ambiguous, the measurement is subjective, and the person wants it to be real. All three conditions are common in occult practice by construction. That is not an argument against practice. It is a strong argument for building a record that is not ambiguous and not subjective.

The three failure modes in the published material

The first is the unblinded design. Almost all work in this area lacks a control condition that the participant would genuinely fail. A participant who has been told they are doing a special exercise and is being asked to look for a result is not a blind observer, and neither is the person recording what they see.

The second is the missing baseline. Without a measurement taken before the practice, there is nothing to compare against, and post-hoc impressions of improvement are cheap. The third is selective reporting. Null results in this field are far less publishable than positive ones, which means the published record is a biased sample of what people found, not a fair summary.

All three failures share a cause, and the cause is not fraud. It is that nobody is incentivised to build the controls. The enthusiast gets a result and a story. The sceptic gets a null and no audience. The person who insists on both a control and a published finding has an unattractive project with an uncertain payoff, and very few of them exist.

  1. No genuine failure condition built into the design, for the participant or the observer.
  2. No baseline taken before the practice begins, so improvement cannot be separated from drift.
  3. Nulls go unpublished, which makes the literature a sample of successes by construction.

A record designed to be able to argue back

The most useful thing you can do for your own reliability is build a record that can contradict you. That means a specific prediction with a date attached, written before the working, and stored somewhere you will not revise it. Not a feeling that something might happen. A sentence with a verb, an object, and a deadline.

It also means recording the nulls with exactly the same care as the hits, in the same handwriting, on the same day. The moment of writing is the moment of maximum honesty, and a record filled in a week later has already been edited by memory in a way that favours the practice.

This is uncomfortably close to the method the founders of the tradition insisted on, and that is not a coincidence. The claim that a claim may be tested was the movement's most durable contribution, and it has been quietly abandoned by most of the people who invoke it. Recovering it costs nothing and it is the only thing that makes the rest defensible.

Writing your own system rather than borrowing one

The most productive move available to a practitioner who wants rigor is to stop collecting methods and start designing one. A system needs a defined question, a defined procedure, a defined observation window, and a defined criterion for what would count as a result. Everything else is decoration around those four things.

Most systems borrowed from other people fail because they arrive without the fourth element. A practice can be beautiful, well attested, historically layered and completely unspecified about what would show that it had done anything. Borrowing it inherits all of that, including the vagueness.

Building your own does not have to mean reinventing. It means taking one procedure, writing down what result would look like before you start, and running it long enough to find out. A small system you understand completely beats a large one you have read about. That claim is not mystical and it is not debatable.

The protocol, on one question only

Take one question small enough to be answerable. Not whether the practice works in general, which cannot be settled by one person, but whether a specific routine produces a specific measurable change in a specific person over a fixed period. The narrower the better.

Write down the measure before the first repetition. Then run the routine on schedule, log each repetition the same day, and stop at the date you set regardless of how you feel about it. The stopping rule is what makes the result interpretable, because it is decided before the data arrives.

If the result appears, the next question is whether you can repeat it. If it does not, the next question is whether the routine was the problem or the measure was. Either answer is worth having, and both are unavailable if you never defined success in advance.

Where sincerity and rigour are compatible

Nothing in this analysis requires a practitioner to be a sceptic or a reductionist. It requires only that the claims be stated at a level the evidence can carry, and that the record be capable of producing a result you did not want. Those two commitments are compatible with any metaphysical position, including the ones you find most compelling.

The positions that fail are the ones that need the evidence to be unreliable. A practice held with full sincerity and tested in a way that could fail is a strong position. A practice held with full sincerity and tested in a way that cannot fail is a faith, and it should be named as one rather than presented as a technique.

The tradition this material comes from was explicit about the distinction. It never asked for belief, and it asked for the record. Those requests are compatible, and the fact that they are compatible is the single most useful thing the founders left behind.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the open-label result actually establish?

That some measurable physiological effects do not require a person to be deceived about what they are receiving. It does not show that belief is unnecessary in general, and it says nothing about whether intention alters external events. Being told the truth, treated with respect and given a reason to expect change are each capable of producing real change through ordinary social and attentional pathways.

Is the five-card result really just luck?

Chance produces one in five, so at a thousand trials a twenty per cent hit rate is what you expect about half the time and thirty per cent is detectable. That makes the deck a good teaching case precisely because the arithmetic is easy. The same reasoning applies to every forced-choice system in this field, and almost none of them are used that way.

Does ritual help with anxiety, given all of this?

Yes, and the reason is well documented. Structured practice with breath, posture and repetition reliably produces autonomic change, including reduced heart-rate variability, and a calmer nervous system is a real and worthwhile outcome. What that finding does not do is confer any particular content on the change. Calmer is not the same as protected.

So is all of this just conditioning?

Part of it, most likely, and that is worth knowing rather than denying. Practising a task for weeks makes you better at it, and better noticing is ordinary improvement rather than evidence of anything unusual. The control that handles it is simple: use a task you have no practice at and keep the record before you know how you will read it.

Is a paid training app worth anything here?

None of the reasoning above is proprietary, and all of the actual method is free. What a paid app can do is hold the bookkeeping that makes the method work: the log, the prediction you registered before the result arrived, the date you committed to stopping, and the saved history that lets you compare a second cycle against the first. That is the unglamorous part and that is what decides whether your record can argue back at you.
