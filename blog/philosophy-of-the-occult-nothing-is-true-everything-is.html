Philosophy of the Occult

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Modern occult practice leans on a small number of quotations, and one of them has done more damage than the rest. Nothing is true, everything is permitted appears in the third stanza of verse one of the Book of the Law, and it is quoted several orders of magnitude more often than any other line in the corpus, almost always to authorise something. Read in place, it is a much narrower claim about sincerity than the popular use suggests. This is an attempt to put the line back where it came from, to separate the three different positions that get collapsed into it, and to look at what mainstream philosophy actually says about each. None of that requires you to give up a practice.

One line and a century of lost context

The Book of the Law was dictated to Aleister Crowley in Cairo in 1904 and published in 1909, and Crowley treated it as the founding text of a new religion rather than a polemic. The line sits in a passage about a sequence of ethical positions, each of which fails, ending on a refusal of the fixed moral code. Read in sequence the line concludes an argument, it does not open a licence.

Everything after 1909 has done the opposite of preserving that context. Crowley himself spent much of the rest of his life explaining that the line was not a permission slip, and his explanations were not widely read. What survived instead was the fragment, which is portable, quotable, and detached from the argument it concluded. Almost every contemporary use of the line is an instance of taking a conclusion out of a chain of reasoning and using it as a premise.

What the line claims, read in place

Three claims are routinely treated as one. The first is epistemological relativism about knowledge: no proposition is beyond the possibility of being revised. The second is moral nihilism about ethics: there is no objective moral fact. The third is practical occultism: act on your own judgement and take the consequences. They are distinct, they come from distinct disciplines, and the first is far more defensible than the second.

Nobody who quotes the line is necessarily committed to any of the three. The reason it recurs is that it names a mood, the mood of a person who has noticed that moral claims are made by people with interests. That noticing is legitimate and the leap from it to the conclusion that nothing constrains action is not. The line is popular because it compresses a real insight and a bad inference into eleven words, and readers take the compression at face value.

  • Epistemological relativism, a claim about knowledge and its revision.
  • Moral nihilism, a claim about ethics and whether any moral fact exists.
  • Practical occultism, a method of deciding and accepting the outcome.
  • The three are independent and can be held in any combination.

Three worldviews and three bargains

Philosophical idealism holds that mind or consciousness is fundamental, so that the world is less independent of the practitioner than common sense suggests. Gnostic dualism holds that the material world is a trap maintained by a hostile power, so that ascent is a matter of escape. Mystical monism holds that there is a single substance underlying everything, so that apparent separation is a feature of how attention works rather than of what exists.

Each implies something quite different about the value of a ritual. Under a strong dualist view, working on material conditions is at best a distraction from the real problem. Under monism, working on conditions is entirely reasonable and even necessary, since the separation you are experiencing is the thing to be examined. Under idealism, the practice is a technique for aligning attention with the structure of what is already there. None of these are stupid. What is stupid is holding all three at once and treating ritual as a tool that somehow satisfies each of them differently depending on the day.

Relativism about knowledge, nihilism about morals

Relativism about knowledge is defensible in a weak form and indefensible in a strong one. The weak form says that what counts as evidence, and which methods are legitimate, is partly settled by a community rather than by nature alone. The strong form says that no procedure can ever establish anything, which collapses into inability to distinguish any claim from any other and makes the position self-undermining.

Moral nihilism is a different matter and a much harder sell. It is not the same claim as saying that moral disagreement is hard, or that moral claims are contested, or that people disagree about values for non-rational reasons. It is the claim that there is no such thing as a moral fact, and almost nobody who quotes the Book of the Law line is in a position to argue for it. The interesting version is descriptive rather than prescriptive, and it says that moral reasoning functions in practice the way a technology functions, which is a claim you can evaluate and which leaves the practice of reasoning intact.

Paraconsistency, and why it fits

Paraconsistent logic is a family of formal systems in which some propositions need not resolve to true or false, and contradiction does not spread the way classical logic requires. It is a live subject in the philosophy of language rather than a curiosity, and it was developed partly in response to dialetheism, the view that some true statements are also false.

It fits magical frameworks uncomfortably well. If a working succeeds while the practitioner's own account of why it succeeded is demonstrably wrong, a classical account must declare one of the two false. A paraconsistent account can let the event stand while marking the explanation as failing to cohere, which is a more honest description of what practitioners actually have. This is not a licence to believe contradictions. It is a formalisation of the practice of noticing when your explanation has stopped fitting and refusing to throw away the observation along with it.

Sincerity as a discipline

Crowley's own gloss is worth more attention than it gets. He insisted repeatedly that the line was a demand for honesty rather than a grant of freedom, and that the test of an act is whether the person performing it can state their motive without flinching. That is a much harder standard than the freedom reading and considerably more useful.

Read this way, the line describes a procedure. Name the motive. Do the act. Accept the consequence rather than renegotiating afterwards. It has the same shape as any other serious commitment, and it happens to rule out most of the uses the line is now put to, because most of those uses involve a motive the person would not state plainly. A practitioner who cannot write down why they did something has already failed the standard the line was setting.

The question about delusion, answered properly

There is a real question underneath the usual one. Certain practices, particularly identification practices, can cause a person to lose a reliable distinction between their own thoughts and something they believe is arriving from elsewhere, and that is a genuine clinical risk with a clear warning sign: the inability to evaluate your own interpretation on demand.

The ordinary check is whether you can still be wrong. Can you say what evidence would show that your last interpretation was mistaken, and would that evidence be acceptable to you if it arrived? Can you still decline a conclusion you wanted? Can you describe the previous evening accurately? These are answerable questions and they discriminate well between intense practice and difficulty. The people most at risk are the ones for whom the second answer is no.

Cynicism as a way of avoiding an argument

Positions that treat every mystical claim as a power move have a certain energy and one real advantage, which is that they are hard to argue with on the ground. If nothing anyone says can be taken at face value, no evidence can be demanded. The position is unfalsifiable in a way that is uncomfortably similar to the position it attacks, and the resemblance is worth noticing.

The better version of the same instinct is simply to ask what someone gains. That is ordinary scepticism applied consistently and it catches a great deal, including a fair amount of practitioner behaviour. What it does not license is deciding the question in advance, because then the inquiry is over before it starts and nothing can be learned from it. Scepticism that is willing to be shown evidence is a tool. Scepticism that only ever finds motive is a rhetorical position wearing a tool's clothes.

Where mainstream philosophy lands

Analytic philosophers have generally been more generous to the practical core than either side of this argument expects. Attention, intention and expectancy are all legitimate objects of study, and the study of how expectation shapes perception is a serious empirical programme. Where the disagreement actually sits is on the external claim, and that is a disagreement about evidence rather than about meaning.

The paraconsistent angle is the most novel and the least developed in occult circles, and it is worth pursuing on its own terms. It suggests a discipline that does not require you to choose between a working mattering and your explanation of it being right. Most practitioners would find that a better position than the alternatives on offer, which are all-or-nothing in one direction or the other.

  • On the epistemology, weak relativism survives and strong relativism does not.
  • On ethics, the descriptive reading is interesting and the prescriptive one is not available.
  • On the paraconsistent reading, it is a live technical position, not a mystical escape hatch.
  • On practical occultism, it is a method claim and is judged on results like any other.

A working rule for the practitioner

This rule is a direct translation of the sincerity reading into something usable, and it costs five minutes per working. It also does most of the work that the popular use of the quotation is supposed to do, without requiring you to accept a position on moral nihilism that you probably do not hold.

The separation of observation from explanation is the part that produces improvement over years. Almost every serious methodological error in this field is an explanation that has quietly rewritten an observation. Keeping them in different lines, written at different times, makes that visible, and visibility is the whole difference between a practice that compounds and one that simply accumulates.

  1. Before any act, write one sentence naming the motive without softening it.
  2. If you cannot write that sentence, the act is not ready to be performed.
  3. Record the outcome in a form another person could check.
  4. Keep the explanation separate from the observation in your notes.
  5. Revise the explanation freely; the observation stays where it is.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the line mean anything goes?

No, and reading it in the passage it concludes makes that fairly clear. It is the end of a sequence in which each fixed ethical position is shown to fail, and the emphasis falls on refusing a settled moral code rather than on authorising conduct. Crowley spent decades explaining that reading. The line can support acting on your own judgement while holding yourself to naming your motive honestly, and that is a considerably narrower thing than a permission slip.

How do I know when I am being delusional?

Ask whether you can still be wrong. Can you state what evidence would show your last interpretation was mistaken, and would you accept it if it arrived? Can you decline a conclusion you wanted? Can you describe the previous evening accurately? The people who are in real trouble are the ones for whom those answers are no, particularly the second one, because it means the checking mechanism itself has stopped working.

Is any of this cynicism?

The position that treats every mystical claim as a power move is best understood as a way of avoiding an argument, since it makes all claims unfalsifiable in the same way mystical claims are supposed to be. Applied consistently, asking what someone gains is ordinary and productive scepticism. The version that only ever finds motive has decided the question in advance, and a scepticism willing to be shown evidence is a different tool from one that has already decided.

How do I know when I am being delusional?

Ask whether you can still be wrong, in three specific ways. Can you state what evidence would show your last interpretation was mistaken, and would you accept it if it arrived? Can you decline a conclusion you wanted? Can you describe the previous evening accurately, without filling the gaps? The people in real difficulty are the ones for whom the second answer is no, because it means the checking mechanism has stopped working and everything else depends on it.

Where does the money actually go in a paid version of this?

Structure, at most. The reading is public and the thinking is free, and any tool that offers to tell you what a source means is offering to replace the part you were supposed to do. A notebook that keeps your written motives next to your recorded outcomes is genuinely useful, since that pairing is the discipline the sincerity reading asks for. A tool that scores your alignment with a tradition is selling an answer you would be better off deriving.
