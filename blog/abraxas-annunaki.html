Abraxas

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 13 min read

Abraxas occupies an awkward position in the history of names. The Gnostic writers who recorded the term did not present it as a god in any reassuring sense, and the occultists who picked it up later disagreed about what the name was supposed to reach. Abrasax, Abrasaxel, Saklas, the 365, the Basilidean demiurge, the sovereign of the outer limit: the sources contradict one another, and the contradictions are visible in translations anyone can consult. That friction is the material's value rather than a defect to smooth away. A myth borrowed as a working symbol is doing something other than history, and a practitioner who blurs those two jobs ends up defending claims that nobody can check. What follows treats Abraxas as a frame for building and testing, with the historical layer held honestly and the speculative layer labelled as speculation.

A Name Kept in Opposition

The name reaches modern occult practice through a chain of texts that were already arguing with one another. The clearest version appears in material associated with the Basilideans, a second-century group whose surviving fragments are late, partial, and largely hostile reports written by opponents. In those fragments the number 365 carries weight: a set of heavens, a set of powers, a boundary figure who rules whatever lies beyond the ordered cosmos. Whether the same name in a Manichaean context, a Sethian context, and a later Coptic summary denotes one being or three separate things is a question the sources never settle. The honest starting position is that we have inherited a word with several provenances and no agreement about its referent.

Two claims can be made with reasonable confidence. The name sits in a family of late-antique systems that pushed the divine further out than most mainstream theology was willing to follow, and the figure is not a member of a familiar pantheon with a biography. There is no birth story, no list of loves, no homeland claimed as a territory. The role is structural. Something stands between the intelligible and the material, and that something is described through number and through a title that resists translation.

For a working practice that difference matters more than it first appears. A deity with a biography hands you images, relationships, and a personality to negotiate with. A boundary figure hands you a position on a map, which is a different and arguably more useful asset. You can work with a position. You can work with the name for a boundary and build a relationship to the direction it marks.

Plenty of later writers quietly repaired the source. They add genealogies the fragments do not contain, they assign offices no text mentions, and they present a coherent figure that never existed as a single thing. Some of that smoothing makes practice easier. All of it is a decision worth making knowingly instead of inheriting by accident.

Tracing the 365 Without Pretending to Certainty

The number shows up often enough to deserve a close look. Three hundred and sixty-five corresponds to the days in an ordinary year, which in a solar culture is an obvious thing to reach for. It also has a long history as a number of completeness in administrative and calendrical contexts well before any Gnostic writer uses it for a spiritual architecture. So when a source says a figure rules 365, the first question is not which secret has just been revealed. The first question is which of several established usages the author is drawing on.

The sources behind that count are not hidden. Sumerian and Akkadian tablets were excavated, catalogued, and deciphered across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and translations have been in public hands for a generation. Where a text has been read and published, you can check the reading. Where it has not, you are able to say so. That distinction does more work in practice than any amount of appeal to authority, because it tells you which of your own claims sit exposed to correction.

  • A count of days suggests a calendrical frame, which is a claim about order and cycle rather than about hidden numbers.
  • A count of powers suggests a hierarchy, where each named unit has a domain and a relation to its neighbours.
  • A count attached to a ruler suggests administration, which is a claim about office rather than about malice.

Where the Modern Reading Came From

A great deal of the strangest material attached to this name arrived in the twentieth century wearing the costume of scholarship. The hypothesis that certain ancient texts describe visitors from another planet is popular, emotionally satisfying, and does not survive contact with the texts it claims to explain. When a source says gods came from a distant land and made people to labour for them, reading that as a spacecraft is a substitution made long after the source was written.

The substitution is worth naming because it changes what the text is doing. In the original framing a creator who makes workers out of a lesser material is making a statement about an injustice among those who labour, and the labour is the entire point of the scene. Reframed as technology the text becomes a claim about hardware, and the ethical content evaporates. What is left is a story about machines, which is a different story with a different emotional payoff and no greater evidentiary support.

The parallel case is Atlantis. It appears in two of Plato's dialogues as a literary device inside an argument about ideal forms, and no contemporary source mentions it. It became a historical claim during the nineteenth century, which is also the period in which it started to interest an enormous number of people. The passage from allegory to alleged fact required no new evidence, only a change in what readers were willing to want.

None of that makes the old material uninteresting. It makes it a different kind of object. A story about a cosmic labour arrangement tells you something concrete about how the people who told it understood power and obligation, and that is real information. It simply does not require the visitors from elsewhere.

What the Sources Will and Will Not Support

The first claim is documented. The second is documented too, though the documentation is mostly polemical, which is a genuine limit on what polemical documentation proves. The third is a modern addition, and the additions are large. Later writers supply offices, genealogies, and functions that no surviving fragment assigns to the name.

A workable rule for practice is to build from the structure the sources actually give you and to import the rest knowingly. If you want a figure who governs a boundary, the sources hand you that. If you want a figure who grants wealth, protection, or revelation, you are working from a later layer, and saying so costs you nothing. A practitioner who knows which layer each element arrived from can use it with confidence, because the uncertainty has been located rather than smeared across the whole structure.

  1. The name and the number appear in late-antique material associated with several groups that were not the same group.
  2. Those groups disagreed with one another and with orthodox Christianity about the structure of what lies above the material world.
  3. The twentieth century added a layer of interpretation that is not present in the sources, and the layer is thick.

Turning the Frame Into a Working Symbol

The charge is where most of the effort belongs. It is not a request to the figure. It is the act of making that boundary the dominant object in your attention for a defined stretch of time, so you start noticing your own crossings instead of drifting through them unnoticed. That is a behavioural mechanism, and it does not require a position on cosmology in order to function.

Draw the mark in a way that carries the meaning rather than the craft. Number can be included when the number is doing work in your construction. Resist the temptation to make it ornate, because ornament is the point at which a boundary stops encoding and starts functioning as a charm, and charms have their own logic and their own failure modes.

  1. Write the limit in one sentence, in plain words, with no myth in it at all.
  2. Give the sentence a name. The name has to be a limit, not an identity, so a word like boundary or threshold beats a word like power.
  3. Draw the mark so that it reads as an edge: a gate, a wall, a horizon, a boundary sign.
  4. State the crossing condition in one line. What has to happen for the mark to count as crossed.
  5. Charge it, then release it back into ordinary activity where you can meet it again.

The Charge in Eight Steps

The eighth step is the one people skip. The charge has to end with the mark handed back, at which point it stops being an object of contemplation and becomes a sign you can act with. A mark that never gets released accumulates meaning until it becomes unreadable, and an unreadable mark is indistinguishable from no mark at all.

  1. Sit down somewhere you can leave undisturbed for twenty minutes, and put the phone in another room.
  2. Read the original one-sentence limit out loud, the plain version with no myth attached.
  3. State the name of the boundary, once, and let the word do its work without commentary.
  4. Draw or trace the mark slowly, keeping to a single line weight if you can manage it.
  5. Say what crossing looks like in observable terms, using verbs a stranger could verify.
  6. Hold the mark in attention for a set count rather than an open-ended one, because open-ended counts never end.
  7. Break the attention deliberately rather than waiting for it to fray, and note the moment you did.
  8. Release the mark into the environment and leave it where you will meet it again this week.

Why the Frame Fails and What to Change

Three failure modes show up often enough to name. The first is identification, where the figure stops being a boundary and becomes an identity, and the practitioner then has to defend an identity, which is tiring and impossible to test. The second is escalation, where the boundary quietly grows, so a limit set on a small evening habit becomes a limit set on a whole life. The third is attribution, where a change that would have arrived anyway gets credited to the mark.

The repair for the first is linguistic and costs nothing. Describe the mark with words that keep it outside you. A boundary is where something ends. A role is a function inside a system. A limit is set. If you catch yourself drafting sentences in which the figure is the subject of a life story, the frame has leaked and the leak is the problem to fix.

The repair for the second is to keep the original sentence. Whatever the boundary was during the first hour, write that text down and leave it visible. If the mark has grown from one behaviour into a full identity inside a month, the original sentence is your diagnosis, and you can read the difference directly off the page.

The repair for the third is measurement, which the next section handles. A frame that produces results you can check is a frame worth keeping. A frame that produces results you cannot check has stopped being a tool and started being a mood.

recording the prediction in advance

The most useful thing you can do before charging anything is write a prediction that could come out false. A prediction phrased as general improvement is not falsifiable, and a falsifiable prediction written after the fact is a memory rather than a record. Both are common. The second is invisible to you at the moment you write it, which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

The format that holds up is simple: one sentence stating what will be different, a specific measure, and a date. A rate works better than a total, because totals let one strong week carry a whole month of data on its back. Date the prediction before the charge rather than after, and keep the note where you will not edit it.

Then record what happens, including the parts that make the frame look bad. Sometimes the frame will be right for reasons unconnected to the frame itself, which is still no argument against using it. It is a reason to keep the ledger, because the ledger is what tells the two situations apart.

Materials, Timing, and Room to Work

Timing matters less than people assume. A short daily practice, as the older traditions advise when they advise anything at all, beats an occasional long one by a wide margin. The attention habit forms through repetition, and repetition is a scheduling problem rather than an astrological one. Pick the smallest version you would still do on a week when everything else has gone wrong.

  • A notebook kept exclusively for this, because a shared page invites editing the record rather than keeping it.
  • A pen over a pencil, since pencil invites revision and revision invites hindsight in the written prediction.
  • A fixed location rather than a fixed time, because a place is easier to defend on a bad week than a clock time is.
  • No incense, no fabric, no expensive ink, until the plain version has proved that it survives contact with an ordinary week.

Keeping the Frame Honest Past the First Week

The first week is the easy part, because novelty is doing most of the work. Somewhere in the second or third week the contribution from novelty drops and the contribution from the practice becomes visible, which is often much quieter than anyone expects. People read that quiet stretch as failure and drop the frame at precisely the point where it would have started compounding, which is why so many practice records end abruptly around day twenty.

Three habits keep it alive. Keep the record visible rather than stored. Keep the original plain sentence in front of you. And keep one small element of the structure changing, so the practice stays mobile without turning elaborate. A frame that never changes becomes a routine, and a routine that has lost its edge is harder to notice than one you built.

A frame that survives a year of small daily work and a written record is a different sort of object from one that produced a striking first week. The first kind is worth the effort. The second is usually a story people tell about the second kind, and the story outlives the practice that was never built.

Common Errors in This Territory

The mistakes here are specific to material that is half documented and heavily reinterpreted, so they are worth listing plainly rather than leaving to be discovered.

  • Treating a hostile polemic as a neutral report, which inverts what can be concluded from it.
  • Citing the number as proof of a hidden arithmetic, when the calendrical reading is older and simpler.
  • Letting the boundary figure turn into a self-description, which is the fastest route from a useful tool to a fixed identity.
  • Building the practice on a twentieth-century reading and then treating that reading as ancient authority.
  • Collecting more source material than working steps, which feels like progress and is not.

Where This Sits Beside the Rest of Your Practice

Used this way, the material belongs with working tools that name a limit rather than with traditions that promise a protector. A sigil built from a name of boundary belongs beside a banishing form, beside a threshold mark, beside anything whose job is to end a state rather than begin one. In that company the historical uncertainty stops being a problem, because the tool is not asking you to believe anything about the past. It is asking you to notice something in the present.

The other place it fits is alongside the reading you do on other traditions. Abraxas is a good exercise in source handling precisely because the record is thin and the modern additions are thick. Practising here means practising the discipline you need everywhere else, on material where being wrong is cheap. That makes it a useful place to learn, and a poor place to build a religion.

Keep it small, keep it written down, and keep it honest about which layer each piece came from. A boundary is a modest thing to work with, and modest tools survive contact with ordinary life far better than grand ones do.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a name from a fragmentary source be used in a working practice?

Yes, provided you know which layer you are reaching for. The late-antique material gives you a figure associated with a boundary and a number. Anything you add on top arrives from later layers and should be labelled as your own construction. Naming the layer costs nothing and keeps the practice open to correction from outside.

How should I handle a source that arrives damaged?

Treat the gap as a decision point rather than a problem to paper over. Read two translations, note where they disagree, and write down what your preferred reading does with the sentence. A lacuna filled by an earlier translator often carries more of that translator's theology than of the author being translated.

Is Atlantis evidence of a lost civilisation?

It is evidence that Plato wrote two dialogues containing a story. No contemporary source records a matching place, and the identification as history appears many centuries after the text was written. As an allegory about ideal forms the story is doing genuine philosophical work, which is a considerably more interesting use of it than a drowned continent.

What is wrong with reading these texts as describing visitors from elsewhere?

Nothing about the reading is incoherent, and everything about it is unsupported. The substitution removes the ethical content of the original, which concerned makers and the people made to labour. A story about power and obligation survives the criticism intact. A story about hardware does not.

How do I keep a myth from hardening into a claim about the world?

Keep a written record with a prediction, a measure, and a date, and keep the source layer labelled on every page. If the frame is producing changes you can see, it is functioning as a tool. If it is producing certainty about the nature of reality, it has changed jobs, and that is the version to set down.
