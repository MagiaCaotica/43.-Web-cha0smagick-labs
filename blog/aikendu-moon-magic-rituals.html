What I Actually Timed By

I timed by three things, in descending order of how much they changed my results. The first is not astronomical at all: whether I had slept, and how. Nothing else in this system comes close to that variable, and every piece of timing work I have done badly traces back to ignoring it.

The second is the lunar phase, used coarsely. Four buckets, not the full set of names. Dark, waxing, full, waning. That is enough resolution for nearly every practical aim and it removes the temptation to make fine distinctions I cannot actually justify.

The third is the weekday, mapped to a planet in the traditional order. This one I use least, and my notes are fairly clear about why: by the time a working is due on a particular weekday, the timing has usually stopped being the interesting variable.

What I do not time by is the exact minute or the planetary hour, and the reason is specific rather than sceptical. I cannot tell the difference between a working done in a favourable window and one done in an unfavourable one, across far too few attempts to detect it. A discipline built on distinctions I cannot detect is a discipline that produces confidence without accuracy, which is the most expensive combination available.

The Two Effects, Pulled Apart

Lunar and planetary timing does two things, and they are so different in size that people who bundle them together end up crediting the wrong one.

The small one is a genuine increase in the number of coincidences available to you. Any working changes the world slightly, and timing it to a window where more things are already happening increases the chance that something lines up. This is a real arithmetic effect, it is tiny, and on its own it would not justify the practice.

The large one is a change in your own attention. When you commit to a specific night with a specific aim, you notice things differently for the following days. You catch an opening you would have missed. You remember to ask. You notice that a conversation went in a direction you could have used. None of that is coincidence, and all of it is much larger than the alignment effect.

The practical consequence is a reframe worth adopting. You are not improving the odds by choosing the right night. You are building a period in which you are more attentive and more committed, and attaching it to a date so that it happens. The date is a scheduling device. That is not a criticism of it; a device that reliably produces a better two weeks is doing its job, and pretending it is doing something else is what makes people over-invest in the astronomy.

Once you see it this way, the phase names stop being claims and start being prompts. A new moon is when you begin something, because a beginning is what a new moon has always meant, and the meaning is doing the work.

Where the Weekday Map Came From

The seven-day week with planets attached to its days is not ancient in the way people assume. It is a comparatively recent arrangement, developed in Babylonian astronomy and taken up in Rome, and the reason it stuck is that it let a week carry meaning rather than merely count.

The order is fixed and worth learning once, because it is arbitrary enough that guessing is useless. Saturn, then Jupiter, then Mars, then the Sun, then Venus, then Mercury, and back to Saturn for the seventh day, with the Moon often associated with Monday as a kind of addition rather than part of the original cycle.

What the mapping is good for is broad character rather than precision. A beginning is a Sun or Jupiter matter. A removal is Saturn. Communication and travel sit with Mercury. Nothing about that survives as a causal claim, and the honest use is as a mnemonic for what kind of work fits a day.

My own use has narrowed over time. I stopped treating a specific weekday as required and started treating it as slightly better than random, which is a much lower bar and the one I can actually support. If a working needs to happen and Saturday is a Mars day and the aim is a conversation, the conversation happens on Saturday. I no longer postpone it for the better window, because there usually isn't one this month.

Planetary Hours Without the Mysticism

The system splits daylight and night into twelve blocks each, with a planet ruling each block, rotating from hour to hour. It comes out of medieval astrology and it inherits the awkward consequence of that: the exact times shift with your location and with the season, so the same label points at a different span of the clock in December and in June.

Strip the framework down and what remains is a scheduling grid with twelve named slots per half-day, and that is genuinely useful for one purpose. It stops you from putting a demanding activity wherever there happens to be room in the day, and makes you notice that some activities want one kind of attention and others want another.

The complication is the calculation. Because the start time depends on sunrise and sunset at your coordinates, the labels move twice a year, and getting it wrong is easy and produces silent nonsense. My notes record one entire month of working on the wrong hour before I noticed, which is a good illustration of how little this system is checking if you do not check it.

Practical resolution: use a tool that computes the table for your location and date, and treat the resulting hour as a rough window rather than a boundary. If the computed window opens in the middle of the night, ignore it and do the work in daylight. The system is not a reason to be awake at three in the morning, and the tradition that says otherwise is a modern embellishment on a much older arithmetic exercise.

Which Phase for Which Sort of Work

The four-bucket mapping below is the part of this that I would keep if I had to discard everything else, because it converts an abstract cycle into four decisions you can make in a second.

Dark, meaning the new moon period, is for beginnings and for rest. Starting a practice, opening a project, or deliberately stopping something all belong here. The symbolism of an unlit moon and the actual behaviour of beginning something are the same shape, and using a date that matches the behaviour is the whole mechanism.

Waxing is for building. Work that needs to accumulate over weeks belongs in the two weeks after the dark period, and the further into that stretch you go the more of it is about momentum than about starting.

Full is for culmination and for visibility. This is the phase most over-used, because anything can be scheduled into a full moon and forcing an unrelated task into it produces a session that feels significant and works badly. If a thing has no relationship to completion, leave it alone.

Waning is for transformation, release and review. Ending a commitment, clearing an obligation and looking honestly at a cycle you are finishing all sit here, and this is the bucket most people skip, which is a shame because it is where the useful conclusions are available.

The Sessions Where Nothing Happened

Roughly a third of the sessions in my log produced nothing I would call a result, and they are the most informative entries in the file. I keep them for one reason: to stop the successful ones from being over-interpreted.

Looking across the failures, the pattern is not mysterious. The nulls cluster around sessions where I had slept badly, where the aim was vague, and where I had stacked a charge onto a working I had already done that week. The timing was in the correct bucket in almost all of them, which is the clearest evidence I have that the timing is doing less work than I assumed.

A second pattern is more interesting. The sessions that produced a visible change were disproportionately ones where I also changed something in my ordinary conduct that week. A different route to the same person, a conversation I had been avoiding, a habit I had stopped. I cannot prove the sigil did anything in those weeks, and I notice I am the only person with access to that information, which is exactly the situation the discipline is supposed to protect against.

So the nulls have a use beyond their own content. They are the control condition. If a session produced a result and the corresponding nulls were all produced by bad sleep and vagueness, the honest description is that the successful weeks were the well-prepared weeks. That is a less satisfying conclusion and a more defensible one.

The Focus Effect Is the Large One, and It Is Misread

The attentional effect is what actually generates results in this practice, and it is routinely credited to the wrong cause, which matters because the two imply different behaviours.

If the effect comes from the moon, then a practitioner who misses a favourable night has lost something and should wait for the next one. If it comes from committed attention, then missing a night costs a commitment, and a commitment made on an ordinary Tuesday works about as well.

My logs support the second reading and my behaviour has changed accordingly. I no longer wait. I do the work when I have a free hour, and I use the phase to decide what kind of work it is rather than whether to do it. That single change removed more friction from this practice than anything else I have done, and it cost me nothing I can detect.

There is a real risk in making this too glib. Some of the calendar's meaning is genuinely useful as a prompt, and a practice that ignores its own inherited structure tends to decay into a list of things you do. The compromise I have settled on is to keep the structure as a checklist of kinds of work and to treat the timing as a default rather than a gate.

The thing to guard against is the reverse error, which is attributing to the alignment an effect that is plainly about attention. It produces elaborate systems for choosing dates, a persistent sense that the good days are spoken for, and an accuracy about the calendar that has nothing to do with the calendar.

What I Stopped Doing

Four things went, and removing them made the practice more coherent rather than less.

I stopped observing every phase. Reading a full phase table and trying to find a sign in each one produced a lot of notes and no additional results. The phase names are traditional symbolism, not predictions, and the ones that mean something to me are four of them.

I stopped timing to the hour unless a calculation was already in front of me. The reason is the seasonal drift, and the reason for that is that wrong windows are invisible. A wrong hour gives no error message.

I stopped postponing. This was the hardest because it goes against what the practice is for. The rule now is that a working waits at most one cycle, and if the window does not arrive it happens anyway on a plain evening.

I stopped recording the astronomical data and started recording the conduct. The log now tracks what I actually did during the fortnight, not what the sky was doing. That change alone made the file legible in a way it had not been, and it is the single most useful thing in this list of four.

Notes on the Calculator

A phase calculator is worth having and worth distrusting, in that order of emphasis on the verb.

What it is genuinely good for is forward planning. Knowing that a dark period falls on a specific week lets you decide in advance that it is the week you start something, which is the only way the decision gets made with any deliberation rather than at the moment.

What it cannot do is tell you anything about whether a working will succeed, and a product that presents itself as a decision-support tool is inviting you to read a meaning into a table of dates. The dates are real. The association between a date and an outcome is yours, not the table's.

One practical caution. Calculators differ on whether they report the exact phase moment or the whole period, and the two produce different answers to the question you are asking. Pick one, use it consistently, and write down which convention you are using. Inconsistent conventions in a log generate months of confusion about whether a dark period was three days or a week.

The useful habit is to record the date and the bucket together, rather than the precise time. A log of dates and buckets stays readable for years. A log of timestamps mostly records when you remembered to open the app.

A Weekly Shape That Survives Contact

This is the arrangement I have ended up with, offered as a starting point rather than as a method, since the entire point of the preceding sections is that most of the structure is scaffolding.

The dark period is for one new commitment, chosen while still dark, which gives the beginning a date without needing the date to be meaningful. Waxing is for the work that builds on it, kept deliberately dull. The full moon is reserved for one thing that completes, and if nothing completes, it is left empty. The waning period is for finishing, clearing and reading the log.

The non-negotiable part is the log, and the useful part of the log is the conduct column. Two lines per session: what I did the week of, and what I noticed. No astronomical detail, no interpretation, no sign. Six months of that is worth more than a year of detailed celestial notes, because it is the only version that can be read by someone who is not invested in the practice succeeding.

The part to add later, once the above has run for a few cycles without needing attention, is one planetary hour per week. Not more. Use it for a single aim, keep it in daylight, and record it in the same file as everything else so it can be compared like anything else.

The whole arrangement is worth about two hours a week. If the timing practice starts costing more than that, something has been added that is doing more harm than the calendar ever promised.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a birth chart to time lunar and planetary workings?

No, and the astrology is a different discipline from the timing. A chart tells you about a specific person's configuration; the weekday and the lunar cycle are shared public clocks. What a chart would add is personalisation of the timing, and my notes do not show a reliable advantage from having one. If you already work astrologically it will fit in naturally, and if you do not, it is a barrier you do not need.

Which lunar phase should I use for money work?

Match the phase to the shape of the activity rather than looking for a lucky one. Anything about beginning, opening or setting something up belongs in the dark period, and anything about building belongs through the waxing stretch. Aiming at culmination, at visibility or at a result you need to be seen is a full-moon matter, and clearing or releasing is waning. Money specifically is usually a building activity, so the middle of the cycle fits it better than the full moon does.

What are planetary hours actually for in practice?

They give you a twelve-slot grid for each half of the day, with a theme attached to each slot, which is a way of matching a demanding activity to the kind of attention it wants rather than putting it wherever there is room. That is the useful part. The complication is that the slot boundaries move with your coordinates and the season, so the same label covers a different span of the clock at different times of year, and a wrong window gives no warning at all.

How do I know whether the timing made a difference?

You probably cannot, and the honest way to handle that is to stop separating timing from everything else and log the conduct alongside the date. The most likely explanation for results in a dated practice is that the date produced a commitment and the commitment produced attention, rather than that the date improved the odds. The one test worth running is a control: a session done with the same structure on a plain day, with no timing rationale attached, and logged in the same way.

Should I skip a working if the window is unfavourable?

Waiting costs you a commitment, and the commitment is the part that reliably produces something. A reasonable rule is to wait at most one cycle for a better window and then do the work anyway on an ordinary evening. In practice the best windows in this system are the ones you happened to be free for, and treating the calendar as a gate rather than a default is the most common way this practice quietly stops being practised.
