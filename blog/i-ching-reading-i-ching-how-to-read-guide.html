How to Read the I Ching: A Working Method for Beginners

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

The I Ching is easy to cast and hard to read. Six coin tosses produce a figure in under a minute, and most of the difficulty sits in the part that happens afterwards: deciding what the figure is actually telling you. People who bounce off the system usually get stuck at the same point, which is that the classical text is compressed, layered, and written for a reader who already knows what a hexagram is. They open a translation, find two thousand characters of poetic fragment about a lake, and conclude the system is decorative. What actually makes it usable is a method. Cast the coins, build the figure, identify the moving lines, read those lines first, and only then consult the judgment. The order matters. Most beginner mistakes come from reading the judgment before the changing lines, which throws away the most specific information the cast contains. A second thing worth saying up front: the I Ching is a thinking tool, not a sensor. It is a structured way to look at a situation from an angle you were not occupying, and the quality of what you learn from it depends on how precisely you framed the question before you tossed. That framing is the part most people skip.

What the Coins Actually Do

Three coins, tossed six times, is the standard method and it is the one worth learning first. Each coin carries two sides, traditionally one side valued three and the other two. Toss all three together, count the total, and that total tells you what kind of line you just generated.

A total of six or seven is an old, solid yang line. Five or eight is a young yang line, which means it is about to change. Seven or six is a yin line, and eight or five is a young yin line that is about to change. The young lines are the interesting ones, because they tell you the situation is in motion rather than settled.

You will see a lot of advice telling you to use a single coin three times, or to draw numbers from an app. Those are all legitimate ways of producing a figure. The three-coin method is preferred simply because the arithmetic is transparent, so you can verify the distribution yourself once you start keeping records.

  • Six or seven: solid yang line
  • Five or eight: young yang line, changing
  • Seven or six: broken yin line

Building a Hexagram From Six Throws

Six throws produce six lines, and the lines stack into a figure of two three-line blocks called trigrams. A solid line is one trigram, a broken line the other, and the arrangement of the two blocks names the hexagram. Every one of the sixty-four possibilities has a name and a number, and a complete list with one-line summaries is worth having open while you learn.

Read the lines from the bottom upward. The lowest line is the first throw, which surprises people who read left to right. The bottom of the figure represents where the situation starts, and the top represents where it is heading if nothing changes, which is why the top lines deal with the outer circumstances and the lower lines with the internal ones.

The two trigrams also carry a relationship. Traditionally each is associated with a family member, a season, a direction and a set of qualities, and reading the two together often clarifies what the hexagram name alone leaves abstract. The five elements give you a second vocabulary for the same information, and it is a fast one.

Primary and Secondary: Reading the Change

When one or more lines came out young, the figure has a second form. Flip every young line, and you get the secondary hexagram. The primary hexagram is the situation as it stands, and the secondary hexagram is the situation in motion, which is where the reading gets its tension.

Order of attention is fixed and worth memorising. Read the judgment, which is the overall shape. Then read the changing lines in order from lowest to highest, because they are the specific instruction. Then read the secondary hexagram's judgment. If there are no changing lines, there is no secondary hexagram and the reading is about a situation that is simply static, which is itself a useful answer.

Three changing lines is the busiest case and the one beginners misread. The classical guidance is to take the line in the middle position, since it is neither the beginning nor the end of the change. Before that, confirm that the lower three and upper three do not already pull in opposite directions.

Why Some Hexagrams Come Up More Often

Here is the part almost every introductory book leaves out, and it matters once you start keeping records. The three-coin method is not a uniform generator. Because coin values differ and the six throws interact, certain lines and certain hexagrams turn up more often than a naive calculation predicts.

The practical consequence is small but real: a particular hexagram appearing twice in a year is not a message. If you have not been recording your casts, the pattern is invisible and you will read significance into coincidence. Once you log them, a repeated hexagram is simply a repetition, and you can weigh it accordingly.

Working out which hexagrams are over-represented is a genuinely useful exercise for a beginner because it replaces a supernatural explanation with a mechanical one. There is a full breakdown of the distribution in our guide to three-coin probability, and reading it changes how much authority you give a dramatic figure.

Asking a Question the Hexagram Can Answer

The single biggest improvement in a new reader's results comes from writing the question down before casting. Not asking a question, writing it. A specific written question produces a specific figure; a vague worry produces a figure that fits twenty different worries equally well.

The productive register is the present tense. There is a real difference between asking about a decision you are making this week and asking about an outcome two years out. The first has information you do not have and can act on, the second is mostly a way of being anxious with extra steps.

One question per cast. Multiple questions in a single reading is the most common error in the first six months, and the resulting figure ends up meaning the average of several unrelated concerns, which is nothing at all.

Recording Readings So the Sequence Teaches You

A single reading tells you very little. A sequence of readings, kept properly, shows you which hexagrams you keep meeting, which lines keep moving, and which of your question types produces the same figure every time. That last one is frequently the most informative thing in the whole record.

Log four things per cast: the date, the written question, the primary hexagram with its changing lines, and a sentence on what you decided. Without the last field the journal is a record of figures. With it, it becomes a record of decisions, and you can audit yourself honestly years later.

This is the single clearest case in the whole practice where software earns its keep. The casting is easy by hand and takes seconds. The record-keeping is what takes discipline, because it is dull and it is the part that produces all the learning.

Where the Meaning Comes From

Translations of the judgments vary in quality by an enormous margin, and most beginners do not realise how much of their confusion comes from the translation rather than from the system. Look for renderings that preserve ambiguity instead of resolving it, because the original text is genuinely uncertain and a confident smooth translation has usually invented something.

Learn the names and the line statements before you learn the philosophical glosses. The judgment of Hexagram 1 or Hexagram 29 is not going to tell you anything useful on a first encounter. The names form a vocabulary, and once you have the vocabulary the images start doing work on their own.

The philosophical tradition layered on top is worth reading separately and on its own terms. Reading the judgments as divination and reading them as a philosophical text are two different activities, and mixing them produces readings that try to be advice and insight at once and end up being neither.

Common Mistakes in a First Year

Reading the judgment before the changing lines. The judgment is the least specific part of the cast, and starting there is why first readings feel so generic.

Asking about other people' motives. The system is a thinking tool, and it has nothing to say about what someone else is thinking. It will happily generate a figure about a situation you have no information on, which is how beginners end up confident and wrong.

Treating an inconvenient figure as a mistake in the casting. Re-rolling until you like the result destroys the only property that makes the method useful.

Stopping after the first unmoving hexagram. A figure with no changing lines is a legitimate and often blunt answer, and treating it as a dud reading is why some people conclude the system is unreliable after a handful of casts.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many coins do I need for an I Ching reading?

Three is the traditional number, tossed together six times, once for each line. A single coin tossed three times per line also works and is easier to carry, but the three-coin method has the advantage that you can verify its probability distribution yourself, which is worth knowing once you start keeping records of what comes up.

What does a changing line actually mean in the I Ching?

A changing line is a young yin or young yang, and it tells you that particular part of the situation is in motion rather than settled. Flip all the changing lines and you get the secondary hexagram, which describes where things are heading. Read the changing lines first, in order from the bottom, because they carry the most specific instruction in the whole figure.

Should I ask the I Ching about the future?

Ask about the present, and specifically about a decision you can make this week. Future-tense questions mostly reflect current anxiety, and the figure generated from them tends to be read as reassurance or doom depending on your mood that day. A written, present-tense, single question produces a reading you can actually use.

Why does the same hexagram keep appearing?

Because some hexagrams are genuinely more probable under the three-coin method, and because you may be framing similar situations in similar language. The three-coin distribution is not uniform, so repetition is expected rather than meaningful. Log your casts and you will see the pattern is arithmetic, which is more interesting than it first appears.

Does an app make it easier to cast and read the I Ching?

Casting is not the bottleneck. Three coins in your hand is faster than any app, and the interpretation is a library rather than a function. What software genuinely improves is the journal, because the record of past readings is what turns a series of unrelated casts into a self-audit that teaches you how you actually ask questions.
