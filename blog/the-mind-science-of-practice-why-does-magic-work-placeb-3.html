The Mind Science of Practice

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

There is a reason magic works differently from how it is usually explained, and the reason is not hidden. It is that the person doing it is an instrument, and instruments behave in ways that are well documented. The claims built on top of that are a mixture of accurate observation, reasonable speculation and confident nonsense, and separating them is not difficult once you know which is which. This is an attempt at that separation, organised around the mechanisms that actually have evidence behind them, the ones that are plausible but unestablished, and the ones that do not survive inspection. It is written for someone who wants to practise accurately rather than to win an argument, and it does not require you to conclude anything metaphysical at the end.

The instrument is you, and that is not a small point

Almost every debate in this area assumes the interesting variable is on the receiving end. In practice the most reliable effects run through the person performing the working, which is a much better studied object. Attention, expectation, posture, breathing rate, sleep, caffeine and stress all move measurable quantities, and all of them are under your partial control during a thirty minute ritual.

This reframes the question. Instead of asking what a working does to the world, you can ask what it does to you, and that question has a research literature, a measurement methodology and a substantial set of known limitations. It is also a question whose answers you can use, since the autonomic effects are modest, reliable and largely beneficial.

Belief is not the only variable

Open-label placebo work complicates the account in which all effect runs through belief. In some conditions, telling someone what they are receiving produces a measurable physiological change; in others it does not, and the difference is not well explained. What this rules out is the simple version in which belief is a necessary condition. It does not rule out expectancy, attention, conditioning or context, all of which are ordinary and none of which require anyone to believe anything supernatural.

The practical consequence is that you cannot design an experiment with belief as a controlled variable, because you cannot blind yourself. That is a structural limitation rather than a personal failing, and it is why writing the outcome specification before you start matters more than any technique for improving your results. An external checkable outcome is partly immune, because wanting something cannot manufacture a phone call that did not arrive.

Attention as hypothesis management

Predictive processing treats perception as hypothesis management. The brain maintains a model of the world and updates it from incoming signal, and expectations are part of that update rather than a layer applied to a finished picture. Under that account, an expectation changing what you notice requires no conscious self-deception, which is convenient, because conscious self-deception is very difficult to do reliably and is the mechanism most people assume when they explain their own results.

Whether the framework is complete is a live question and not one that matters much here. What matters is the operational consequence. Expectation is not a force acting on reality from a distance; it is a feature of how attention is allocated, and attention is observable in yourself. That is enough to work with, and it is also enough to explain a body of practice results without positing anything beyond the practitioner.

The autonomic signature, which is the solid part

Ritual reliably produces measurable autonomic change. Heart rate variability tends to fall, which is what sustained attentional effort does. Respiration slows and deepens. Muscle tension rises in the hands, jaw and shoulders. Skin conductance shifts with the emotional tone of what is being imagined. None of this is specific to occult practice and none of it requires belief, since the identical pattern appears in any activity with a fixed posture and a held attention.

The magnitudes are modest and the directions are favourable, which is why ritual is a reasonable anxiety intervention even for a committed sceptic. What it is not is a mechanism for producing external events. The two claims get confused constantly, and keeping them apart is the most useful thing you can do with this material. The autonomic effect is real and modest. The external effect is the part that needs a specification and a deadline.

Where conditioning does the work

Some results that feel like magic are straightforwardly habituation. A sequence performed at the same time on the same days acquires a conditioned response, and the anticipation itself produces a state change before anything is done. This is a well characterised phenomenon and it accounts for a great deal of the felt power of a long-established practice, including the confidence and the sense of being in a particular state at the moment of performing.

It also explains why practices feel stronger over time without becoming more effective. The added effect is in your nervous system, not in the aim. This is not a criticism, since the conditioned state is often exactly what is wanted, but it does change what you are optimising. If you are conditioning yourself into a useful state, a long daily practice is the right design. If you are trying to affect something outside yourself, conditioning is not doing the work and the specification is what matters.

The arithmetic problem

The deck used in the classic telepathy experiments has five symbols, so a guess is right one time in five. That single number is the whole lesson, and it is the reason those experiments are still taught. Results landing at twenty per cent are fully explained by the arithmetic. Results landing at ninety per cent across thousands of trials are not explained by anything else on offer.

Most everyday claims about intuition and premonition are ambiguous between the two cases, and the way to tell them apart is counting rather than argument. Ask what chance would produce. Ask how many attempts were needed to get there, because a hundred tries will produce a striking run and the person who tried a hundred times will remember the last twenty. This is a discipline rather than a technique, and it is the difference between a claim that can be tested and a claim that can only be felt.

What ritual time actually does

Instructions that specify durations in candles, repetitions or tides rather than minutes are not making a metaphysical claim about time. They are structuring a fixed-length state, which is the only reliable way to hold one. A person told to burn a candle for one hour has been given a container, and most of the effect comes from the container rather than from anything about the hour.

Elapsed time gets reconstructed rather than filed away, which means the sense of how long a working took is reconstructed afterwards and is quite unreliable. Set a timer, not a mental estimate, and record the actual figure in your log. People routinely find their workings are much longer than they believed, and that discovery changes how they design the next one.

The failure modes in the research

Five problems account for most of what has gone wrong, and they are worth knowing because they apply to your own practice as well. If you test one aim on a good day and declare victory, you have a small sample and a flexible outcome. If you have no baseline you cannot distinguish a change from a fluctuation. If you only record the workings that did something, you have built a machine for producing consistent positive results from noise.

The last two are the ones that are hardest to notice, because selective reporting and practitioner data are exactly what a personal log is. This is the strongest practical argument for writing things down at the time rather than reconstructing them. The person reconstructing a year of workings is not the same person who lived them, and the difference shows up systematically in the direction of flattering.

  • Small samples with a large number of variables tested, so a positive result is expected by chance.
  • Flexible outcome definitions chosen after the data is seen.
  • No baseline, so ordinary fluctuation is read as change.
  • Selective reporting, which the published literature on this problem makes no longer surprising.
  • Practitioner data, recorded by people with an interest in the answer.

What survives contact with this

The defensible position is small and sufficient. A committed practice reliably changes your internal state, and it does so through mechanisms that are ordinary and well documented. Whether a given working also changed anything outside your body is an empirical question, answerable only per working, with a written specification and a date. Nothing in the research either requires you to believe something or forbids you from doing the practice.

The uncomfortable part is that this position will not tell you whether to continue. Nobody outside your own head is going to make that decision for you, and nobody inside is going to be objective about it. The most that any amount of research can do is keep your records honest, and the most that any amount of personal testimony can do is tell you whether the practice is currently worth your time. Both of those are answerable. The rest is not, and pretending otherwise is what makes this area exhausting.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know a result is real?

Write the outcome in a form that could be wrong, before you start, and check it on a date you set in advance. Belief is not controllable in your own case, so the specification has to be external and checkable by someone who was not there. A filed document, a phone call and a date are all hard to argue with. A feeling of having worked is the wrong instrument, since it is precisely what the practice is supposed to be moving.

Does ritual help with anxiety?

The autonomic changes are real, reasonably consistent and modestly beneficial, and they do not require belief. Fixed posture, held attention, a counted duration and a marked ending produce predictable physiological settling. What is not supported is any claim that ritual reaches outward and changes external conditions. Those two effects are easy to conflate and separating them is what makes the practice useful rather than merely comforting.

Is magic just placebo?

The question assumes belief is the only possible mechanism, and the placebo account is more complicated than the usual summary. Expectancy, attention, conditioning and context all contribute, none requires conscious belief, and ritual produces measurable physiological change independent of what anyone believes. You cannot claim an external effect you have not measured. The discipline of writing the specification first is what keeps you honest about which kind of claim you are making.

Is a paid tool worth it for a practice log?

A place to keep the log, which is the one part that is genuinely hard to do by hand over years. The procedure itself needs no software: a notebook, a timer and a willingness to write down a failure. A tool is worth having if it holds every working with its date, its intent and the outcome you recorded, so that six months of them can be read side by side. Avoid anything that interprets your results, because interpretation is where honesty is lost.

Why do the effects feel stronger after years of practice?

Because conditioning is doing part of the work, and it accumulates. A sequence performed at the same time on the same days acquires a conditioned response, and the anticipation of it produces a state change before anything happens. That explains the confidence and the sense of already being in the right state, and it means the practice can feel more powerful over time without becoming more effective. Whether that is a problem depends on what you are optimising for.
