CLAUNECK

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

Clauneck appears in the Goetia catalogue as a duke of the arts and sciences, credited with teaching the liberal arts and with working out the motion of the stars. That office has a property none of the other offices share: a claim about knowledge can be reviewed in a way a claim about wealth or status cannot. Anyone can open the book and check whether it says what the catalogue says it says. So the useful way to work with this figure is not to request a favour. It is to take the posture the catalogue already describes, which is that you are the one doing the reading and the reading is the actual labour. This piece is a reference frame for that work. It gives you a triage table, four questions to ask before you start a text, and a sheet layout that has survived a few years of use without flattering the reader.

The Office Is the Reading

Clauneck is listed among the dukes, and the domain attached to him is knowledge itself: the liberal arts, writing, the stars, and the crafts that sit under a teacher. Nothing in that description is exotic. It is the domain of the person who reads the difficult book and then has to say what was in it.

That framing has a practical consequence. If you want a favour from this figure, the favour is a better question. A better question is something you can act on this evening, on paper, without equipment and without an audience. Most people asking a spirit for hidden knowledge actually want to avoid the work of reading. The request is comfortable because it implies that the knowledge will be delivered rather than assembled.

A Triage Table Instead of a Canon

The single most common error in this area is the hunt for one definitive book. Practitioners spend a year looking for the volume that explains everything, and they do not read what they already own. The tradition is not singular. It is a set of overlapping traditions, most of which were already overlapping when they were written, and several of which were assembled by people with no access to each other's work.

A triage table sorts candidate texts into four columns: what the text claims about its own origin, what can actually be established about that origin, what the text is useful for, and how much of it deserves rereading. Almost every book improves when you fill the second column honestly, because almost every book overstates the first one.

The table is not a ranking. It is a routing decision. A text that cannot survive the second column may still be worth an hour, provided you know in advance that you are reading it for texture rather than for data.

The Enoch Question Will Not Go Away

One item recurs in every conversation about suppressed material, so it deserves a direct treatment. The Book of Enoch counts among the oldest fragments of Jewish apocalyptic literature: preserved among the Dead Sea Scrolls, quoted by several early church fathers, and eventually dropped from most modern Bibles. The transmission history is documented, the manuscript evidence is real, and the exclusion from later canon lists is a historical decision rather than a suppression in the dramatic sense.

What that means for reading is straightforward. You can read Enoch as a genuine second-century text with real theological content and real value as history of the period. You cannot read it as a leaked archive of forbidden knowledge, because the evidence points to a book that people wrote, argued over, and eventually excluded from a particular canon for particular reasons.

This is the cleanest available demonstration of the discipline. The text is interesting. The framing that flattens it into a scandal is the part that fails.

What a Skeptic Does to a Claim

Any provenance story survives the same three questions. Who wrote it, and when? How do we know that? Who was in a position to know, and why were they not in a position to publish?

Liber Null and psi passes the first two cleanly. Peter Carroll, released in 1977, is a short book with an author and a date that anyone can check in a library catalogue. It is thin on apparatus, which is why it reads fast, and it remains the most-used entry point into modern chaos practice for exactly that reason.

The Kybalion fails the first question. It was published in 1908 with no named author, and the claim that it transmits an ancient Hermetic teaching is unsupported by anything that would let you check it. That does not make it useless. It makes it a modern text of unknown origin, and you should read it that way. The material on mentalism is serviceable. The provenance story attached to it is decorative.

The Precedence Problem

Fiction sits in the middle of this shelf and gets handled badly in both directions. Readers who enjoy strange fiction dismiss the whole category as noise and lose the fact that a story is sometimes where a writer puts a working model of a worldview.

The Necronomicon and the culture built around it are a case in point. The stories are inconsistent with each other and frequently inconsistent with the mythos they invoke, which is a literary property rather than a defect. What they encode, clearly, is a particular set of attitudes toward knowledge, inheritance, and the cost of looking. Read that way they are informative. Read as a grimoire they are decorative.

The rule that keeps this clean: a text that asks to be believed about the world gets checked against the world, and a text that asks to be enjoyed or studied for structure gets read for structure. Applying the first test to the second kind of book is how people talk themselves into nonsense.

The Four-Column Sheet

The most durable tool I have found is a sheet with four columns and no ambitions. Column one holds the text, its author, and its date as claimed. Column two holds what can be established, with the source of the claim. Column three holds one sentence on what the text is good for. Column four holds the number of passages worth coming back to.

Column four is the one people abandon and the one that carries the weight. A book with three marked passages is a book you understood. A book with none usually means you read it in a mood rather than in a method, which is a pleasant afternoon and produces nothing usable.

Fill the sheet in one sitting while the book is still open. Adding to it months later is where self-flattery creeps in, because memory converts unclear passages into important ones.

Reading Against Your Own Conclusion

If you reach the same conclusion the text reached, you have learned very little. The exercise worth doing is to write the strongest case against your reading before you write your own, and then to check whether any of it lands.

This is slow and slightly uncomfortable, which is the point. Most of the pleasure in this material comes from recognition rather than from discovery, and recognition is cheap because it agrees with you already. A reading that has survived an attempt at its own defeat is at least defensible, and defensible is what you need if you are going to build anything on it.

Keep the counter-argument. If you cannot write one, you have not read the material, you have joined it.

How Many Before You Write

Three is enough to start producing, and the third is where the first two start to look different than they did.

One book gives you a voice with no argument. Two give you an argument with no comparison. The third is the first point at which you can tell which of the earlier claims are the author's and which are the field's, because now you have a second author disagreeing in print. That disagreement is the actual material, and you cannot reach it by reading more of the same author.

After that, additional books mostly buy precision. They are worth buying occasionally and not worth accumulating.

A Verdict You Can Defend Later

Clauneck governs a domain that rewards the person who does the reading and has little patience for the person who skips it. That is an unfashionable position in a market that sells discovery, and it is also the one that produces anything.

The defensible version of this work looks unspectacular. You have a sheet. You have three books you have actually finished. You have a list of passages you can quote without looking. You have one text you distrust and can say precisely why. None of that is worth much in a conversation about hidden knowledge, and all of it is worth something when you want to build a practice on top of a claim rather than on top of a mood.

That is the whole office. The stars move, the liberal arts get taught, and neither of those happens to you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which book on hidden knowledge should I actually start with?

Start with the shortest thing that a named person wrote in a year you can check, and read it in one sitting. Liber Null and psi fits that description and takes an evening. Use length as the first filter here. Most of the long books in this category run long for reasons unconnected to whether the material is any good. If you want a second, read one book written to disagree with the first.

Is the Kybalion genuine Hermetic tradition?

Its provenance does not support that claim. It appeared in 1908 without a named author, and nothing in the historical record lets you trace it to an older Hermetic source. The manual itself is still usable as a modern text about mental models, and reading it that way costs you nothing except the attractive story that used to come attached to it.

How can I tell whether a grimoire is worth my time?

Fill in the author, date, and where those claims come from. Then write one sentence on what the book is good for and one on who it is not for. If you cannot write the second sentence, you have picked something that makes promises instead of delivering content. A title that only offers to reveal things you are not ready for is a sales pitch, and the four-column sheet is the fastest way to tell the two apart.

Does knowing which texts are real take all the pleasure out of it?

It does the opposite, though the shift is uncomfortable for a while. The pleasure of a fake provenance story depends on not checking, so it dies the moment you check and something more durable replaces it: you know which parts of a text are load-bearing, and you can build on those. The material is more interesting once it stops being a rumour with a cover price.

Why does nobody agree on which grimoires are important?

Because the people writing about importance are usually arguing about method rather than content, and each method protects its own texts. A ceremonial practitioner rates a book by how well it supports the rites. A chaos practitioner rates it by whether it can be used without a tradition attached. A historian rates it by what it can prove. All three are coherent, and they produce different shortlists from the same shelf.
