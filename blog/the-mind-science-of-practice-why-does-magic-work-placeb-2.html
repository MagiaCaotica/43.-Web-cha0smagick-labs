The Mind Science of Practice

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

The question behind this material is not whether magic works in some grand sense. It is narrower and much more answerable: when you perform a working, what exactly changed, and how would you know? That question is uncomfortable, because most people who do workings have not asked it and would rather not. This is a procedure for asking it anyway. It moves in order, from defining the result, through a baseline, a schedule and a review, to the point where an honest conclusion is available. It assumes you have a practice and some scepticism about your own reporting, which is the combination that makes any of this worth doing. None of the steps are difficult and none of them require any belief at all, which is the second reason this is worth doing separately.

Step one, defining what the result would be

Write down the outcome in a form that could be wrong. I will feel calmer by Friday is not an outcome, because there is no observation that would contradict it. I will have made the call about the second property by Thursday at noon is an outcome, because someone could check. The test is simple: could a friend look at what happened and say that did not occur.

Most working specifications fail this test, and the failure is what makes the results unfalsifiable later. If the aim was a clearer mind and you feel clear on a good day, you have confirmed it. If nothing happened you cannot point to, you have not disproved it either. Spend ten minutes on this step and the rest of the procedure becomes possible; skip it and everything downstream will produce numbers that mean nothing.

Step two, a baseline you can compare against

You need to know what the situation looks like when you are not working on it. Seven days of noting sleep length, one thing you did and one thing you avoided, and a one-word rating of your own state each evening is enough. It is unglamorous and it is the only thing that will let you distinguish an effect from a good week.

A baseline also does something less obvious. It converts a vague sense that things have not been working into a specific quantity, and quantities can be compared. People are usually surprised by how little difference the seven days make, and that surprise is itself information. If your baseline is already stable, you have a clean test. If it swings wildly, any result you get from a single working will be swamped by noise.

Step three, the smallest version that could do something

Choose a working proportionate to the aim. A large multi-stage ceremony for a small request introduces so many variables that you will never learn which part mattered. One glyph, one charge, one dismissal, a duration decided in advance, and nothing else happening that week. That is the whole design.

Proportionality also protects you from the thing that makes casual experiment impossible, which is that rituals are enjoyable and enjoyable things get repeated regardless of results. A small working can be abandoned without loss, which means the experiment is genuinely falsifiable in practice and not just in principle. Keep the ceremony for the aims that deserve it and use small workings to find out what works.

Step four, a schedule that survives a real week

The schedule is the part that determines whether you get any data at all. Three charges across a week is a realistic number for a person with a job, and three is enough to look at. Seven is the number people plan with and it collapses around the third, which means the record has two charges and a gap, and a gap is indistinguishable from a result.

Keeping the time of day fixed is a small discipline with a large effect, because it removes the main nuisance variable in any self-tracking. It also makes the record legible later. A log with consistent timestamps can be compared with a log of moods or sleep or anything else you were measuring, and a log without them cannot.

  1. Pick the days before you look at the calendar, not after.
  2. Choose three rather than seven, since three will actually happen.
  3. Write the days down where you will see them without having to remember.
  4. Do the charge at the same time of day rather than whenever is convenient.
  5. Do nothing else different on those days, and note it if you do.

Step five, the review and the arithmetic

Set the review date when you set the working, and treat it as binding. On that date, compare the outcome against the specification you wrote in step one, not against how you feel about the working. Did the thing you defined as the result occur, on or before the deadline, in a form a sceptical friend would accept? Three answers are possible and all three are fine.

If you allow yourself a vague yes, you will produce positive results forever and learn nothing. The question is binary on purpose. The binary is what makes three data points worth having, and three data points is what separates a person who has tested something from a person who has performed something several times.

Step six, recording the nulls

Write down every run that did not produce the specified outcome, including the runs where you were certain something happened and it did not. A log with only successes cannot answer the only question anyone ever asks a log, which is whether any of this does anything.

The temptation to soften a null is strong and mostly unconscious. It arrives as a more careful description of the result rather than as a decision to lie, so it is worth having a rule. Write the outcome line before the interpretation line, and do not read the first one again until the second is finished. That ordering is cheap and it is more effective than any amount of good intention.

The open-label problem

Open-label placebo studies complicate the account in which all effect runs through belief. In some conditions, telling people what they are receiving produces a measurable physiological change, and in other conditions it does not, and the difference is not yet well explained. What it rules out is the simple version. It does not rule out expectancy, context, attention or conditioning, all of which are ordinary and all of which operate without anyone believing anything supernatural.

The practical consequence for a practitioner is that you cannot design an experiment in which belief is a controlled variable, because you cannot blind yourself. This is a structural limit, not a personal failing, and it is why the outcome definitions in the earlier steps matter so much. An external checkable outcome is partly immune to the problem, because believing harder about it cannot manufacture a phone call that did not arrive.

A mechanism nobody has to believe in

Predictive processing treats perception as hypothesis management. The brain generates a model of the world and updates it with incoming signals, and expectations participate in that update process rather than merely colouring a finished image. Under that account, an expectation changing what you experience requires no conscious self-deception at all, which is convenient, because conscious self-deception is very hard to do reliably.

Whether or not the framework is complete, it makes a practical point. Expectation is not a separate force acting on reality, it is a feature of how attention is allocated, and attention is something you can observe in yourself. That is enough to work with. It also means the sceptical position, which asks you to believe nothing, is not obviously the only honest one, since the mechanism does not require the supernatural and the practitioner does not have to be a sceptic to use it.

What ritual reliably does to the body

This is the part of the subject with the best evidence behind it. Ritual reliably produces measurable autonomic change. Heart rate variability tends to fall, which usually accompanies sustained attentional effort. Respiration changes, often slowing and deepening. Muscle tension rises in the hands and jaw. None of this is mysterious and none of it is individual to occult practice, since the same pattern shows up in any activity requiring a fixed posture and a held attention.

The magnitude is modest and the direction is favourable, which is why ritual is a reasonable anxiety intervention even for someone who holds no supernatural belief. What it is not is a mechanism for producing external events. Conflating the two is the central error in this whole area, and keeping them apart is the single most useful thing a practitioner can do with the research literature.

The Zener deck as an arithmetic lesson

The deck used in the classic telepathy experiments has five symbols, so a guess has a twenty per cent chance of being right. That number is the entire point of the example. When results land near twenty per cent across many trials, the arithmetic explains the data completely and no further hypothesis is required. When results land at ninety per cent across thousands of trials, no plausible explanation remains except the effect itself.

Most everyday claims about intuition and premonition are ambiguous between those two cases, and the way to tell them apart is arithmetic rather than argument. Count the outcomes. Ask what chance would produce. Ask how many attempts were needed to get there, because a hundred tries will produce a striking run by chance and a person who tried a hundred times will remember the last twenty. This is the discipline that separates a claim that can be tested from a claim that can only be felt.

When the honest conclusion is nothing

The likely outcome of a first serious test is that it fails, and that is not a disaster. It is the test working. Most things people try once and never examine would not survive being written down beforehand and checked afterwards, and finding that out early is worth more than two years of comfortable ambiguity.

There are honest next steps after a null. Run it again with a different specification, since a poorly specified aim is a common cause. Run it again with the aim you actually cared about, which is often not the aim you wrote down. Or stop, and keep the log. A documented null is a real result and it is much more valuable than an unexamined habit.

A protocol you can run on one question

The whole protocol is a couple of hours of writing spread across a fortnight, and it produces something most working logs never do, which is an answer. The answer is usually no, and a no with a good specification behind it is a far better position than an unexamined maybe, because you can then adjust the specification and try again with the benefit of having actually tested something.

If the answer is yes, the same protocol tells you how much to trust it, which is the other half of the work. One success out of three is a coin. Three out of three is worth investigating, provided the specification was written in advance and the schedule was fixed. Both of those provisions are the entire scientific content, and they are the part people skip.

  1. One sentence outcome, checkable by someone who was not there.
  2. Seven days of baseline notes, evening rating, nothing elaborate.
  3. One small working, three times, on days chosen in advance.
  4. Nothing else deliberately changed, and any slip recorded.
  5. Review on a fixed date with a binary pass or fail, written down.
  6. A log line for every run, successes and failures, before any comment.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know whether a result is real?

You write the result down in a form that could be wrong, before you start, and then check it on a date you set in advance. Belief is not controllable in your own case, so the specification has to be external and checkable by someone who was not there. A phone call, a filed document and a date are all difficult to argue with. A feeling of having worked is not, and it is the wrong instrument because it is what the practice is supposed to be moving.

Does ritual practice actually help with anxiety?

The autonomic changes involved are real, reasonably consistent and modestly beneficial, and they do not require you to believe anything. Fixed posture, held attention, a counted duration and a marked ending produce predictable physiological settling. What is not supported is any claim that ritual reaches outward and changes external conditions. Keeping those two effects apart is what makes the practice useful rather than merely comforting.

Is magic just placebo?

That question assumes belief is the only possible mechanism, and the placebo account is more complicated than the version usually repeated. Expectancy, attention, conditioning and context are all in play, none of them require conscious belief, and ritual produces measurable physiological change that does not depend on what anyone believes. What you cannot do is claim an external effect you have not measured, and the discipline of writing the specification first is what keeps you honest about which one you are claiming.

What if the result is ambiguous?

Then your specification was too loose and you should tighten it before testing again. Ambiguity is the normal failure mode of self-experiment, because the outcome is chosen by the person recording it and their judgement drifts toward the conclusion they wanted. A fixable example is a mood or energy aim, which almost always looks positive afterwards. Rewriting the specification to name an observable event is nearly always better than arguing with the ambiguous result.

What is the paid upgrade for this kind of practice actually buying?

The record, and nothing else. The procedure is a notebook, a timer and a willingness to write down a failure. What a tool is genuinely good for is holding the log so you can put six months of workings side by side and see the pattern, including the ones where the same specification produced nothing three times running. Anything offering to interpret your results for you should be avoided, since interpretation is the step where honesty is lost.
