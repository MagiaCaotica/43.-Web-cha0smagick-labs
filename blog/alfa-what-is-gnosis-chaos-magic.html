ALFA

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Gnosis is one of the most flattering words in the vocabulary and one of the least examined. It promises a shift in how you see things, and the promise is the problem, because a shift you have been told to expect will be recognised whether or not it occurred. The version of this practice that survives contact with an honest record is much smaller than the version sold. It is not a technique for changing reality by conviction. It is a way of organising attention, testing a claim, and writing down what happened in a form that a sceptic could read. This is the beginner's route: one short exercise, one record with a column you refuse to fill, and a month of running it before you decide anything.

What the word promises, and what it delivers

The popular framing treats gnosis as a permanent alteration of perception, after which previously invisible things become obvious. That framing is unfalsifiable from the inside, because the person who has not had the experience will never recognise it, and the person who has will recognise it in almost any favourable week. A practice built on a claim like that cannot be evaluated by the person running it, which is a structural problem rather than a personal one.

What can be evaluated is smaller and more durable. You can specify in advance what you will do, write down what you expect, run the exercise for a set period, and then compare the record against the expectation. The result will not be enlightenment. It will be a short, dated set of observations that either line up with a prediction or do not, and that is enough to tell you whether to keep the method, change the aim, or drop it.

The parabola, and why it is the load-bearing idea

The central idea in the early theoretical writing is the parabola: a magical operation has no reality of its own and serves whatever purpose it is applied to. It sounds like a hedge, and it is usually quoted as one, but its practical consequence is large. If the operation has no fixed meaning, then the meaning is supplied entirely by the aim you wrote and the context you built around it, which means the design of the exercise matters more than the ritual objects.

Read constructively, the parabola is a warning against vagueness. An operation that means whatever you need it to mean will confirm whatever you currently believe, which is a reliable way to feel certain and learn nothing. The alternative is to make the aim narrow, the timing fixed, and the outcome written in advance. Then the operation can fail, and a failure you can produce is worth more than a success you cannot.

Expectancy, and the small size of the effect

Expectation has measurable influence on some things. Reported pain is the clearest case: what a person expects to feel changes what they report feeling, reliably enough that researchers have to control for it in every trial. Certain physiological responses shift too, particularly those tied to arousal. None of this touches the existence of external objects, and the gap between a real effect on pain and an imagined effect on events is enormous.

The practical consequence is that expectancy-based practice has a real ceiling and you can find it quickly. If your exercise involves wishing for an outcome, the expectancy channel can colour your perception of the outcome without changing it, which is exactly the configuration where a confirmation engine starts. If your exercise involves attention, effort, and a decision, expectancy is not the main mechanism and there is much less room for it to contaminate the record.

Where the confirmation engine starts

The engine has three moving parts, and any practice that rewards all three will seem effective regardless of mechanism. It rewards noticing successes, because successes are vivid and recent. It does not punish ignoring failures, because failures are easy to explain away as timing, mood, or an imperfect charge. And it lets the aim drift, so a disappointing result can always be reclassified as having been about something other than what you wrote down.

The way to keep all three from running is structural rather than moral. Fix the aim in writing before the month starts. Record failures in the same breath as successes, with the same detail. And set a date at which the exercise ends, so that the drift cannot continue indefinitely under the heading of still learning. A rule you follow because you wrote it down before you were disappointed is worth a great deal more than a rule you are currently trying to remember.

A first exercise, in four lines

Choose one question you can actually answer, not one about the world at large. Something like whether a particular pattern holds across the next thirty observations, or whether a specific habit you control shows up on most days. Write the question as a yes-or-no with a date attached. Commit to a fixed, short period of attention: the same ten minutes daily, or a fixed count of observations, and no variation once it starts.

That is the whole exercise. The four lines are the question, the period, the daily or per-observation commitment, and the date it ends. Notice what is absent: no new vocabulary, no initiatory framing, no requirement that anything be felt during the process, and no permission to change the question. The absence is the design, because every addition is a place where the record can quietly stop meaning what it said on the first day.

The record, and the column you must not fill

Keep the record in a plain list: date, one line of what happened, and a yes, no, or unclear. Then leave a fourth column physically present and permanently empty, headed something like reasons this might be wrong. The temptation will be to fill it, especially in the middle of a promising stretch, and the reason the column must stay empty is that your explanations at that point are not evidence.

The empty column is not a display of rigour. It is a physical reminder that a favourable run needs an explanation and an unfavourable run does not get one. If you want to write why a particular result might be chance, do it in a separate document at the end of the month, when the emotional temperature is lower and you have the whole run in front of you rather than only its most recent and most flattering part.

Reading calm as proof

The most common beginner error is treating a good mood during or after the exercise as the result. Calm is real, it is worth having, and it is also the state in which almost any belief about oneself feels plausible. Because a favourable mood raises confidence in whatever you are currently thinking, the same quiet sitting that confirms a working can equally confirm a doubt, and neither reading carries any information about the aim you wrote.

The way out is to grade the record on the criterion you set in advance and nothing else. If the criterion was a count, a yes-or-no, a schedule followed, or a specific choice made, then a wonderful week where the criterion was not met is a failed week. Keeping the grade tied to the pre-committed criterion is what makes the practice a test rather than a mood, and it is the whole difference between a record and an anecdote.

What a paradigm shift is not

It is not a change in what is available to perception, however convincingly the change feels from the inside. It is not a reinterpretation of past events in which everything that happened now means something it did not mean before. It is not an improvement in judgement, and it is not the acquisition of information you did not previously have access to.

It is closer to a change in the questions you find yourself asking and the criteria you find yourself applying, sustained over enough time and across enough situations that other people can notice it. That version is slower, less impressive, and far more likely to be real. It also has the advantage that you can check it: ask someone who knew you six months ago whether anything is different, and treat their answer as more informative than your own impression on a good evening.

The beginner's version of doubt

Beginners tend to be offered two symmetrical errors. The first is credulity, where a strong feeling during an exercise is taken as confirmation of an external claim. The second is reflexive dismissal, where any practice that does not produce an immediate measurable result is treated as worthless. Both mistakes come from grading the practice on the wrong scale, and both are corrected by the same move: specify the result in advance and grade against the specification.

Doubt that arrives after a disappointing week is useful, because it points at a real weakness in the aim, usually vagueness. Doubt that arrives before you start is not a finding, it is a mood, and it deserves no more weight than the confidence that would have replaced it. A practice worth keeping can survive a bad month. A practice that only survives a good month is not a practice, and the bad month is how you find that out early and cheaply.

Running the exercise again with a different aim

Once a cycle has closed, the second run should change one element and keep the rest. Keep the length of the period, the recording method, and the discipline around the empty column, and change only the aim. This is what turns a series of disconnected attempts into an accumulating practice, because the method stays constant and the only variable is the question.

There is a good reason not to change more than one thing. If the aim, the schedule, the record, and the grading all shift at once, a good result and a bad result are both uninterpretable, because you have no way of knowing which change mattered. One variable per cycle is slow and slightly tedious, and it is the difference between a practice that teaches you something and a practice that generates a memorable month every so often.

When to keep the record and stop the practice

There are two separate decisions and they are often confused. Keeping a record costs almost nothing and has ongoing value, because a year of dated observations is a resource you cannot reconstruct later. Stopping the practice means admitting the exercise is not producing anything you can use, and that is a separate judgement made on the criterion you committed to, not on how the record feels to read.

A reasonable rule is to run three cycles and then decide. If the criterion was met in at least two of them, the practice is working as a practice, and you can raise the stakes on the aim. If it was met in none or one, the method is not producing results for you, and continuing past that point is habit rather than inquiry. The record survives the decision either way, and it is the thing you will be glad to have when you decide to try again with a different aim.

The second exercise, and what changes

Between the first cycle and the second, almost nothing in the method changes and one thing in the aim does. What changes in the practitioner is the baseline. You now have a dated record that shows you what your ordinary week looks like under observation, and that is the most useful artefact the first cycle produced, because it is the only comparison point you have for anything that happens later.

The temptation at this stage is to make the second exercise bigger, more dramatic, or more confirmatory, on the theory that the first one was too small to show anything. Resist it. The correct upgrade is a harder aim within the same size of method, and the most useful thing you can add to the record is a column you previously left out, filled in honestly at the end of the month rather than predicted in advance. Growth in this practice looks like a longer record and a more demanding aim, not a larger ritual.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does gnosis actually mean in chaos magic practice?

In early theoretical writing it referred to a state of contact with a pattern or a reality that the ordinary self does not maintain. In practice it is far less dramatic: sustained attention on something, held long enough that the usual filtering stops. Treat any claim that it permanently alters perception as untestable from the inside, and evaluate a working instead on whether it changed what you did.

How do I stop fooling myself with expectancy?

You cannot eliminate it, and trying usually produces a subtler form of it. You can structure the practice so that expectancy has less room: fix the aim in writing before you start, grade against a criterion committed in advance, record failures with the same detail as successes, and leave a column headed with reasons this might be wrong permanently empty until the end of the period.

Is a strong feeling during an exercise evidence that it worked?

No. Feeling is one of the least reliable indicators available, because a good mood raises confidence in whatever you currently believe, including a doubt. If the practice is working, the evidence is behavioural and dated: a count you kept, a schedule you followed, a choice you made differently. Those can be checked by someone who was not there.

How long should each cycle run before I judge the method?

Long enough to produce a number you would not have produced otherwise, and no longer. Thirty days with a daily commitment is the standard starting point because it produces both enough observations to see a pattern and enough friction that a purely motivational start cannot survive. Run at least three cycles before deciding the method does not work for you.

What if my record shows the criterion was never met?

Treat it as information and change the aim rather than the grading. The usual cause is that the written aim was vague or the criterion was outside your control. Write a narrower aim with a criterion you can actually observe, and run it again. Stop only after three cycles have failed the same way, and keep the records either way, since they are the only baseline you will ever have.
