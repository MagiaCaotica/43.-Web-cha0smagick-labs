Dreams, Astral Projection and Tulpas

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 13 min read

Most people arrive at this material through a story rather than a plan, and the order in which the claims get sorted out matters more than it usually seems. Dreams are treated as a single subject with a single reputation, and they are not. Inside the general category of dream work there are five distinct claims, and they differ enormously in what has actually been measured, what is documented but disputed, and what belongs to literature rather than to practice. A quiet awareness that you are dreaming has a measurable base rate in ordinary populations, and reliable induction techniques exist. The tulpa comes out of a specific religious tradition in Tibet, where it is a discipline of visualisation and deity yoga rather than a way of manufacturing a separate creature. Remote viewing has failed to replicate under prospective conditions while the training attached to it genuinely improves visual description. Out-of-body experiences are reported by a small minority and they do not arrive on command. And a layer of material that circulates under the same name belongs to fiction rather than to any tradition of practice. Once those five are sorted, the shape of the field becomes obvious, and the map tells you something practical about where your effort is worth spending. The reason this arrangement is the right one to start with is that the categories collapse constantly in conversation. A practitioner who has had one lucid dream and read a great deal of the older material tends to treat all five as a single skill with a single obstacle. They are not, and the differences between them are large enough to change what you should attempt this month.

Five claims, and they are not equal

The word dream work covers at least five separate claims, and they sit at different levels of established fact. One is awareness inside a dream. One is a visualisation discipline with a documented religious provenance. One is a claim about perceiving distant places, with a specific experimental history. One is a claim about the body and the sense of location. And one is a body of invention that has no practice attached to it at all. When these are averaged together into a single opinion, the average is wrong, and it is usually wrong in the direction of the least supported member, because the fictional material is the loudest and the most narratively satisfying.

Sorting them is not pedantry. It determines whether effort spent this month is likely to produce anything, and it determines what counts as a result. Against a well-documented claim, a practice with a track record is a reasonable expenditure. Against a claim that has failed prospective replication, the only thing worth buying is skill in description. Against something that belongs to literature, there is nothing to spend at all, and the honest move is to notice that and stop.

The one with a measurable base

Lucid dreaming is the most tractable item in this whole field, and it is worth saying plainly why. It has a measurable base rate in ordinary populations, which means a meaningful minority of people report it spontaneously, and it has induction techniques that have been tested enough to be described with some precision. Nothing about that makes it mystical. It makes it a skill, in the way that remembering a route home is a skill. The hard part is the one consistent feature across every account, which is that the moment of realisation is available only after the fact.

That lag is the entire technical problem, and it is also what makes the field teachable. You cannot decide mid-dream that you will notice, because the deciding mind is the one asleep. What you can do is build a cue that survives into the dream state, and practice the recovery of the moment rather than the prevention of forgetting. Practitioners who treat this as a contest of willpower usually stall. Practitioners who treat it as a cue-and-recovery exercise usually get somewhere within a month.

The practice that comes from a religion

The tulpa is usually introduced as though it were a technique of Western esotericism that happens to share a name with a Tibetan one. The provenance runs the other way. In the Tibetan traditions the method belongs to visualisation practice and to deity yoga, where a practitioner systematically holds a form in mind with a specific purpose and then relates to it in a specific way. The discipline includes a withdrawal period and restrictions on speech, and those restrictions are not decorative. They are part of what the practice is for.

What the tradition does not contain is the modern claim that the practitioner manufactures an autonomous being who then continues to exist and can be injured or dismissed. That is a later addition and it is a significant distortion, because it changes the practice from a discipline of the mind into a relationship with a creature. Read as what it is, a systematic visualisation, it is a demanding practice with a long history and it fits alongside the other contemplative work in the same tradition. Read as creature manufacture, it is a different activity with a thinner record and a worse ethical profile.

The one with disputed results and real gains

Remote viewing has a specific and unusual shape. The prospective results did not replicate under controlled conditions, which is not a small finding and should not be explained away. And yet the training attached to it produced something that transfers: people who train report that they describe a target in terms that are more precise, more structured, and more independent of stereotype. That is a real gain and it is a gain in a different skill from the one the claim is about.

This split matters more than the verdict does. If your interest is in whether intention reaches across distance, the honest position is that the evidence has not supported it. If your interest is in whether you can look at a place you have never seen and describe it without filling the gaps with expectation, that is a trainable ability, it is worth training, and it has nothing to do with the claim. Practitioners who keep the two separate get something usable. Practitioners who fuse them end up defending a claim they were really only trying to develop a skill.

The one that does not come at will

Out-of-body experience is reported by a small percentage of the population and has a fairly consistent pattern of conditions. It tends to occur near sleep onset, which means on the edge of waking rather than deep in a dream. It occurs more often during illness, and more often during sustained meditation. It does not reliably respond to a decision made beforehand. That last point is the one that should govern expectations, because the entire structure of a sigil working depends on being able to point the intention somewhere and have it land.

The practical consequence is that this item is not a good candidate for a first working. It is a good candidate for something that happens and gets written down. The reasonable position is to build a record over months rather than to run a protocol designed for voluntary production, and to accept that a protocol tuned for a voluntary phenomenon will mostly measure the protocol. Reading the conditions as instructions is how people end up with a practice that appears to fail repeatedly, when what is actually happening is that they are waiting in the wrong place for the wrong thing.

The one that is mostly fiction

A large amount of material that circulates under the dream heading belongs to literature rather than to any documented practice. Bilocation appears in a handful of modern works and has no antecedent in the traditions that are otherwise being drawn on. Precognitive dream claims appear in research papers and have not held up. The elaborate architecture of layered planes, and the taxonomy of what exists on each, has no source that a historian would accept. None of this is worthless as imagery, and some of it is genuinely good imagery. It is simply not evidence and it is not instruction.

The reason to name it is that fiction is the most generative source in this field and the least self-policing. A fictional framework will happily accept any observation you bring to it, because it was not built to be constrained by observation. That is a feature for writing and a serious problem for practice. Keeping the layer visible as a layer is the difference between using it as a source of images and using it as a source of claims, and the second use is where the trouble starts.

Why the tiers are the structure

Once the five are separated, the domain stops being flat and starts having a shape, and the shape is a hierarchy of what has been checked. One item has a measurable base and reliable technique. One has a documented practice and a provenance. One has failed replication but a real transfer skill. One has a consistent incidental pattern and no voluntary control. And one has no support at all. Almost everything useful about how to spend effort follows from that ordering, and almost none of it follows from any argument about the nature of consciousness.

This is why the map is worth more than an argument. An argument about the field's nature can be settled rhetorically by anyone with the patience. A map can be checked against the record. When someone tells you that the five items are really one item, you can ask which of the five their evidence covers, and the question either has an answer or it does not. That is a much better position than defending a view of what dreams are for, which is a conversation nobody finishes and everybody enjoys.

What the morning window is for

There is one practical detail that belongs in the structure rather than in the technique sections, because it is the one that reliably changes results. REM periods lengthen across the night, which means the last hour before waking carries the highest density of the kind of dreaming that recall can reach. A practitioner who sets an alarm for a natural waking time and stays still for a few minutes on either side of it will get more usable data per week than one who reads a technique for a month and then tries to apply it in the middle of the night.

The mechanism is straightforward and it is not mysterious. Longer REM periods near morning mean more opportunity for the kind of content that a cue can latch onto, and a still body on waking means the transition is not overwritten by the ordinary business of starting a day. Both effects are small and both are reliable, which is the combination worth having. The advice sounds trivial and it is the difference between a person who collects a handful of entries a year and one who collects a few a week.

The training that does transfer

Across these five items, the training that reliably improves results is the same. It is the deliberate construction of a cue that will survive into the state you want to notice, followed by practice at recovering the moment of recognition rather than the moment itself. That is the whole technique for the first item, it is the right shape of approach for the third, and it applies in a modified form to the second. It is not a mystical procedure and it does not require a particular belief about what any of it means.

What is striking is how unremarkable it is. The cue is usually a physical action, repeated, done at a fixed point. The recovery is a question asked immediately on waking. Both are dull. Both work, and the reason they work is that they address the actual mechanism of failure, which is that the noticing mind is unavailable at the moment of noticing. Everything else in the field is either decoration on that mechanism or an attempt to bypass it, and bypassing it is why most of the material in this area has so little to show after a year of effort.

Where the field is genuinely uncertain

Two things are worth admitting as uncertain rather than argued about. The first is the phenomenology of the lucid state itself, where reports of what awareness feels like inside the dream vary widely and there is no good account of why. The second is whether any of the voluntary techniques do anything beyond establishing a cue, which is a question that is hard to settle because a cue is hard to rule out from the inside. Both of these are open. Neither of them is a reason to stop, and both of them are reasons to keep a record instead of relying on impression.

There is also a commercial uncertainty that affects everything. Much of the material available on this subject is sold by people who benefit from the ambiguity, and the incentives run consistently toward keeping the layers fused. A course that teaches one well-documented skill and a framework that claims four unsupported ones will often be presented together, and the presence of the first lends credibility to the second in a way that survives neither logic nor evidence. The map is the antidote, because it names the supported item and lets the rest go.

How to spend effort across the tiers

Given the structure, the allocation follows almost without argument. Most of your effort belongs in the first tier, because that is where the technique is reliable and the result is checkable. A smaller amount belongs in the second, because it is a demanding contemplative discipline and it will absorb whatever you give it, but it is not the place to look for a fast result. The third tier deserves a bounded investment in the training and no investment in the claim. The fourth tier is worth a passive record. The fifth tier is worth reading as literature and nothing else.

The most common mistake in allocating effort is the reverse one: a great deal of time on the fourth and fifth tiers, where the material is most absorbing and the feedback is least available, and a little on the first, where the results would have arrived quickly. That pattern is easy to explain. The interesting material keeps being interesting and the boring material eventually produces something. If you want the thing that works to work, the allocation is not a philosophical question, and spending four fifths of your attention on the part of the field that cannot answer you is a choice you can revise whenever you like.

The map worth keeping

The useful artefact from all of this is short. One item has a measurable base and a reliable technique, and it deserves most of your time. One is a documented religious practice and it deserves patience rather than results. One has a failed replication and a real skill, and those should be kept apart. One arrives incidentally and is best recorded rather than produced. And one belongs to literature. Written on a single page, that map will do more for a year of practice than any framework that claims the five are one, because it tells you where to point your effort and it survives being argued with.

Keep it near the notebook, not near the reading. The failure mode in this field is not a lack of interesting material, it is the slow re-fusion of the layers, and re-fusion happens every time you read something written by someone who did not sort them. One page with the five named and separated, checked against your own record at the end of a month, will hold the structure in place when the vocabulary around it starts to blur. That is the whole job. The vocabulary changes every season and the structure has not changed in forty years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is lucid dreaming the only part of this field with real evidence behind it?

The clearest evidence sits with awareness inside a dream, which has a measurable base rate in ordinary populations and induction techniques that have been described with enough precision to be taught. Remote viewing has a failed prospective replication, though its training transfers. The tulpa has a documented religious provenance rather than experimental support. Out-of-body experience shows a consistent incidental pattern without voluntary control. A further layer is literary rather than practical. The first item is the one that will repay effort on a measured timescale.

Where did the tulpa actually come from, and is it what modern practitioners say it is?

It comes from Tibetan traditions, where the method belongs to systematic visualisation and to deity yoga, with a withdrawal period and speech restrictions as part of the discipline. Those traditions do not describe manufacturing an autonomous creature. That framing is a later Western addition and it changes the practice from a discipline of the mind into a relationship with an entity. Read as visualisation, it is demanding and documented. Read as creature manufacture, the record is thin and the ethical profile is worse.

If remote viewing results did not replicate, is any of the training worth doing?

Yes, and the reason is that the training and the claim are separable. Practitioners who train report more precise, more structured description of targets, independent of stereotype. That is a real transferable ability and it can be developed deliberately. The claim that intention reaches across distance is a different matter and has not been supported under controlled conditions. Keeping the two apart lets you use the skill without defending the claim, which is the position most likely to be both useful and accurate.

Why does the morning matter so much for dream recall?

REM periods lengthen as the night progresses, so the final stretch before waking carries the highest density of the dream content that recall can reach. A still body on waking also keeps the transition from being overwritten by the start of the day. Both effects are modest and both are consistent, which makes them worth more than elaborate methods applied at the wrong end of the night. Setting an alarm for a natural waking time and staying still for a few minutes either side of it is the cheapest useful intervention in this entire field.

How do I stop the five categories from merging back into one?

Keep the separation on a single written page with the five named, and check your own record against it monthly. The re-fusion happens through reading rather than through practice, because most of the material fuses them deliberately or by inattention, and a good phrase from an unsupported layer will migrate into a supported one without anyone noticing. The monthly check is what holds the structure in place. The vocabulary around it changes every season; the arrangement has not changed in forty years, and the page is what makes that visible.
