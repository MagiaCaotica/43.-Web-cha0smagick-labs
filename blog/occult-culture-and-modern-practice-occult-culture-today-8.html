Occult Culture Today: A Practitioner's Protocol

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

Somewhere in the last fifteen years occult practice changed shape without announcing itself. The people arriving now almost never come through a lineage. They arrive through a search result, a forum thread, a video, a playlist, or a friend who mentioned something. The old initiatory route, where you were handed a practice by someone older, is now the exception. That is not a decline. It is a different transmission structure, and it produces different problems, most of which are organisational rather than spiritual. How do you know whether what you found is usable. How do you get feedback when nobody is holding the standard. How do you avoid becoming the sole custodian of a practice you have had for eight months. The protocol that follows is about those problems, and it is more useful than any list of correspondences.

Discovery Went Online and Did Not Come Back

For most of the twentieth century, finding esoteric material meant bookshops, letters, lectures and personal contact. That channel is not gone, but it is no longer the main one, and the shift has a consequence that gets underdiscussed: search results have no authorship, no curriculum and no agreed standard of what a beginner should be told first.

What arrives is a fragment. A technique with no prerequisites, an explanation with no history, an argument with no source. Most people assemble a practice out of these fragments and call the result a tradition, because nobody told them the fragments had gaps.

The fix is small and unglamorous. Before doing anything, spend an hour on provenance: who wrote this, when, and what did they assume the reader already knew. Two hours, honestly, will save you from months of practice built on a technique that was never designed to stand alone.

Family Practice Is Not a Reconstruction

Witchcraft in Peru, Mexico, Brazil and the Caribbean differs substantially from the Northern European reconstruction that dominates English-language publishing. Much of it carries plant knowledge, remedies and household practice that arrived with Spanish and Portuguese colonisation and mixed with Indigenous and African material afterwards. None of it passed through Wicca, because Wicca is twentieth-century Britain.

This matters practically. Reconstruction traditions are internally consistent and often internally documented, which makes them easy to learn from a book. Family practice is frequently undocumented, transmitted by demonstration, and inconsistent between households, which makes it much harder to learn from anything except a person. A practitioner who finds access to a living family tradition is holding something genuinely rarer than a reconstruction manual.

The other thing this tradition is good for is correcting a bias. English-language occult publishing overrepresents white European lineages and treats everything else as a variant. A wider reading makes the whole field look less like a canon with branches and more like one of many regional attempts at the same human problem.

DIY Has Been Going Since the Late Seventies

Punk and industrial scenes have hosted self-directed occult practice since the late 1970s, running in parallel with formal orders rather than as a reaction to them. The format repeats: borrow whatever is legible, strip out the hierarchy, keep the parts that survive contact with a daily life, and share the result through records, zines and conversation.

What makes it durable is the low barrier. Nobody has to be initiated or approved, so the number of people experimenting is enormous and the number producing usable results is also, in absolute terms, large. It functions as a distributed research programme with no institution attached.

It also produces a specific kind of drift, because nothing enforces a standard. A technique that was once tested gets repeated until it is repeated rather than tested. This is the main cost of the DIY route, and the only real correction is a personal record: date the claim, set a deadline, write down the failures.

Shared Symbols Without an Institution

The KEK archetype is the cleanest recent example. A joke format with no author and no intent turned into a genuinely shared symbol that people invoke in earnest, then adjusted, remixed and given new meanings by use. It is a live case study in collective reinforcement, and it is running on nothing but repetition and recognition.

Meme-based practices are interesting partly because they are so obviously not initiatory. Anyone can join, which means the group is enormous and the shared meaning is thin, held together by the fact that everyone already knows the reference.

The honest question is whether that is a weaker version of what orders provide, or a different version. Probably both. You get far more people and far less depth. What protects the depth is doing one thing long enough to develop your own judgement about it, which is precisely what the format cannot give you.

Follow a Teacher, Then Stop

A teacher is genuinely useful for a defined skill. Learning letter reduction from someone who has been doing it for twenty years saves you months of badly designed sigils. Learning the timing and pacing of a practice from an experienced practitioner saves you from the over-effort that ends most beginners.

The structural risk is different. Following one figure for a decade means your entire method inherits one person's blind spots, including any that are frankly wrong, and you have no reference point outside their system. The problem is rarely that a teacher is bad. It is that you stop reading anyone else.

A workable arrangement is to use a teacher for a bounded competence and read everyone else for context. Learn one thing well from one person, take the history from several authors, and take the criticism from somebody who has no stake in the tradition. Then check the teacher's claims against your own record, which is the only authority available to you.

Being Public Costs Little Now

Obscurity used to be a form of protection: if nobody knows, nobody can be targeted. That has stopped being true. Contemporary practice is largely public, searchable, and discussed in places with an audience, and there is no version of it that stays private while also benefiting from community.

The practical question is therefore about content rather than visibility. Being known as someone who does this has almost no cost. Being publicly committed to a specific controversial position has a cost, and it is the only cost worth managing.

Keep the two separate. Post the practice. Do not post the argument you have not tested yet. Most of the reputational damage in this field comes from public positions taken before any record existed to support them.

A Working Protocol

None of this is traditional and all of it is what keeps a self-directed practice from dissolving. The record is the part people skip, and it is the part that produces the only evidence you will ever have about your own results.

The review date does the remaining work. Most practices are abandoned not because they failed but because no result was ever written down, so at the point of decision the whole thing looks like nothing happened. A year of dated entries changes that conversation entirely, including the ones where the entries show the work failed.

  1. Spend the first month on history and method, not technique. Provenance before practice.
  2. Pick one system and go properly deep, rather than sampling four traditions shallowly.
  3. Write a dated entry for every attempt, including the ones with no result at all.
  4. Take one concrete criticism from someone outside your tradition and act on it.
  5. Set a review date, and on that date read your own record rather than your memory of it.

When Practice Becomes a Social Circle

Communities form around a practice and they are mostly good, because feedback is the thing self-directed work cannot supply. The difficulty is that communities are also where norms settle, and norms settle toward whatever the most confident members do rather than toward whatever the evidence supports.

The practical test is whether asking an uncomfortable question in the group changes how you are treated. A space where the answer is a faster, more specific, more falsifiable framing is doing you real work. A space where the answer is a reassertion of shared belief is a social space, and those are worth having as long as you know which one you are in.

The One-Month Test

A month is long enough to produce a countable record and short enough that abandoning it is an obvious decision rather than a slow drift. The only requirement is that the record exists by the end of it.

If you want a single measure, count the entries where nothing happened. That number is the honest one. A month with twelve attempts and four null results is a functioning practice; a month with twelve attempts and one remembered success is a month of nothing that felt like something.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I read first?

Liber Null and Liber Kaos for method, since one is a working manual and the other explains the thinking behind it. Then one solid history, read for provenance rather than for atmosphere. A critical volume by an author who has no stake in the tradition belongs in the same batch, because it is the book that keeps the other three honest about which claims are inherited and which were printed recently.

Is it acceptable to practise publicly?

Completely, and there is no longer a meaningful alternative. Contemporary practice is mostly public and searchable, so obscurity protects nothing that a private post would not. Keep visibility and commitment separate instead. Sharing the practice costs you almost nothing. Announcing a position you have not tested is what actually attracts the argument.

Should I follow a single teacher?

For a bounded competence, yes, and it is the fastest route to competence. Holding one figure as your only source for a decade is the structural risk, because you inherit their blind spots with no outside reference to catch them. Use a teacher to learn one thing properly, read several authors for context, and take criticism from somebody outside the tradition entirely.

How do I know whether a practice is working?

You rarely can from a single instance, which is why the record matters more than the impression. Write the prediction before the attempt, set a date, and count every attempt including the failures. A run of thirty dated entries will tell you more than a memory of one good evening ever will, and only the dated version of that question can be answered honestly.

Is there a paid app that helps with a daily occult routine?

A daily practice is cheap in materials and expensive in continuity, and continuity is what a tool can actually help with. An app is worth buying if it holds your dated record, your notes and your review schedule in one place, because the failure mode for most practitioners is not a lack of technique, it is losing the thread. If it only supplies prompts, write the prompts on paper. My own preference is to own a place for the record outright rather than hold a subscription against something I may stop caring about in eight weeks.
