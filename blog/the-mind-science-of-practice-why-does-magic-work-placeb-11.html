The Mind Science of Practice

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

Most defences of practice against its critics fail in the same way: they concede every point about mechanism and then act as though the concession settles nothing. That is a mistake worth correcting, because the honest position is both weaker and more useful than either camp usually states. Ritual reliably changes measurable physiology, and it reliably changes behaviour, and neither of those facts requires anything supernatural. Whether that is enough to justify a working depends on what you wanted the working to do. This page takes the sceptical account seriously in its strong form, including the parts that undercut claims people make in the literature, and then asks what survives. The short answer is a narrower claim than practitioners usually make and a better-supported one: structure lowers the cost of acting on a decision, and attention is a trainable input. The long answer involves what a good prediction looks like, which is where most of the trouble is.

The Strongest Version of the Objection

The serious version of the sceptical case is not that nothing happens. Almost nobody claims nothing happens. It is that everything that happens has a mundane mechanism, that practitioners are systematically bad at telling coincidental outcomes from caused ones, and that the vocabulary of practice is built to make the difference hard to see. That is a real critique and it deserves a straight answer rather than a joke about placebo.

Three parts of it are simply true. Expectancy changes what you notice, which means a practice that announces an outcome will produce more reports of that outcome regardless of its mechanism. Ritual is demanding and often costly, which means people who stick with it are already selected for a certain kind of persistence, and that selection is invisible in any uncontrolled comparison. And the success vocabulary in this literature is wide enough to absorb almost any event, which makes failure reports rarer than they should be.

The mistake in the sceptical case is the leap from those three concessions to a general conclusion about value. Confusion, selection, and vague language are all reasons to distrust particular claims. They are not reasons to conclude that a practice which measurably changes your physiology and your behaviour has no function. Conflating the two is the most common error in the entire debate, and it is an error on both sides.

What Predictive Processing Explains and What It Does Not

The account that has done the most to replace the older and cruder version of expectancy is the treatment of perception as hypothesis management. On this view, the brain does not receive a stream of input and interpret it; it generates predictions and corrects them against incoming data. Expectancy is not a filter laid over perception afterwards. It is part of the machinery that produces perception in the first place.

What that buys practitioners is a specific and defensible claim. If perception is partly predictive, then attention directed at a question for a sustained period will change the salience of things connected to that question, and this does not require you to deceive yourself about anything. You are not lying to get a result. You are allocating expensive perceptual machinery to one question, and the machinery works that way.

What it does not buy is anything about external reality. The model explains why experience is organised around expectation. It says nothing about whether a working affects anything outside the operator. Claiming a mechanistic account as evidence for external effect is a category error that practitioners make constantly and that makes their strongest-sounding arguments their weakest. You can hold the mechanistic account and remain entirely agnostic about the external question, and holding both is more coherent than choosing either.

The Autonomic Results Are the Solid Part

If you want the least disputable claims about practice, start with the autonomic ones. Slowing your breathing changes heart-rate variability within minutes, by a mechanism nobody disputes. Repeating a fixed sequence of physical actions produces measurable settling in arousal levels. Deprivation of sleep, alcohol, or food degrades cognitive performance in ways that are well characterised. None of this is magic and all of it is relevant, because a working is a concentrated dose of exactly these conditions.

That has a direct consequence for how you build a practice. A session that keeps you upright, still, and breathing in a fixed pattern for twenty minutes is doing something measurable whether or not anything else happens. So is a session that writes the target down, because it converts an intention into a commitment that has a date attached. The best-supported claim about structured practice is that it is an efficient way to produce a specific internal state and to bind a decision to a time.

The honest limitation is that these effects are also produced by exercise, cold water, meditation apps, and a good night's sleep. Autonomic change is not evidence of anything beyond autonomic change. It is a foundation you can build on, not a proof of anything, and treating it as a proof is how the field loses people who were otherwise sympathetic.

The Zener Deck as a Reasoning Exercise

The card set used in Rhine's work on extrasensory perception has five symbols repeated five times each, twenty-five cards in total, and a hit rate of one in five by pure chance. That arithmetic is the reason the deck has become a standard teaching instrument, and it is worth using as such, because most claims about practice fail against exactly this kind of calculation and nothing else is required to expose them.

The useful exercise is not to test yourself. It is to test a claim. If someone reports a run of hits, the first question is how many runs were performed, because a 20% hit rate produces long striking streaks in a handful of attempts. Twenty-two cards in a row is roughly a one-in-four-thousand event, which sounds remarkable and turns up regularly across thousands of sessions by many different people. The number of sessions is the part that gets left out of the retelling.

The second lesson is about the shape of a bad test. A deck with a 20% base rate needs a very large number of trials before small differences become visible, and most informal sessions run nowhere near that. Which means the ordinary experience of a session tells you almost nothing, in either direction. This is a genuinely humbling fact and it applies to your own results as much as anyone else's, which is the reason to adopt the habit rather than argue about whose friend has better luck.

Cost and Commitment as the Actual Mechanism

Of the mechanisms on offer, the one that survives every objection and has the least to do with metaphysics is cost. A working that takes twenty minutes, a specific object, a fixed hour, and a written record imposes a cost on the operator. Anything that imposes a cost gets weighted differently from the same thought had it arrived unprompted. This is the same mechanism as a gym membership, a cure, or a phone you are not permitted to check, and it works on anyone regardless of what they believe.

Cost also solves the measurement problem that defeats casual practice. If the working requires a written log, then a session that does not happen leaves a hole, and the hole is visible. You can no longer accumulate a flattering impression of how often you did the thing. A practice with no artefact, no timestamp, and no minimum duration produces a memory rather than a record, and the memory is always more favourable than what happened.

This is why the recommendation that comes up repeatedly in this literature is boring: a short session at a fixed time, a written target, and a log. Nothing about that list is mystical. All of it is designed to make the practice legible to the person doing it, which is the only observer reliably available.

Three Failure Modes Nobody Writes Down

These are the ways a practice produces a misleading result even when everything is done correctly.

  1. Retroactive definition. The target is written after the result is known, and phrased so that the result counts. Repair: date the statement and keep it in a file you do not edit later.
  2. Selective memory of attempts. Failures are not logged because the session ended early or the day was bad. Repair: log a failure line for every scheduled session, including empty ones.
  3. Outcome substitution. A different but related event is accepted as evidence, because it is easier to score. Repair: one observable per working, chosen before the attempt.

What Survives If You Drop the Ontology

Assume for the sake of argument that nothing external is happening. Everything above still holds. Structure lowers the cost of acting on a decision. Attention is a trainable input that can be pointed at a question. Autonomic states can be produced deliberately. A log makes an outcome legible. A glyph makes a specification stable. None of that requires a claim about what is on the other side of the practice, and all of it is enough to justify the practice as an instrument for changing behaviour.

If something external is happening, the practice is a reasonable way to find out, provided you measure. The same log that makes a mundane explanation look adequate also makes an extraordinary one testable, because you wrote down what you expected and when. That is the only asymmetry in the debate that favours neither side: a well-run working is informative under both hypotheses, and a badly run one is not.

The conclusion worth carrying is modest and durable. Keep your claims small enough that you can check them. Write the prediction, set the date, count every attempt. You will find that most arguments about the mechanism become uninteresting once you can see your own record, and that the practice survives the demotion without complaint, which is a test it should have passed from the start.

Frequently Asked Questions

If the mechanism is expectancy, why keep a practice at all?

Because expectancy-driven change is still change. Deciding in advance to spend twenty focused minutes on one question at a fixed hour is a mechanism that works on your behaviour whether or not you think anything supernatural is involved. The claim people want to defend is usually much grander than the claim they need, and the grand one is where the trouble starts.

How do I tell a real effect from a coincidence without fooling myself?

You probably cannot from one instance, and the honest move is to stop trying. Register a prediction with a date before the attempt, then count every attempt including the ones you expected to fail. Most apparent successes turn out to be events that were already going to happen, and the only way to see that is a record of attempts rather than a memory of the good ones.

Does the sceptical account mean magic is impossible?

No, and it is worth being precise about why. A mundane account of the mechanism leaves the external question open rather than answering it. It shows that all the internal effects can be produced without invoking anything hidden, which is a statement about sufficiency, not about impossibility. Plenty of people hold both positions at once and find it more useful than picking one.

Should I keep a practice that I suspect only affects me?

If the effect is real and measurable in your own behaviour, that is the only effect you ever had direct access to. Whether the target was externally influenced is a separate question, and one that your own log cannot settle. A practice that reliably makes you sit still, write a target down, and act on a decision is doing something regardless of how you label the mechanism.

What is the most common statistical error in this literature?

Ignoring the number of attempts. Streaks at rates around one in five are unremarkable over a few hundred sessions and each one is reported as though it were the only run performed. The second most common is using a 20% base rate to interpret a small sample as a result. Neither error requires any hostility towards the practice; both are simple failures to count.
