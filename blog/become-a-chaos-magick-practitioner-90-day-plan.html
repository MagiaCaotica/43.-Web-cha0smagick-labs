How to Become a Chaos Magick Practitioner: A Ninety-Day Plan That Replaces Reading With Work

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 15 min read

The question behind almost every beginner question about chaos magick is whether there is a way to find out if you are doing it right, and the honest answer is that there is no initiation, no lineage check and no exam. Austin Osman Spare produced a notation rather than a grimoire, deliberately, and the tradition inherited that refusal. What replaces initiation is a log. The practitioner who can show a dated record of what they did, what they expected and what happened is further along than the practitioner with three initiations and no record, and this article is built around that claim. Ninety days, four fields per entry, one small dated working in the first fortnight. Most of what follows is the boring machinery that makes the difference, and none of it requires believing anything in particular.

Nobody can certify you, and that is the design

Crown through the veil, with fear in his hand, and no hesitation in his eye is a line from a Liber expansion and it is doing more work than beginners realise. The whole architecture of the tradition runs on crossing without permission. Austin Osman Spare, the figure the modern practice is built around, published a shorthand for making signs rather than a system with degrees, and Peter Carroll, who named the thing, made the same choice on purpose: an initiatory structure would have reproduced exactly the gatekeeping chaos magick exists to remove.

So the first honest fact is that nobody can tell you whether you qualify. Not a book, not a forum, not a person. If the practice is real, that absence of certification is not a gap in the design, it is the design. The question is what fills the role a certificate would otherwise fill, and the answer is not enthusiasm. It is a record.

This is uncomfortable for people drawn to the tradition partly because of its mystery, and the discomfort is worth sitting with rather than resolving. A practice with no gate has no gatekeeper to disappoint you and no authority to defer to, which means every claim you make about your own progress has to survive on its own evidence. That is a higher bar than being told you are on the path. It is also the only bar that tells you anything.

The gap between the eight hours and the two years

The standard initiation story is a weekend. Read Liber Null, or its equivalents, in one sitting, and you are in. This is true in a narrow and literal sense: the reading takes about eight hours and you will be changed by it. Novels do this to people.

What eight hours produces is not competence. It is an interpretive frame, and frames are cheap and useful. What competence requires is a body of dated evidence that you can lose, plus the ability to state what you expected before you find out, plus a working method for changing something you can verify. That takes considerably longer than a weekend, and most people who claim it took a weekend are describing the frame and calling it the competence.

A fair estimate from people who have kept long logs is somewhere between eighteen months and three years before a practice feels like a reliable instrument rather than an occasional effect you cannot repeat. Some of that is skill. A significant part is that repetition at a modest rate produces a body of cases, and a body of cases is what lets you tell a real pattern from a coincidence you liked.

If someone offers you a fast track, they are not lying about their own experience. They are describing a different thing than the one you are hoping for.

Why beginners stall, and the one that matters most

The dominant failure is not fear, and it is not disbelief. It is consumption. The beginner who has read six books and performed nothing has a rich vocabulary and no data, and the vocabulary is more immediately rewarding than the data because reading produces the feeling of progress while a log produces the feeling of doubt.

The mechanism is easy. A book is finite, so it can be finished, and finishing is a clean reward. A daily practice is infinite, so it never finishes, and ten minutes of signing a notebook produces nothing that looks like an accomplishment. Reading wins on every short timescale, which is the only timescale most people are optimising for.

The second failure is upgrading too early. The beginner who spends their first month acquiring tools has a beautiful setup and no results, and the tools then become the thing they are attached to. A notebook and a source of genuine randomness are sufficient for everything in this plan. Anything you buy in month one should be bought because it removed a specific blocker you have already hit and named.

The third is asking the practice to confirm a decision you have already made. If the working is going to be about a relationship you have already decided to leave, you will find the result ambiguous no matter what happens, because the result cannot be what you needed it to be. This one appears in the first month more often than the other two combined and it is the least discussed.

The minimal kit

Four items. A notebook that cannot sync to anything, because a searchable notebook is a document you will reorganise instead of write in. A pen. A source of genuine randomness that you cannot predict, which is the one piece of equipment people get wrong most often. And ten minutes a day.

On that last point: genuine randomness is not a random number generator you built, because if you built it you can influence the sequence by when you ran it and how you understood the seed. It is also not a shuffled deck you prepared, for the same reason. It is an external source with no relationship to your intention at all. A pack of Zener cards run with per-session scores is the best-known version, and the important part is not the cards, it is that you are scoring against a real random sequence with a fixed trial count and writing the number down.

That is the whole kit. Everything else in the tradition, the candles, the specific inks, the planetary timings, the correct materials, the expensive books, all of it is optional and most of it is downstream of a decision you have not yet made. The tradition's own founders had almost nothing and were highly productive. A spare Sigil, cut from a cheap notebook, was Austin Osman Spare's entire toolkit and the method is still taught.

If you want to spend money in month one, spend it on a deck of Zener cards and a notebook, and nothing else.

The four-field log

This is the mechanism the whole ninety days runs on. Every entry, every practice, has four fields and no more. Date. What you did, in one line, specific enough that a stranger could tell whether you did it. What you expected, and when. What happened, written at the review date rather than the day of.

The order matters enormously. The third field is filled in before the second. Most people write what happened on the day it happened, which is retrospective and heavily edited, and a log of retrospective reports is a record of moods rather than outcomes. Writing the prediction first and the result later is the cheapest available upgrade to the reliability of anything you are doing, and it costs four minutes.

The fourth field is where the practice actually lives, because it is the only field that can contradict you. If you keep entries where the review comes back nothing, over three months, you have learned something genuinely useful about the method, and that something is not what the method's advocates claim. If your reviews are all hits, you have either been extremely lucky or your predictions are too vague to fail, and both are worth knowing.

Keep failures. This is the field where beginners cheat themselves hardest, because a log of successes is a more pleasant object to own. A log with a third of its entries reading nothing is worth more than a log of pure hits, for exactly the reason that it can still be checked.

Days 1 to 14: literacy and the log

Nothing is invoked in the first fortnight. You are building the instrument you will use to find out whether anything works, and you cannot do that while also testing the thing.

Read two things and stop. Austin Osman Spare on sigilisation, because it is the origin and it is short, and one framework text, because you need the vocabulary to be able to distinguish a technique from a mood. Do not read six. Reading six produces the consumption trap in its purest form, and the sixth book mostly restates the first five with different vocabulary.

Then write log entries. No workings, no invocations, nothing addressed to anything. You are training the write-it-down behaviour, which is the single most predictive habit in the whole practice and the one everyone skips. Ten minutes a day of a dated entry describing what you did, what you expected and what happened will take you an hour a fortnight and it is the part that will still be going in year three.

Somewhere in the second week, run a proper scored Zener session. Per-session scores, fixed trial counts, written down. This is your first real data and it will be worse than you expected, because everyone is worse than they expect on their first scored run. The correct reaction is to keep the number and run it again, not to decide you are not a natural.

Days 15 to 30: one small working, dated

One working. Not a money sigil, not a relationship, not anything you care about. This is the part where the plan is most conservative and it is the part people skip, and skipping it is why the second month rarely happens.

What you want is something with a clear, checkable, low-stakes outcome, and the best candidates are all boring. A letter you have been avoiding. A piece of admin with a known due date. A conversation you have been putting off. Something where success is visible to you within a fortnight and where nobody else is affected if it fails.

Sigilise it, charge it, and write the prediction with a date. Then put the practice down for two weeks and do not raise it, because the most common error at this stage is checking hourly and treating a lack of a result as a result. The review date is when you look, and the log's fourth field is where you record it.

The reason for the smallness is not humility. It is that a first working tells you almost nothing about magic, because you have no baseline and no comparison. It tells you a great deal about your own follow-through, which is measurable, and follow-through is the variable that predicts whether you will still be doing this in a year.

Days 31 to 60: the daily sigil

Now the practice proper. One sigil per day, written by hand, charged, and the log entry filled the same evening. This is where the ninety days either become a habit or quietly stop, and the mechanism of stopping is almost always the same: the daily sigil gets treated as a task to clear rather than a record to keep.

The discipline that keeps it alive is to make the daily entry earn its place. If the entry is a form with a blank for the sigil and a blank for the result, you will fill the first and leave the second, and the practice degrades into drawing. If the entry requires you to write what you expected and when, the entry is no longer administrative and you will do it because you want the record.

A second discipline, and this one is underrated: keep one sigil from each week that you have completely released from memory. Do not keep the design. Keep nothing. At the end of the sixty days, you will have a set of workings that you cannot reconstruct, and that set is the real output of the month. Whether you can reconstruct a sigil tells you something about how the method behaves when the visual anchor is gone, and it is one of the few structural tests in the tradition that nobody argues about.

If you want the sigil made for you rather than drawn, a free generator covers the design step. Drawing it by hand is not more powerful and is not less. It is a different thing you are practising, and that is all.

Days 61 to 90: one thoughtform, and the first honest review

The last month has one construct in it. A servitor, a thoughtform, an entity you design, and it is the first time in the ninety days you build something rather than perform something. Design it on paper before you build it, because the design sheet is the work. What it may do, what it must refuse, how you address it, what ends it, and what you put in at the build.

Then run the review. This is the step that makes the ninety days worth having and the one most people skip. Sit down with the whole log and count. How many entries. How many predictions. How many came back with something. How many came back with nothing. How many you never reviewed because the date slipped.

Write the result as a single number and a single sentence. The number is your hit rate on dated predictions. The sentence is what you think is causing it, written before you go looking for a better explanation. That sentence is the most valuable thing you will produce in ninety days, because it is the only one of your explanations that is falsifiable.

Everything in month four depends on that sentence being roughly right. If you got hits, the natural next step is to design a test that could have failed. If you got nothing, the natural next step is to check whether your predictions were specific enough to fail, which is a harder thing to be sure about than most people expect.

Do you need a teacher, and what does one actually do

The short answer is no, and the reason is structural rather than mystical. Anyone who can teach the method can already be read, and the sources are short and public. Austin Osman Spare's contribution is a few pages. What a beginner needs is not instruction but correction of a habit, and most of the habits that need correcting are visible in a log.

The honest case for a teacher is different. There are specific problems a book cannot fix. If your log shows a pattern and you cannot see why you cannot see why, that is a real need. If you are working with something that gets worse rather than dormant, that is a real need. If you cannot tell the difference between a noetic impression and a mood, that is a real need, and it is the one that the verification work in the gnosis material addresses more cheaply than a person would.

The dishonest case for a teacher is lineage as comfort. There is no initiatory structure to hold and no certificate to be issued, and anyone offering either is selling something the tradition explicitly does not contain. That is not a moral judgement about the person. It is a description of what they are selling.

If you do find one, the test is whether they keep a log of their own. Practitioners who keep records can be corrected. Practitioners who only instruct cannot.

Three reasons to stop, honestly stated

Most of these are not the reasons people cite. Not fear of the dark, and not a conflict with a religion, although those are real and legitimate and you should take them seriously if they are true for you. These are the three that show up in the logs.

The first is that it has become a way of talking about your life rather than a way of changing it. This is legible in the log. Every entry is about a feeling, a fear or an imagined future, and none of them names an action with a date. The working has become the interest.

The second is that you have started building on outcomes you cannot afford to be wrong about. A health working, a legal matter, a relationship where someone else is the decision-maker. These are not things to practise on and the tradition's own history is full of people who found this out expensively.

The third is that you have no log and no intention of starting one. The practice without a record is a mood with vocabulary attached, and that can be a perfectly good thing to have. It just will not do anything, and if what you wanted was for it to do something, the log is the price and there is no cheaper route.

None of these mean you failed. They mean the thing you actually wanted is not what you were doing, and the log is what makes that visible.

What the ninety days should cost

A notebook and a pen, roughly ten dollars. A deck of Zener cards, roughly fifteen. Two books, one free. That is the whole financial commitment, and the tradition's founders had a fraction of it.

The expensive path is a documented one. Grimoire collections, gold-leafed reprints, initiatory programmes, course bundles, and the standard marketing apparatus of the modern occult all monetise the same scarcity, which is a perceived entry ticket. The tradition has no entry ticket. Everything load-bearing is free and older than the internet.

The most common real expense is not the kit. It is the subscription pattern. People buy an annual membership to a practice app, use it for eleven days, and then pay again for a different one, and the accumulation of tools is a way of feeling like the practice is progressing when it is not. The free options genuinely cover most of what month one requires, and a free sigil generator is materially the same object as the paid one.

If you find yourself researching equipment in month one, that is the consumption trap and it feels exactly like progress.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to become a chaos magician?

The reading that introduces people to the tradition takes about a weekend. Competence, in the sense of having a body of dated cases and a method you can repeat, runs somewhere between eighteen months and three years at a modest daily rate. Those are different milestones and most people who describe a fast initiation are describing the first one while meaning the second.

Do I need to be initiated or belong to a lineage?

No, and the absence is deliberate rather than an oversight. Austin Osman Spare produced a notation instead of a grimoire and Peter Carroll named the tradition without adding degrees, because an initiatory structure would reproduce the gatekeeping the practice exists to remove. What takes the role a certificate would fill is a dated log you keep yourself, and nobody can audit it but you.

What should I do first, concretely?

Two things, in this order. Read Austin Osman Spare on sigilisation, which is short and is the origin of the central technique. Then start a log with four fields: date, what you did, what you expected and when, and what happened, with the last field written at the review date rather than on the day. Do not invoke anything for the first fortnight and do not buy anything beyond a notebook and a deck of Zener cards.

Is it just the placebo effect?

Possibly, and you cannot settle it from the inside, which is why the log matters. What the placebo account does not explain is a practice that produces reliable behavioural change in the practitioner, but that observation is compatible with several mechanisms including expectancy restructuring and plain commitment devices. A dated log with recorded failures is the only instrument most practitioners have for telling these apart, and running one is more informative than arguing about it.

What if my first working produces nothing?

Write that in the log and run another. The most common cause of a silent first working is not the failure of magic, it is a prediction written loosely enough that nothing could have matched it, checked too often, or attached to an outcome outside your control. So the second working should be smaller, more specific and given a single review date. Three entries in a row with nothing, written carefully, is real information about the method. One entry with nothing, written loosely, is information about the entry.
