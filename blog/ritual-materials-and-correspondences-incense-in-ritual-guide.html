Ritual Materials and Correspondences

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 12 min read

Most people who want to start a ritual practice imagine they need equipment first. A robe, a blade, a particular resin, a table laid out with everything in the correct position. Almost none of that is load-bearing. You can perform a complete piece of work tonight with a pen, a scrap of paper, a match and twenty minutes that nobody else claims. The apparatus in most traditions grew up for reasons that had more to do with guild economics and inherited aesthetics than with what happens during a working. that does not render it worthless; it only makes it optional, and optional is a far easier place to begin from. What follows looks at what the traditional lists of tools actually contain, which parts carry a function and which are pure frame, and how to decide where the line sits for you. It also touches the one material question that is a real question rather than an aesthetic one: what you are permitted to burn, where you are, and why that answer changes so sharply between neighbouring towns.

An evening with what is already in the drawer

A complete first working fits in twenty minutes and needs the space of a table. You want somewhere private, something to write with, a surface to put your hand on, and a flame you can light and put out on purpose. Everything practitioners argue about, the coloured candles laid in a fixed order, the knife with no other function in the house, the cloth folded to a pattern nobody can state the reason for, is refinement. It arrived later and it can wait. Starting with the bare minimum has one advantage over starting with the full kit: you find out which parts of the practice actually need the object, because the object was never there to be doing it.

This is not an argument against equipment. It is an argument about order. If you acquire everything first, you have no way of telling whether a working went well because of the arrangement or because of the person performing it. If you acquire almost nothing, every later addition becomes a deliberate test. That is a much more useful position to be in, because what you end up with is a practice you understand rather than one you are performing for an audience.

Correspondence, stated as plainly as it can be stated

Correspondence is the practice of linking qualities across separate categories. Each planet, metal, colour, number, weekday, gemstone and hour carries a set of attributed qualities, and a working uses those links to give a symbolic form to an intention. Fire and the Sun and Tuesday and copper and the number one all sit in one cluster for reasons that are partly traditional and partly arbitrary. Nobody has ever shown that copper conducts solar qualities. The linking is a memory device, and a good one.

The practical value shows up when you are trying to hold a large intention in your head. A sigil, a colour, a day and a material together compress a paragraph of specification into something you can act on without reconstructing it. That compression is the mechanism most people are actually using when they describe themselves as working with correspondences.

Where the tables came from and what they are for

The most-cited source for Western correspondence tables is Agrippa's Three Books of Occult Philosophy, printed in 1533. It was the first major printed synthesis of the planetary, angelic and demonic hierarchies assembled in Europe, and it remains a primary source for the tables that followed it. Later writers expanded, disagreed and cross-listed. Crowley reshaped them for the twentieth century. Nearly every modern table you will find descends from that 1533 arrangement, sometimes heavily.

Reading that lineage is worth a couple of hours because it dissolves a great deal of mystique. When you see that a table was a sixteenth-century arrangement refined by a nineteenth-century occultist and reprinted by a twentieth-century publisher, you can weigh its claims properly. A table built that way is a historical document about a synthesis. It is not a report from a laboratory, and nothing in its arrangement depends on a mechanism.

Scent, and the one sense memory keeps

olfactory memory is unusually durable, and largely separate from verbal memory; smell is the one sense whose pathways run without an initial relay through the thalamus, and the associations it forms tend to persist without the decay that applies to other memories. That is the entire reason a distinctive incense works as a ritual anchor. You are not loading the smoke with anything. You are building a cue that later retrieves a state you have already been in.

The cue works best when it is unusual and slightly abrasive at high concentration. A room that smells strongly of a resin nobody burns in that building becomes associated with the work within a handful of sessions, and the association holds for months afterwards. A pleasant, common fragrance manages none of this, because it arrived with associations of its own and will absorb yours into them without competing for the job.

Flame, and why the match is the instruction

Traditional instructions reach for a match rather than a candle for a reason the manuals rarely state plainly. A match gives you a small flame you control completely, and it supplies its own ending. A candle keeps burning whether or not you have finished. The dismissal step, which is where improvised workings most often go wrong, has been designed out of existence by choosing a flame that stops when you tell it to stop.

That reasoning generalises beyond candles. Anything in a working that has an unambiguous end state is worth preferring over something that must be manually concluded, because a manual conclusion is the step most likely to be skipped by someone who is tired. Choose instruments whose use completes itself, and you remove an entire category of failure from the design.

Smoke, and the question of what you are burning

Resins, woods, herbs and synthetic incense compounds all end up performing the same kind of act: a sustained, directed combustion in a small space. The traditional lists are long and specific, and some of the entries carry real pharmacological weight rather than symbolic weight. You do not need that weight for a working to function, and you do not need it badly enough to justify filling an enclosed room with smoke for the duration.

Ventilation is the practical constraint that decides most of this. A working in a sealed room with a resin burner is an irritation risk with no ritual upside attached to it. The most useful material decision available to a beginner is opening a window first and worrying about the rest of the correspondences afterwards. If a tradition requires a sealed room, that requirement is doing something other than protecting you.

Substance, legality, and the parts to leave alone

Several plants with a long history in religious and magical use also have a legal status that differs completely between jurisdictions, and in some places between neighbouring states. This includes cannabis in some Hindu, Rastafarian and folk-Christian contexts, and it includes a short list of compounds that are controlled outright almost everywhere. History inside a tradition is not a defence anywhere I am aware of, and a practice that depends on a controlled substance is a practice that one arrest can end permanently.

The workable advice is to build the working so that it functions without any controlled substance at all, and then treat the substance question as settled rather than open. A working that only operates with the substance is more fragile than it appears, because it quietly teaches you that the substance was the part that mattered.

What counts as a tool and what counts as an accessory

A tool earns its place by changing what you are able to do. An accessory earns its place by telling you and other people what you are doing. Both have value, but only one of them is performing any work, and it is worth being able to say which is which about your own kit. Four questions will usually settle it.

The last of those questions is the one that matters most. Equipment bought for other people's eyes accumulates faster than equipment bought for the working, and the accumulation is easy to mistake for progress. A small kit you reach for without thinking about it is worth more than a complete one that has to be laid out before you can begin anything.

  • Does it change the physical action of the working, or only its appearance?
  • Would the working still be possible without it, at some small cost?
  • Is it stored somewhere you would not keep a kitchen implement?
  • Would you still want it if nobody ever saw you using it?

A first working using objects you already own

Here is a complete one, with nothing added that was not already in the room. The instructions are deliberately plain, because at this stage the form of the sequence is doing the teaching and the ornament would only get in the way of learning it. The whole sequence fits comfortably inside a single evening and can be repeated with a different sentence tomorrow.

Do it again the next day with a different sentence, and again the day after. The repetition is not accumulating power inside the notebook. It is building an association between a short, specific physical sequence and a state of attention, and that association is the thing you can then carry into work that looks nothing like this one.

  1. Write one imperative sentence and then leave it alone.
  2. Reduce the sentence to a glyph, quickly and without redrawing it.
  3. Place the notebook where it will survive the next thirty days.
  4. Light a match, touch the page, close it, extinguish the flame.
  5. Write one line in the log before you leave the room.

Building a kit at the pace of a year

The one-year delay is intentional. Buying a full kit in the first week means you have no idea which objects your practice actually leans on, and the temptation to explain a miss by the absence of a particular item becomes very strong. Acquiring slowly keeps the variable count low enough that a miss still means something when it happens.

Notice that most of the additions in that schedule are storage and clearance rather than power. A dish for ash, a cloth kept separate from household use, a bin bag for spent material. These are the objects that make a working repeatable, and repeatability is what produces a record worth having.

  1. Month one: a pen, some paper, a match and an open window.
  2. Month three: a small dish for ash and a cloth kept separate.
  3. Month six: one incense you genuinely like, not a shelf of six.
  4. Month twelve: revisit everything and remove what is never called for.

Using a correspondence table without pretending it is a machine

Use the table to choose, never to prove. If you are working on a matter of communication and the table offers Mercury, and Mercury is associated with the day you happen to have free, that is good enough reason to work on that day. The table has told you something you already half knew, in a form you will actually remember, and consulting it cost you nothing. That is the entire return on the apparatus.

The failure mode is treating a mismatch as a block. If the correspondences do not line up for the work you want to do, the traditional response is to wait for a better alignment and the modern response is to work anyway while noting the compromise in the log. The second is almost always more productive, and both are defensible provided you are honest about which one you chose.

The point at which apparatus replaces practice

There is a stage where collecting substitutes for doing, and it is not always obvious from the inside because collecting feels like progress. The signs are reliable enough if you look for them. You are reading the history of materials more often than you are working with them. You have deferred a working because you could not find the correct item for it. You can describe the arrangement in detail and cannot say what happened the last four times you performed it.

The cure is not to throw the kit out. It is to schedule at least one working each week in which only the bare minimum is permitted, with everything else treated as unavailable for the duration. Most practices that get stuck recover immediately, because the object of the working was never the object to begin with.

The record worth keeping when materials change

A one-line log with four fields is sufficient: the date, the sentence you worked, the materials actually used, and the outcome you observed inside a window you stated in advance. The materials field is the one people drop, and it is the one that eventually makes the whole corpus worth anything, because it is the only field that reveals whether a particular object is associated with better results than the rest of the kit.

Keep the failures, which is the harder discipline. A log containing no failures is not a record of a practice, it is a selection from one. After four months, the entries you would have been embarrassed by are the ones that show you precisely where the practice is not working and what to change next.

  • Date, worked sentence, materials used, outcome inside the window.
  • One further line on what else was happening that week.
  • Nothing more. Extra columns are where a log quietly goes to die.

What you can safely skip for a whole year

Elemental balances calculated to two decimal places. Colour correspondences applied meticulously to a sigil you charged alone at midnight. Lamps of light constructed for a planet you were not working with. Ritual purification of a bedroom you have slept in for six years. Cabalistic name calculations for a working that already has a name. None of these are forbidden, and several are worth learning eventually. They are simply later, and doing them early tends to make the practice feel like bookkeeping rather than practice.

Spend the year on the four things that carry most of the method: a stated intent, a reduction, a charge, and a record. Add everything else only once a specific failure has taught you what you are missing. That is close to what the founders of the modern tradition advised, and it survives contact with the apparatus considerably better than the apparatus itself does.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which part of this is the part that costs money?

Very little of it, and the part that does cost money is rarely the part that helps. A better pen, a sturdier notebook and matches that light reliably are the useful purchases. Sigil generators, correspondence tables and ritual builders are conveniences that remove a few minutes of arithmetic from a process that was going to occupy you for that long regardless. Buy them if you enjoy them and keep the record either way, because a log maintained by hand is the one thing a purchased tool will never do on your behalf.

Why does everyone say match instead of candle?

Almost every manual offers a reason involving intention and smallness of will, and that reason was usually written after the fact. The practical origin is duller. A match can be put out in a single motion without leaving anything behind, so the working arrives at its own conclusion rather than depending on a tired person remembering one. If you prefer a candle, use a candle and accept that the ending has become your responsibility instead of the flame's.

Do correspondences matter mechanically?

No, and nobody has produced evidence that they do. What they do is give a large intention a compact and memorable form, so that when you sit down you are not rebuilding the specification from scratch. Used consistently they also give a practice a stable internal structure, which has value on its own account. Treat them as a notation and they will serve you well for years. Treat them as a mechanism and every correspondence question becomes an argument you cannot win.

Do I need expensive ritual tools?

You need a pen, some paper, a surface, a flame and a private hour. That traditional minimum is genuinely sufficient for a complete working. The expensive apparatus in most lineages is inherited rather than functional, and acquiring it early makes it harder to tell which parts of your practice are doing any work. Start bare, add a single object when a specific failure has taught you what is missing, and the kit you end up with will fit your working rather than an illustration of one.

Is there anything here worth paying for at all?

Two things, possibly. A reliable calendar or lunar phase reference, because getting the date wrong wastes a working outright. And a simple timer, because the instruction to keep the charge short is far easier to follow with something enforcing it than with your own sense of elapsed time. Neither is a ritual requirement. Both remove a small predictable source of avoidable error, which is a better reason to spend money than any claim about the effect itself.
