Wealth and Abundance

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 11 min read

Wealth practice has a reputation for being complicated and it is not. Almost everything worth doing in this area can be attempted by someone who owns a pen, a notebook and about fifteen minutes, and almost everything that goes wrong in this area goes wrong because the target was vague or the review was never scheduled. This is a first-month guide, written in the order you would actually do the work. It covers naming what you want in a way somebody else could check, reducing that to a sigil, charging it, closing it properly, logging the entry, and deciding at the end of the window whether the whole thing earned a second cycle. It is deliberately unglamorous, because the unglamorous version is the one that still functions in month four when the novelty has worn off and the only thing left is the sequence.

Day one, write down what you actually want money for

Not how much. What for. A specific purchase, a specific debt, a specific number of weeks of expenses. A statement in the form I want five hundred by the fifteenth is answerable. A statement in the form I want more money is not, and it will produce a working that can never be evaluated because there is no condition attached to it. The purpose of writing this on day one is not to be dramatic about it. It is to give the rest of the process something to point at.

If you find you cannot write a specific sentence, that is useful information and you should sit with it rather than proceeding. Most people who cannot state the target can state it after twenty minutes of thinking, and the ones who still cannot are usually trying to escape something rather than obtain something. That is a real thing to discover and it is much cheaper to discover it on page one than in month three.

Day two, pick a target that somebody could observe

Test your sentence against a hostile reader. Would somebody who does not believe in any of this, reading only your log, be able to say whether it worked. If the answer is no, the sentence is not finished. Balance in an account is observable. A bank balance is not. The number of enquiries from a particular employer is observable. Whether an employer regards you as a serious candidate is not, and a working aimed at the second will generate a permanent supply of unresolvable questions.

This constraint feels severe for about a day and then it becomes the most valuable habit you acquire in this work. Almost every frustrating ambiguity in the genre comes from a target that nobody, including you, could have evaluated. Choose targets with a number or a date attached and the entire rest of the practice becomes straightforward bookkeeping.

Day three, reduce the request to one command

Reduce the sentence to a glyph, in whatever method you use, and do it once. If you have never done this before, the manual method is better for the first few attempts because you can see what you are doing. A letter-per-line reduction, then remove the letters you do not need, then join the remaining strokes. It takes ten minutes and it will be ugly. Ugly is fine, and a first attempt that looks crude is a better starting point than an hour spent on software you have not used yet.

The important part is the constraint of producing exactly one glyph from the sentence. Not a page of variations, not a design you iterated on. One. If you produce several you will spend weeks choosing between them and will charge none of them, which is the most common way this practice stalls before it has started.

Day four, charge it badly and finish anyway

Draw or display the glyph, focus on the sentence rather than the drawing, hold the state for about a minute, and then close it. That is a complete charge. The instruction in the literature is longer and describes attention rather than duration, and a person who cannot stay present for the full prescribed length will abandon the working rather than shorten it, and an abandoned working is worse than a short one.

The closing matters more than the length. Put the object somewhere you will not handle it again before the window closes, take down anything you set up, and leave. The discipline of an unambiguous ending is what stops a working from hovering in the background of your attention for the next six weeks, and it costs about fifteen seconds.

Day five, declare the window in advance

Write the review date on the same page as the sentence, before the charge rather than after. One to three months is the range most practitioners land on, and the choice between them matters less than the fact of choosing. Also write, on the same page, the specific thing you will look at on that date. Writing the observation in advance is what stops the review from becoming a conversation with yourself about how things feel.

Then put the notebook somewhere out of sight. Not in a drawer you will open every few days. This is the part that feels like suppression and is actually just a refusal to check, and the checking is what turns a practice into a compulsion. If you look at it daily you will find something that counts as a result, which is a way of feeling productive that is entirely disconnected from whether the method works.

Week two, do the boring part on schedule

This is where the practice is actually made and where almost everyone stops. The working was one evening. The next eleven weeks are a matter of doing the one specific action your sentence implied, on a schedule, without re-ritualising it. Send the invoice. Make the call. Submit the thing. The sigil is not the thing. The scheduled action is the thing, and the sigil is what made you choose it.

If you find week two difficult, the difficulty is not a sign that you are doing it wrongly. It is the ordinary friction of doing a thing you have deferred, and it is exactly the friction the method is designed to remove. Everyone who keeps a log for three months describes this same week as the least impressive and the most decisive.

Week three, check the log before you check the bank

At the halfway point, read your own entry before you look at anything external. The entry contains the sentence, the date, the target and the observation. Reading it first reminds you what you were actually testing, which is not always the same as what you have been thinking about. People routinely discover at this point that their attention has drifted to a different outcome from the one they wrote down, and that drift is worth noticing because it will be there at the review.

If the log says you were testing one thing and your attention has been on another, decide deliberately which you are testing. Either is fine. Silently switching is what produces a record full of ambiguous results that teaches you nothing. The correction takes thirty seconds and it separates a compounding practice from a growing pile of anecdotes.

Week four, decide whether to run a second cycle

By the end of the first window you should have a yes or a no, and either is useful. A yes means the observation produced something and a second cycle is warranted, ideally with one variable changed so you learn something. A no means the observation produced nothing, which is a genuinely valuable result and not a reason to escalate. Escalating after a miss is the characteristic error of this genre and it converts an ordinary null into a financial problem.

If the answer is no, the most useful next step is a different target rather than a more expensive version of the same one. If the answer is yes, change exactly one thing about the second cycle and keep everything else identical, so that the comparison means something. Changing five things is the standard mistake and it makes two cycles worth exactly as much as one.

What belief is doing in this, and what it is not

The tradition is unusually clear that no belief is required, and this is a real feature of the method rather than a reassuring addition. A sceptic can perform the whole sequence, keep the log, and receive whatever the log says, and everything that is going to happen still happens. The method does not require you to hold any particular view about the world.

What belief does, in practice, is supply motivation. A person who expects the working to work will follow the schedule in week two. A person who thinks it is nonsense will not, and the practice will fail for the uninteresting reason that they did not do the boring part. That is the entire role belief plays in this, and it is a role that can be supplied by curiosity, by stubbornness, or by an honest desire to find out, none of which require a supernatural commitment.

Reduction instead of petition

The glyph works as a compression device. A sentence about money can be twenty words long and mean several overlapping things. A glyph cannot. Reducing it forces you to strip the sentence to its operative content, and that stripping is where the value sits. People who write the sentence and then reduce it frequently discover at reduction time that the sentence contained three aims and could not be charged without choosing between them.

The other function is temporal. A spoken intention is made once and dissipates. A glyph is a fixed object that keeps representing the sentence every time you see it, which is a passive maintenance of the goal at no cost to your attention. That is a modest but real benefit, and it is entirely ordinary psychology rather than anything unusual. If sigilisation appeals to you, this is why. If it does not, writing the sentence five times works about as well.

The correspondence you can use and the one you can skip

For a first month, use exactly one correspondence and let it go. If you want a colour for a matter of gain, pick one from a table, use it, and do not consult a second source. The reason is that correspondence systems are internally inconsistent between writers, and cross-checking them in your first month means you will find contradictions on day two and conclude that the whole thing is arbitrary, which is defensible and is not the use you need from it this month.

What a single correspondence does for you is give the working a defined shape, which makes the log more specific and the practice easier to repeat. That is a modest benefit and it is real. What it does not do is improve the odds, and no amount of additional correspondence will. If you enjoy the table, use it heavily. If you do not, the practice is unaffected by your answer.

Where the compulsion risk appears first

It appears as checking, and it appears quickly. The first time you look at your account more often than once a week, something has shifted. The mechanism is not mysterious. You are anxious about an outcome you cannot control, and looking is the only available action, and the looking reduces the anxiety briefly without producing information. That loop is the foundation of a great many unhelpful behaviours and it responds to the same treatment every time.

Set the review date, and treat the interval as final. If the anxiety between now and then is unbearable, the honest response is to reduce the intensity of the work rather than to increase the frequency of checking. Most of the people who get into financial difficulty through this kind of practice got there by adding a second working when the first one appeared to fail, not by any supernormal event.

The tools that earn their place in month one

A notebook you will not tidy away, a pen, and something that measures time. A calendar or lunar reference is worth having if you intend to use dates at all, and a timer is worth having if you intend to keep the charge short, which you should. Everything else can wait, and much of what is sold as essential for sigil work is a convenience for a process that takes about ten minutes by hand.

A sigil generator is a reasonable purchase if you intend to work regularly, mostly because it removes a small amount of tedium. It is not going to produce a better glyph than the manual method. Check that any tool you buy stores nothing about you beyond what you type, because a record of your financial intentions is not something you want on a server you do not control, and this is a genuine and frequently overlooked concern in this particular corner of the market.

What to do in month two, and what to stop doing

Month two is for exactly one thing: running a second cycle with one variable changed. Not three workings, not a new system, not a course. Two cycles with a comparable design will tell you more about the method than any amount of reading, and they are the minimum needed to say anything at all about whether this works for you.

Stop doing anything that requires escalating investment after a miss. Stop checking the outcome between windows. Stop adding new correspondences, new objects and new rules during the cycle, because every addition makes the result uninterpretable. And stop treating an ordinary null as a signal that the process needs more energy, more frequency, or a different offering. Those are the three moves that turn a simple method into a problem, and avoiding all three is most of the skill.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a paid version of this that does it better?

Not at the method level. The method is a sentence, a reduction, a short charge, an ending and a log, and none of those five improve with purchased tooling. What paid versions reliably add is convenience and, more importantly, reminders. If you are the kind of person who will not send the invoice without a prompt, a tool that prompts you is worth real money. If you will send it anyway, you have bought a nicer font for a procedure you could do with a pen in ten minutes.

How much should I expect to see in the first window?

For most targets, the visible change is behavioural rather than financial in the first cycle, because a single action produces a small number and habits produce repetition. If the target is a specific payment, you may well see it. If the target is an amount, you probably will not, and that is not a reason to escalate. A first cycle that produces one completed action and a written log has already told you whether you will do the work, which is the only question worth asking this early.

Do I need a lunar phase or a particular day?

No, and treating the date as load-bearing is the most common way beginners talk themselves out of starting. Pick a date you will be available, write the review date beside it, and get on with it. The small benefit available here is that a fixed date keeps the goal in front of you on a specific day, which is a salience effect and worth having for exactly that reason. Presenting it as planetary influence adds nothing except a way to postpone indefinitely when the phase is inconvenient.

What if nothing happens at all?

Then you write that down and you run a different target. A null is the most useful thing this practice can produce, because it is the one result that tells you something specific. Escalating after a miss, which is what the genre tends to suggest, converts a null into a financial risk while teaching you nothing. The discipline of stopping after a miss is worth more to your practice than the outcome of any single working.

Should I tell anyone what I am doing?

No, and the reason is practical rather than mystical. A prediction you have told someone becomes a prediction you are maintaining socially, and social maintenance is a different activity from testing. You will find yourself protecting the statement rather than checking it, and the whole point of pre-registering an observation is that it can be lost. If you want accountability, tell a friend the review date and not the target, and ask them to check whether you looked.
