Chaos Magic: the 10 mistakes beginners make, and what to do instead

Why most first workings fail

Not because the practitioner lacked power, and not because the technique was wrong. Almost always it is one of ten things, and they are the same ten for almost everyone. They are listed here in the order they tend to appear, because the first four account for most of the failed first attempts and the last six show up once the first working has succeeded and the beginner believes the method is the problem.

None of this is a matter of belief. Every point below is about what you can observe in your own record, which is the only thing that separates a practice from a hope.

Mistake 1: treating the sigil as the spell

The sigil is a focusing device, not the working. It is a way to hold an intent in one shape so the mind stops restating it, which is what makes the charge possible. People who treat the drawing as the act spend their energy on the aesthetics of the symbol and arrive at the charge with nothing left.

What it looks like: three attempts at the same intention with a different sigil each time, and the sigil taking twenty minutes.

The correction: draw the sigil once, quickly, without judging how it looks. If you do not like the shape, draw another one, but never redraw a sigil you have already charged with. The shape is not the point and neither is the shape's beauty.

Mistake 2: charging with force instead of focus

Intensity is the most common advice beginners receive and the least useful. Charging is a matter of sustained attention, not effort. Pushing harder on a vague intention produces a vaguer intention at higher volume, and it produces tension, and tension is the opposite of what a charge needs.

What it looks like: the working ends with the practitioner tense, drained, and unsure what was done. Or a charge that is repeated four or five times because the first one did not feel like it landed.

The correction: write the intention as a single sentence in the present tense, with a subject, a verb and a condition, then charge it calmly for a fixed time, long enough to be boring. If nothing happens, that is information. It is not a verdict on your power, it is a data point about the intention.

Mistake 3: no record, so no feedback

This is the mistake that turns a practice into a hobby. A working with no record cannot be compared with the next one, so nothing accumulates. Most beginners keep a dream journal and no practice journal, which is the wrong way round: the dream is the input, the practice is the thing being trained.

What it looks like: a year of workings, a strong feeling that something worked once and no way to find out which one it was.

The correction: one line per working. Date, intention in one sentence, symbol, method, what happened, and what you would count as a miss. That is the whole format. Fifteen seconds per entry, and it is the difference between a practice and a sequence of events.

Mistake 4: changing the method every time it fails

When a working does not produce an observable result, the natural response is to try something else. Then you have two variables and no conclusion. If the method changes every time, no method is ever tested, and the practitioner concludes that nothing works, when in fact nothing was ever given the chance to work twice.

What it looks like: a different sigil system, a different charging technique and a different timing rule for every attempt, with the conclusion drawn from the last one.

The correction: hold the method constant for a run of attempts and change only the intention. Ten workings with the same method and ten intentions tell you something. Ten workings with ten methods tell you nothing except that you were busy.

Mistake 5: not defining what failure looks like

If a working does nothing, is that a failure? Maybe. But most people cannot say, because they never said in advance what they would have counted as a result. The answer arrives afterwards and it is always generous.

What it looks like: a working that produced nothing, later described as a subtle success, a delayed effect or a lesson. Sometimes that is true. Without a criterion written in advance, it cannot be told apart from self-deception.

The correction: before the working, write one sentence: "this would count as a result if...". It can be mundane. A letter arriving, a change in a recurring situation, a conversation that goes differently. What matters is that it was written before the result and that it could have been written down as a miss.

Mistake 6: starting with the Goetia

The Goetia is a hard, particular and well-documented body of work with its own conventions, its own evocation structure and its own safety considerations. It is a fine place to be after a year of sigils, and a poor first project, because a failed Goetia working tells a beginner nothing they can act on. The failure modes are too many to diagnose and the tradition expects more context than the beginner has.

The correction: start with a single sigil for a small, mundane intention. Learn to charge, to record and to define a result. The Goetia will still be there, and you will be able to tell what went wrong.

Mistake 7: skipping the mundane

Chaos magick insists on a mundane component: the walk to the mailbox, the email that gets sent, the phone call. Beginners skip it, because the magical part is more interesting. The mundane anchor is what makes the working verifiable, and without it the result cannot be observed, only felt.

What it looks like: a working with no action attached, and no way of knowing afterwards whether anything happened.

The correction: attach one ordinary action to every working. It should be small, it should be something you would notice, and it should be written in the log before the working.

Mistake 8: borrowing symbols without the system

Using a sigil from someone else, or a planetary symbol from a tradition without knowing what it stands for, is common and it produces two problems. The symbol carries meaning that was designed for a different intention, and the practitioner cannot inspect their own symbols, which is the skill the whole practice builds.

The correction: draw your own. Even a rough one. The act of constructing the character is where the intention gets encoded, and it is the only part of the process where you can be wrong in a way that teaches you something.

Mistake 9: selling the outcome

A working that is promised as a guarantee is already a worse working, for two reasons. It raises the stakes so much that attention scatters, and it makes a miss feel like a personal failure instead of a data point, which is what stops people recording misses at all.

The correction: state the intention in terms of a situation you can influence, and leave the outcome out of it. "The situation where the invoice is overdue" is a workable intention. "Money arrives" is a promise, and promises do not get charged.

Mistake 10: treating a bad working as a failed method

The opposite error, and it comes later. After a run of misses, the practitioner concludes that the method is wrong and abandons it, when the record usually shows something narrower: the intention was too vague, the symbol was drawn badly, the timing was not observed, or the mundane action was too small to notice.

The correction: read the log before changing the method. The log will usually name the problem. If it does not, then you have learned the only thing the log could teach you, which is that the method was not recorded well enough to judge.

A way to check whether you are doing this right

One page, six lines per working, and no system more complicated than that:

LineWhat it captures
Date and timeEnough to spot a pattern in timing
Intention, one sentenceWhether it was specific enough to charge
MethodHeld constant so that comparisons are possible
SymbolYour own drawing, and how it felt to draw it
Mundane actionOne ordinary act, written before the working
Result, or "no result"Recorded the same day, with the criterion from before

After ten entries, three things become visible that were invisible before: which intentions are too vague, which hours work and which do not, and whether the method is producing anything at all. None of that is available to someone who remembers their workings instead of writing them down.

FAQ

Is the sigil really necessary for a chaos magic working?

No. The sigil is a focusing device, not a requirement, and plenty of practitioners work with a written intention alone. What matters is that the intention is held in one shape for long enough to be charged. A sigil is the most common way to do that, not the only one.

How long should a charge last?

Long enough to be boring. Anything past the point of concentration is wasted effort, and anything shorter is usually just an intention without a charge. Pick a time you can hold, keep it, and keep it the same for a run of workings so that the variable stays constant.

What if I get no result at all?

Then you have a data point, which is what the log is for. Check the criterion you wrote before the working, and check whether the intention was specific and the mundane action observable. If both were sound, change one thing and repeat. That is the whole method.

Does this mean chaos magic does not work?

It means the practice is trainable. Nothing on this list is about whether the tradition is true. It is about whether you can tell, after the fact, what you did.
