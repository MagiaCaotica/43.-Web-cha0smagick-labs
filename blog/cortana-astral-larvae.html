Cortana and the Astral Larvae: Sleep Paralysis, Named Correctly

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 6 min read

The felt presence at the edge of sleep has a name, and the name is sleep paralysis. The presence is real as an experience. The interpretation attached to it, an astral visitor, an incubus, a foreign entity lodged in the house, is a story layered on top by a long line of people who found the experience frightening and needed an explanation. This is not a debunking exercise. The experience is vivid, the sensation of a presence in the room is reliably reported by a large fraction of people who have had an episode, and the correct description is more useful for reducing the episodes than the occult one. That is the whole argument of this piece: naming the mechanism gives you something to change.

Two sleep states, two entirely different phenomena

Two conditions get called nightmare and are different in almost every relevant way. One occurs during deep non-REM sleep, is most common in children, involves intense autonomic arousal, and is usually a single event rather than a recurring state. That is a night terror.

The other occurs during REM sleep with the muscle inhibition that normally accompanies it still in place. The body is in physiological REM and cannot move, the mind is substantially awake, and the episode ends with full motor recovery. That is sleep paralysis, and the sense of a presence in the room is one of the most commonly reported features.

The distinction matters clinically because they call for different responses. Night terrors need reassurance and often resolve with age. Recurrent sleep paralysis needs sleep hygiene and, in some cases, medical attention. If you have been treating one as the other, you have been applying the wrong intervention.

Sleep paralysis and the intruder frame

The intruder hallucination is a specific, well-documented subtype. The sleeper wakes into REM atonia and perceives a presence at the bedside, which may be human-shaped, non-human, or simply felt as a spatial sense of something there. The perception is not imagined in the sense of being fabricated, which is why it is frightening, and it is not external either.

Predictive processing accounts explain it without needing an intruder. The brain is running a threat-detection system while unable to move, and it is also applying an incomplete perceptual model to a room it cannot currently see clearly. Presence detection in darkness and during immobility is a highly conserved function, and false positives are exactly what you would expect from a system tuned that way.

The subjective conviction is high because the system generating the presence signal is the same system that generates your ordinary sense of someone being in a room. From the inside, an inference and a perception are indistinguishable, and the emotional state of being unable to move amplifies the attribution enormously.

Hypnagogic imagery and why it feels like a visitor

The imagery that accompanies a sleep paralysis episode is generally visual, often unformed, and drawn from whatever is salient. A threat you were worried about that afternoon appears as a shape. A figure from a dream in progress persists for a few seconds after waking.

Because perception and dream imagery share neural substrate, an image that appears at the moment of waking is experienced with full perceptual weight rather than the reduced weight of a dream. A vague figure in a dream is easy to dismiss. The same vagueness at the moment of waking, with the room still dark and the body paralysed, is experienced as someone standing there.

This is the same mechanism as the dream recall phenomenon in lucid dream practice, and it is why lucid dreamers report that the transition is often unnoticed. The experience is not a supernatural intrusion. It is a dream state leaking into waking perception through a door that happens to be open for a few seconds.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an astral parasite and do they exist?

Astral parasite is a modern practitioner term for a presence attributed to a discarnate or non-human entity during sleep. There is no evidence for an external agent and a well-documented mechanism for the experience, so the correct reading is a sleep phenomenon with a mythological overlay rather than a visitor.

Can you make them leave?

A banishing performed at the moment of waking will feel like it worked, because the episode ends within seconds either way. What you can change is the frequency, and the interventions that have evidence behind them are sleep regularity, side sleeping, stress reduction and reducing sleep deprivation. The ritual has an unmeasured effect and the habit changes do not.

Is it dangerous to have sleep paralysis?

It is uncomfortable rather than medically dangerous, and the episodes themselves are not harmful. It becomes a problem when frequency causes sleep avoidance, when the fear response is strong enough to produce anxiety that persists into the day, or when the experience is indistinguishable from another condition. Those three warrant a clinician rather than a grimoire.

Why do the episodes come in clusters?

Because the underlying conditions cluster. Sleep deprivation, irregular schedules, jet lag, stress peaks and sleeping on your back are all commonly associated with a cluster, and clusters usually follow a period where several of those apply at once. Finding which one is active in your case is most of the work.

Should I use an app to track these episodes?

A log is the honest first move, and a one-time log purchase beats a service that keeps billing you to look at data you already own. What you need recorded is the date, the time, the position you were in, how long you had been awake before, and how much sleep you had. Three or four episodes of data is enough to start seeing a pattern.
