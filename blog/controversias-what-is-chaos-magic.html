CONTROVERSIAS

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 14 min read

Almost every dispute about this subject that gets aired publicly is about vocabulary, and almost all of that is settled. The name is a pun, the founding documents are short, the lineage is documented, and none of this is in interesting contention. The arguments that actually have substance are about four things: whether what emerged in the nineteen-seventies is a religion in any meaningful sense, what to do with a founder whose private correspondence makes him impossible to admire uncritically, what it means for a practice built on appropriation to keep using material it took without asking, and what the effect has been of the single most quotable slogan anyone in the field has produced. This is a reference sheet rather than a position paper, and it is built to be argued with. Each dispute below has the strongest case on both sides, stated without a strawman, and a note on what would change the assessment. Where there is no honest dispute, the entry says so, because a list of settled questions presented as open ones is itself a form of the problem.

The word on the tin, and what it settles

The name is a pun. This has been established since the first publication and it is not contested by anybody with a serious position, though it is raised often enough by people who have not read the founding documents that it is worth disposing of once. The name joins the ordinary English phrase for disorder to a title drawn from an earlier text, and the joining is deliberate rather than accidental. What is sometimes missed is that this is an act of affiliation, not a description. Taking a name from a specific earlier work is a way of announcing which tradition the writer considers themselves continuous with, and the announcement is part of what the practice then does.

So the settled question is: yes, it is a pun, yes, the affiliation is deliberate, yes, the founder's earlier work is the reference point. What remains genuinely open is a narrower and less comfortable question, which is whether an affiliation announced through a title is the right mechanism for a practice whose stated contribution is methodological rather than lineage-based. If your contribution is that claims should be tested rather than inherited, then attaching yourself to a lineage by name is a slightly odd way to announce it. This is not a serious objection. It is a real tension in the position, and it is the kind of thing that separates a practitioner from a propagandist.

The dispute that has actual teeth

If you have to pick the one that matters, it is the question of whether this is a religion. The argument against is straightforward: there is no body of claims about what is the case, no required practice that a community enforces, no external standard a member must meet, and the defining instruction is to test rather than believe. On that reading, a member can hold any metaphysical position at all, including none, and remain entirely within the practice, which is not a shape that religions have.

The argument for is equally serious. A religion, strictly, is a set of practices and a body of material that a group sustains and transmits, and by that definition this is one. There is a founding date, a small body of foundational text, a lineage of transmission, initiatory material, and an organised structure that has persisted for five decades with continuing internal disputes, which is the part most often left out of accounts. Secular organisations do all of that too, which is why the argument cannot be settled by the definition. What actually settles it, in practice, is that the label is doing rhetorical work: it is used to make the group look either more legitimate or more like a cult, depending on who is speaking. Drop the label and the substantive questions become much easier to discuss.

Whether this is a religion

The useful move here is to stop treating this as one question. It is four questions that have been run together, and they have different answers. Is there a community? Yes, a small one, with internal argument. Is there a body of transmissible material? Yes, a very small one, and its smallness is a genuine problem for anyone wanting a complete system. Is there a requirement of belief? No, and this is the load-bearing observation. Is there a body of claims about what is the case? No, and this is the other load-bearing observation. Most of the heat in this dispute comes from the first two being treated as decisive, when they are not what distinguishes religion from other durable groups.

What would change the assessment is evidence about behaviour rather than structure. If members began to acquire leadership on the basis of claimed authority rather than demonstrated competence, or if the practice began to require a commitment that people could not leave without social cost, the label would start to fit better. None of that has happened at scale, and the people who have predicted the slide have been predicting it for thirty years with no supporting evidence. The most defensible summary is that this is a durable practice community with a minimal canon and no belief requirement, which is a real and unusual shape, and that the argument is being fought over a word rather than over a description.

The inheritance question nobody skips

The founder of the earlier tradition this practice names itself after left a large private correspondence, and in it he expressed views about race that were explicit, repeated andugly. This is documented, and it is not something that can be resolved by noting that the work is separate from the correspondence, because the work was produced by the same person with the same assumptions. The honest description is that the earlier tradition contains both a body of practice material that many people find genuinely useful and a set of written views that make its founder impossible to hold without qualification.

On one side of this is the position that the material is separable from the person and should be used, with the person acknowledged. On the other is the position that a practice which borrows a figure as its central motif and calls the borrowing an affiliation is making that person's framework central, and centrality cannot be qualified away by a footnote. Neither position is unreasonable and there is no settlement available, because the dispute is about what a lineage is for. The practical consequence is that a person who has made their own commitment to testing claims rather than inheriting them has a slightly awkward position, and the honest thing to do with the awkwardness is to state it rather than to resolve it rhetorically.

The export that damages the practice

Of everything in this list, the slogan is the most consequential export and the least examined. The line that nothing is true and everything is permitted was written to state a discipline about the difference between a practitioner's perception and the world's contents, and it has been quoted continuously for decades as though it were a licence. The most common use is to justify an action that would need justifying anyway, and the effect on the person using it is reliably negative: having invoked the slogan, they find the matter settled and stop thinking. That is the exact opposite of what a discipline of sincerity is for.

There is a second and more damaging export, and it is not a quotation. It is a general climate in which any result is attributed to the practice. This is not taught anywhere, so far as can be established, and it is nonetheless the standard that newcomers absorb within a year, because it is what the surrounding culture rewards. A practice that can absorb any outcome has no way of being wrong, and a practice that cannot be wrong teaches nothing. Practitioners who have lasted have almost all found their way to the opposite standard, usually after a period of several years in which they believed the first one and could not understand why nothing ever improved. Getting there sooner is the single most useful thing a beginner can do for themselves, and the test is simple, which is whether the practice is capable of disappointing you.

The disputes that are only vocabulary

A substantial share of public argument is about matters that were resolved decades ago and that nobody who has read the founding texts still disputes. Whether the term is a pun. Whether the earlier text contains the figure the name refers to. Whether the founding pamphlets are short. Whether the practice has a stated metaphysical position. All of these are settled and the answers are boring, and the arguments about them are usually being conducted by people who have not read the sources and who are using the question as a proxy for whether the whole thing is respectable. The polite response is to answer it once, accurately, and move on.

The reason this matters is not pedantry. Repeatedly re-litigating settled questions makes a practice look less mature than it is, and it crowds out the questions that would actually improve anyone's practice. When someone asks whether the name is a pun, the interesting question behind it is almost always whether there is anything here worth learning, and the answer to that does not depend on the etymology. Practitioners who find themselves enjoying these arguments should notice that they are enjoying the argument rather than the practice, and that this is a good use of an evening exactly once and a poor use of it annually.

The map, with a position on each side

Here is the reference, and the point of it is that each row has a real disagreement in it. Religion or not: leans towards no, on the grounds of no belief requirement and no claim set, with the acknowledgement that the group has the structural features of one. Founder's writings: leans towards use with acknowledgement, on the grounds that the practice material is separable, with the acknowledgement that centrality cannot be qualified by a footnote. Appropriation: leans towards the discomfort being appropriate and permanent, on the grounds that a method can be borrowed without a question being asked of the people it was taken from. Slogan: leans towards it being a misreading, with the acknowledgement that the damage is already done. Later trajectory: leans towards continuity, with the acknowledgement that continuity with a founder is not a virtue by itself.

The map is useful for three things. It stops you from being surprised by an argument you have not met, which matters more than it sounds, because most of the difficulty beginners have comes from discovering at a gathering that a position they hold has a standard objection. It gives you something to say that is not evasive. And it makes clear that the honest answer to most of these is a position with a cost attached, which is what distinguishes a considered view from a slogan, including the slogans of people who are otherwise on your side.

What the founding documents actually asked

Both foundational pamphlets are short, and their actual content is narrower than their reputation. What they ask for is a specific procedure: write an aim, reduce it to a glyph, charge the glyph, record the results, and be prepared for the possibility that the record will not show what you hoped. The instruction that appears in both is that experience should be the test, not doctrine. Neither document makes claims about the nature of reality that a member must accept, and neither sets out a system to be mastered. Read in order, the two of them are a manual for running experiments, and the metaphysical content attributed to them is largely a later accretion from commentary and from the earlier tradition they draw on.

This matters for the disputes, because most of the hard positions above depend on the practice having a metaphysics, and by its own founding documents it does not. A tradition with no required belief is much harder to call a religion, and a tradition with no doctrine cannot be accused of dogmatism, because there is nothing to be dogmatic about. What it can be accused of is something worse, which is being unfalsifiable in practice while claiming to be empirical in theory. That is the live criticism, it is well founded, and it is a criticism of practitioners rather than of the documents.

The affiliation built into the name

The affiliation is worth returning to because it is a design decision rather than a fact of nature. A name that announces continuity with an earlier figure does work: it tells a prospective member what the practice is in dialogue with, it places the newcomer inside a lineage, and it makes certain arguments available in advance. The cost is that it also forecloses options. Once the practice has a name that references a specific figure, every question about that figure becomes a question about the practice, and the practice inherits an obligation to deal with the figure's writings that it might otherwise not have.

Practitioners who use the name and disagree with this decision have a practical option, which is to use the name for identification and to describe the practice by its method when discussing what it does. This is what most careful practitioners do without making a point of it. The ones who make a point of it tend to be pushing the other way, in favour of a cleaner break, and their position is coherent too, though it has the odd effect of being more dependent on the earlier figure than the position it opposes, since you cannot break from a name by pointing out that you never needed it.

Where the map is useful

The map is useful in three specific situations and useless outside them. In the first, you are new and you have just been given a position by someone you respect, and you want to know whether it has an obvious answer. In the second, you are in a conversation where somebody is confidently wrong about a settled matter, and you need to know whether correcting them is worth the social cost. In the third, you are about to describe your practice to someone outside it, and the description will land differently depending on whether you invoke the lineage or the method.

The third situation is where it earns its keep most reliably. When you explain this practice to somebody outside it, the first question is almost always about the group, and the second is about whether it is safe, and neither question is really about what the practice does. If you lead with the method, you get a conversation about method, which is the one that can be had. If you lead with the lineage, you get a conversation about the lineage, which is the one that has been had for thirty years and goes nowhere. This is a small piece of practical technique rather than a position, and it will do more for recruitment of the right kind of people than any amount of argument about the disputes.

What the map cannot settle

The map cannot settle the disputes, and it would be dishonest to present it as though it could. The religion question has no answer that satisfies both sides, the founder question has no answer that permits both using the material and maintaining a clean position, and the appropriation question has no answer at all, because there is no version of it that makes the historical fact go away. Anyone offering a resolution to these is selling something, and the most common thing being sold is a decision to stop thinking about them.

What the map can do is let you hold a position with its cost visible, which is different from being certain. A practitioner who can say, this is what I think, this is the strongest objection, this is what I am giving up to hold it, is in a completely different position from one who has a confident answer. The first can be talked with and can change. The second can only be defended. If you take one thing from this reference, take the habit of naming the cost, because it is the difference between a position and a slogan, and this subject has more slogans than positions.

The version worth defending

The version worth defending is the small one. A handful of short texts, a procedure you can run in an evening, a record you keep afterwards, and a standing obligation to be willing to be wrong. That is the whole of it, and it is genuinely unusual, because most of what is called a tradition in this field is not that and cannot be. If you describe your practice in those terms to somebody outside it, the description will sound modest and will be accurate, and the modesty is not a deficiency in the description. It is the description.

The version worth defending is also the one that costs the most, because it requires being able to say that a practice failed, which is exactly the ability the culture of this field is organised to remove. Any position that avoids that requirement is not a stronger version, it is a weaker one, because a practice that cannot report failure is not a practice. If you hold this version, you will occasionally have conversations that end badly, and the people who end them well are the ones you want to be having them with. That is the whole test, and it is available to you without anyone else's permission.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is chaos magic a religion?

That is four questions run together, and the substantive ones are easier. There is a community and a transmissible body of material, so it has two of the structural features. There is no requirement of belief and no set of claims about what is the case, which are the two that distinguish religion from other durable groups. The argument is fought over a label that is doing rhetorical work rather than over a description, and dropping it makes the real questions easier to discuss.

How do you handle the founder's racial views when using the tradition?

By stating the position rather than resolving it. The correspondence is documented and the views were explicit and repeated, so there is no version of this that dissolves. The defensible position is that the practice material is separable from the person and can be used with acknowledgement, alongside the acknowledgement that a practice which borrows a figure as its central motif has made that person's framework central, and centrality cannot be qualified away by a footnote.

Is the line about nothing being true actually a licence to do anything?

It was written to state a discipline about the difference between perception and contents, and it has been used continuously as though it were a licence. The most common effect of invoking it is that the matter feels settled and the thinking stops, which is the opposite of what it was for. The more consequential problem is not the quotation but the surrounding practice of attributing any outcome to the working, since a practice that absorbs any result has no way of being wrong.

What would count as the practice being wrong?

A record kept over a fixed period that does not show the effect you were looking for, and a willingness to keep the record. That is the whole test, and it is available without anybody's permission. The reason it is worth insisting on is that the alternative standard, where any result is attributed to the practice, is not taught anywhere as far as can be established but is nonetheless the standard newcomers absorb within a year, and it is the reason a great many people in this field cannot describe what changed.

Which disputes are actually settled and not worth having?

The name as a pun, whether the earlier text contains the figure referenced, the length of the founding pamphlets, and whether the practice has a required metaphysical position. All of these are settled and boring, and arguments about them are usually conducted by people who have not read the sources. If you find yourself enjoying one of these arguments, notice that you are enjoying the argument rather than the practice, which is worth an evening once and not annually.
