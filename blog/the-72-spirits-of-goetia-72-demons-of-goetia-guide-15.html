The 72 Spirits of Goetia

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 13 min read

A goetic working done properly is mostly administrative. That sounds like a deflation of the material and it is the opposite of one. The catalogue that circulates today invites the reader to treat a name as a summons, and the sequence of steps is what stops that from happening by requiring the uncomfortable parts before the interesting ones. You pick a row from a reference before you light anything, which means you have to have already decided what the office is and what you are narrowing it to. You write the declaration as a single line, which means you cannot hide a vague intention inside a paragraph of atmosphere. You keep a record in the ten minutes after, while the memory is still uncorrected. And you carry the row for a fortnight afterwards, which is the part that separates a working from an evening. None of this is dramatic and all of it is the mechanism. The order is not a formality borrowed from ceremonial manuals. It is the part that stops inflation, because the uncomfortable decisions are placed early and the flattering ones come last, and a practitioner who follows the order cannot reach the flattering part without passing through the parts that would have told them the aim was too wide.

The working, and the order that holds it together

The goetic material is usually approached as a list of names with powers attached, and that framing produces a working which consists of picking the most impressive entry and asking for something. The sequence approach produces a different thing, and the difference is the whole of this method. The order is fixed and the fixing is the point. Row before candle, office before name, declaration before charge, record before the fortnight, and the fortnight before any conclusion. Each step produces an artefact that the next step depends on.

The reason this is worth the trouble is that the flattering version of the material is available at every single step, and the sequence is arranged so that taking the flattering option breaks the chain rather than merely feeling better. If you charge first and choose the row afterwards, you have a charge with no office attached, and a charge with no office is where a working stops being a working and becomes a wish with a ritual attached. If you conclude during the first evening, you have no record and no fortnight, which means the only evidence available is your memory of an evening you were hoping would go a particular way.

Choosing the row before the candle

The catalogue presents seventy-two entries, and the instinct on meeting that number is to look for the one whose office sounds most like what you want. The sequence asks you to do the opposite, which is to read the office as a description of a function and ask whether that function is one you could name without embarrassment in a job description. The nine Presidents, the thirty-six Dukes and the thirty Kings are grouped into families of two, three or four, and that grouping tells you which entries share a method rather than which are more powerful.

Choosing this way is slow and it is the most useful hour in the practice. A function you could write a job description for is one you will still recognise in month two. A function chosen for its reputation is one you will abandon, and abandoning it costs more than the wrong choice would have, because the abandoned entry is still in the catalogue and you will be tempted by it again. The row you pick does not have to be impressive. It has to be legible at a distance of three months, which is a much stricter test and a much more useful one.

Reading the office without inflating it

The office in a goetic entry is a description of work. That is what the word means in the context it was written, and the inflation happens when the description is read as a statement of power rather than of function. An entry described as helping with navigation is not more potent than one described as helping with the recovery of a lost object. The second description is more checkable, and checkability is the property you actually need.

There is a test that catches most of the inflation before it sets. Write the office as though you were describing a role in a small organisation, with a title and a list of tasks, and see whether it survives. If it survives, you have something to work with. If it dissolves into grandeur the moment you try to make it concrete, you have found a row that will be interesting for one evening and useless for a month. The catalogue contains plenty of both kinds and the difference is not visible until you write it out in that register.

The three ranks, and what each could do

The seventy-two are arranged in three ranks. The nine Presidents are described as the ones who operate at the level of the noble houses, the thirty-six Dukes at the level of dignities, and the thirty Kings at the level of causes. This division is a piece of organisational structure borrowed from a very different context, and it is more useful than it first appears, because it tells you the scale at which each entry was understood to act. That scale is a claim about the material's own self-understanding rather than about anything outside it, and reading it that way keeps the entry inside the text.

The practical consequence is modest and worth having. If your working is about something that happens between two people in a room, then a King, whose described level is causes, is the wrong register and a Duke is the right one, because the material's own hierarchy places smaller operations below the top rank. Choosing by register rather than by reputation is a two-minute adjustment and it removes most of the mismatch between what you are doing and what you are invoking. It also makes the choice defensible, which matters more in month two than it does on the first evening.

The families, and what the grouping is not

Entries appear in families of two, three or four, and the family is a reliable guide to method. Members of the same family were written with the same technique behind them and they tend to be described in similar language, which makes them a natural set for a first working. What the family is not is a hierarchy. There is no ordering within a family, and there is no entry that stands above the others because of the company it keeps.

This matters because the family structure is the part of the catalogue that survives every layer of modern overlay. The material descends from Jewish and Christian demonological sources, particularly the pseudepigraphal Book of Enoch, arriving through the seventeenth-century Conjuratio Ben-Maimon and the Lemuria tradition, and that history is visible in the structure rather than in the imagery. The families are older than the ranks. When you want something you can check, they are the better guide, and they are the reason a first working on a family member is usually a better bet than a first working on a famous solitary entry.

The declaration, in one line

The opening of the working is a declaration, and the sequence requires it in a single line. That constraint is doing more work than it appears to. A paragraph can contain a request, a justification, a description of the current situation, an account of previous attempts, and a fallback, and a reader who has written that paragraph has committed to none of it clearly. A single line forces a choice, and the choice usually reveals that the aim was several aims stacked on top of each other.

The line also settles the direction of the address. The historical form of a ceremonial opening is a request made upward, to something with standing. The form most people actually want is an instruction to a function, and those are different documents. Writing the line forces you to notice which one you have written, because a request upward and an instruction downward cannot be expressed in the same sentence without a small grammatical sleight of hand. The sequence does not forbid either. It asks you to know which you are using before the candle is lit.

The charge, and where the sequence stops you

The charge is attention held flat and long enough to matter, and there is no technique here that substitutes for it. What the sequence adds is a stopping point, and the stopping point is placed after the declaration rather than before it, which is where most practice puts it. By the time you reach the charge you have already committed to an office in writing, and a commitment made in writing is very hard to argue with when the charge produces nothing and you would prefer to believe something more dramatic happened.

This is the mechanism by which the sequence defends against inflation, and it is worth being explicit about it. Inflation in this material almost always begins as a failure of aim, not a failure of technique. A person whose office was written down as a job description and whose declaration was a single line has, by the time the candle is lit, already excluded the possibility of a spectacular result. That is not a limitation to be overcome. It is the reason the working, if it does anything, produces the thing you asked for rather than the thing you wanted to have happened.

The record, in the ten minutes after

The record goes in the ten minutes after the working closes, and the window is short for a reason that is not mystical. Interpretation begins almost immediately and it is not something you can switch off. A record written at the end of the same evening is still contaminated by the part of you that wanted it to work. A record written the next morning is contaminated by the part of you that has constructed a story overnight to explain what happened.

So the entries are small and they are constrained. What was the office, in the words you wrote. What was the single-line declaration, verbatim. What happened physically, without interpretation. What was the state on closing and what was the state an hour later. Those four lines are enough to make a working auditable and they are close to impossible to fudge without noticing, because each one is a transcription rather than a recollection. The ten-minute window is what keeps them transcriptions.

The fortnight that carries it

The fortnight after the working is the part that does the work, and it is the part that gets abandoned. Two weeks is long enough for the emotional charge of the evening to settle and for ordinary life to reassert itself, and long enough to see whether anything in the target area has moved. The entries during that fortnight are not interpretations. They are notes on whether the specific thing named in the office occurred, and the discipline is to record occurrence and not quality.

A working in this material frequently produces an effect on the practitioner's own attention rather than on the target, and the fortnight is what lets you see that. If the only change is that you have become more aware of the domain for two weeks, that is a real change and it is worth writing down as one. It is not evidence about the entry. Recording it as such is the most common way these workings are misread, and the fortnight is long enough to make the distinction visible to anyone willing to keep the two columns separate.

The three places the sequence fails

The sequence fails in three specific places, and it is worth naming them because they are the places where a working goes wrong while feeling like it is going according to plan. The first is the row. Choosing an entry because of its reputation rather than its office produces an aim that cannot be written as a job description, and the failure surfaces at the declaration step as an inability to write the single line. The second is the declaration itself, where a request and an instruction get blended and the actual direction of the working is never decided. The third is the fortnight, which gets cut short by an event that makes you want the answer more than you want the record.

Each of these has a diagnostic signature. A row problem shows up as a declaration you cannot finish. A declaration problem shows up as a charge that produces a lot of feeling and no discernible direction. A fortnight problem shows up as a conclusion drawn from a week of notes. None of them is a failure of faith and none of them is remedied by trying harder. They are all addressed by going back to the step that failed and doing that one differently, which is only possible because the sequence is written down and the steps are in a known order.

The row you would actually use

If you are making a first working from this material, the entry to use is a family member at the lower register whose office is small, physical, and checkable. Something in the range of a lost object, a document, a direction, a place to sleep, a mechanism that has stopped. These exist in the catalogue precisely because medieval and early-modern compilations drew on a wide range of practical concerns, and they are the entries that survive contact with a written declaration and a fortnight of notes.

The famous solitary entries are the wrong choice for a first working and it is worth being clear that this is not a claim about their power. It is a claim about what a first working is for. A first working is a test of your own practice, and a test needs an answer you can recognise. An entry whose office is broad enough to cover anything will produce a result you can interpret in whatever direction the evening has left you, and that is precisely the outcome you were trying to avoid by working through a sequence in the first place.

What the working cannot do

Nothing in the sequence reaches into anyone else's decisions, and the material is fairly clear about this when read as a description of administrative work. An entry associated with locating something can help you find where you left it. It is not in a position to make a person who has decided against you change their mind, and a working aimed at that outcome is aimed at something the framework has no purchase on. Where the catalogue appears to promise otherwise, the promise is a later addition sitting on top of a text about function.

The same limit applies to the practitioner, and it is the more important one. A working in this material can organise attention, produce a written aim, and make a decision more difficult to avoid. Those are real effects and they are within reach. What it cannot do is supply a will that is not yours, sustain an intention you do not hold, or convert a fortnight of ordinary life into evidence of anything. The sequence exists to keep you honest about the difference, and a working that ends with the difference intact has done its job even if nothing else happened.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the order of the steps matter so much in a goetic working?

Because the order is arranged so that the flattering option is unavailable until the uncomfortable ones are behind you. Choosing the row before the candle forces the office to be decided in writing. Writing the declaration in one line forces the aim to be narrowed to something specific. Keeping the record in the ten minutes after stops interpretation from arriving first. Each step produces an artefact the next depends on, so breaking the order does not merely change the flavour of the working, it removes the evidence that would let you evaluate it.

How do I choose an entry from a catalogue of seventy-two?

Read the office as a description of a function rather than a statement of power, and ask whether you could write it as a job title with a list of tasks. Prefer an entry whose described level matches the scale of what you are working on, and note that families of two to four share a method rather than a rank. For a first working, a family member at the lower register with a physical and checkable office is the sound choice. A famous solitary entry is a poor first choice because the aim will not survive being written down.

What should go in a goetic record, and why is there a ten-minute window?

Four small entries: the office as you wrote it, the single-line declaration verbatim, what happened physically without interpretation, and your state on closing and an hour later. The ten-minute window exists because interpretation begins almost at once. A record written that evening is coloured by wanting it to have worked, and one written the next morning is coloured by an overnight story about what it meant. Transcriptions resist both. The entries are short because anything longer becomes narration.

How do the three ranks in the Ars Goetia actually help me choose?

They tell you the scale at which each entry was understood to act, which is a fact about the material's own self-understanding rather than an external claim. The nine Presidents are placed at the level of noble houses, the thirty-six Dukes at dignities, and the thirty Kings at causes. If your working concerns something that happens between two people in a room, that sits below the top register, so a Duke is a better fit than a King. Choosing by register rather than reputation takes two minutes and removes most of the mismatch between aim and entry.

Is it pointless to work with a goetic entry if the sequence cannot produce a spectacular result?

The sequence is designed to produce the specific thing you asked for rather than the thing you wanted to have happened, and the difference is the point. A working in this material will usually produce a real effect on your own attention: a written aim, a decision made harder to avoid, a fortnight of heightened noticing. Those are within reach and they are what the framework is built around. What it cannot do is reach another person's decisions or convert a fortnight of ordinary life into evidence. A working that ends with that boundary intact has worked.
