HAZEL

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

The temptation in a piece like this is to promise a sequence that ends somewhere further away than lucid dreaming. That would be dishonest, and it would also be a mistake, because lucid recall is the most tractable item in the whole dream domain and everything past it is contested. Lucid dreaming has a measurable population base and induction techniques that survive replication, out-of-body events occur for a small percentage of people and almost never at will. Remote viewing has not replicated. This page builds one practice, on the strongest ground available, and explains where it deliberately stops.

Start with recall, not with leaving

A lucid dream is one in which you know you are dreaming while it is happening. That is the whole event. There is no travel, no view of a distant place, no meeting with anyone who is not part of your own memory. It is a change in the state of awareness during sleep, and it is unusual because sleep is normally a state in which metacognition is largely offline.

This makes it a good first target for reasons that owe nothing to mysticism: it is the only dream phenomenon in the standard literature with a reliable induction literature behind it. Techniques involving reality checks and dream journaling are replicated enough that you can build on them. Everything that follows the moment of lucidity is far less certain, and the sensible beginner stops at the threshold.

Why the morning is where the yield is

Sleep cycles lengthen as the night goes on. The early cycles are dominated by deep slow-wave sleep, and the long periods of rapid eye movement that produce vivid, memorable dreams cluster in the last third of the night. Wake yourself in that window and your chance of noticing a dream at all, and of being awake enough to notice that you are dreaming, is substantially higher than if you wake at six.

This single adjustment is worth more than any technique. People who keep a dream journal for a month and then add morning waking typically report the change within a week, and the mechanism is mundane: more dreams to work with, and a shorter gap between the dream and the act of writing it down. The gap is the variable. Memory for dreams decays fast, and a practice that depends on recall will fail on recall alone.

The three checks that hold up

A reality check is a small action or question whose answer differs between waking and dreaming. Looking at your hands, reading text twice, trying to push your finger through the other hand, asking yourself whether you are dreaming. The reason it works is not any property of the action: it is that the check is a deliberate act of attention, and a deliberate act of attention is a moment of partial lucidity.

When you practise the check during the day, you are training the association between the check and a brief return of awareness. In the dream, the same act fires and the return comes with it. The most common mistake is choosing a check that depends on something unreliable. Text in dreams is unstable but readable once; light switches and mirrors behave inconsistently. Pick one check, use only that one, and use it every time you wake and a few times while awake.

A night-time practice that fits a real week

Two things, both small. A journal: whatever you remember, written the moment you are upright, even if it is four words. And a fixed waking time on weekdays, which is the part that actually produces the morning window. A practice that requires you to get up at an hour you will not get up at will last four days, and the failure will be attributed to the technique.

Give the journal thirty days before you judge it. Most people find the first week unremarkable, the second week more memorable dreams, and the third week the first clear lucid event, and the sequence is common enough that abandoning at day nine means abandoning before the technique has had a chance. If you wake at a bad hour, log what you remember anyway. A partial entry is a data point; a skipped morning is a hole in the record.

What to write at the moment of waking

Three lines, in this order: where you were, what was happening, and how you knew it was a dream. That third line is the one that matters, because it records the transition rather than the content, and the transition is what you are training. A long vivid description is pleasant and almost useless; a note that says checked my hands and the light was wrong tells you exactly what worked.

Do not interpret at this stage. Interpretation takes the memory and turns it into a story, and the story then replaces the memory in your mind, so that at the next recall you remember your interpretation rather than the dream. The raw three lines should sit in the journal for at least a month before you look for patterns, and patterns found later are more likely to be real than patterns found in week one.

The first lucid dream, and how to be sure of it

You will probably not know for certain. That is normal, and the standard of evidence people apply to their own lucid dreams is far stricter than the standard they apply to waking events, which is one reason lucid dreams are treated as less impressive than they actually are. A reasonable standard: the event was a dream, and you knew you were dreaming while it happened, and afterwards you can point to the check you used.

The dreams that get counted when they should not are the ones where you realise you was dreaming after waking up, or where you were half-asleep and thinking about a dream. Both are real experiences and neither is lucidity. The distinguishing question is whether the knowing was inside the dream. If you cannot answer that, log it as a maybe, and keep going.

Why projection is not the next step

out-of-body events occur for a small percentage of the population, and they cluster near sleep onset, during illness, and during deep meditation, rather than occurring at will. That distribution is the problem: if the state appears when the system is unusually depleted or unusually quiet, then it is not a skill you can practise, it is a state you can wait for.

The practical consequence is that a practice aimed at deliberate projection is a practice waiting for a low-probability event, and most people who build one will spend a year of daily practice with nothing to record and then conclude the method failed. Build the recall practice first. It is more reliable, it is a genuine result, and for a large share of people the sense of leaving the body is not what they were actually looking for.

What tulpa practice is and where it comes from

Tulpa work originates in Tibetan Buddhist traditions, where the method is used for visualisation training and for deity yoga. Its purpose there is disciplined concentration: the practitioner develops the ability to hold a complex image steadily in attention, which is a contemplative skill with a long history of use and a specific purpose that is not the creation of an autonomous being.

Transplanted into Western practice, the tulpa is usually described as a thoughtform with its own existence, and that description is an addition made by the tradition that received it. The training is the same either way. If you do it, the defensible claim is that sustained visualisation of a fixed form trains sustained attention on a fixed form, and the long list of independent existences reported afterwards is not evidence that any of them exists.

Remote viewing, and what the record shows

Remote viewing was funded at a number of research institutes for decades, on the promise that a viewer could describe a sealed site from a distance. The prospective results did not replicate reliably, which is the finding that ended the programmes rather than any dramatic refutation. The interesting part is the narrower finding that came out of the training: people who trained this way did improve at describing visual scenes in vivid, specific detail.

That improvement is real and explainable, and it is worth having. Describing an image in rich detail trains a describable visual attention, and a sharper visual description is valuable in drawing, in writing, and in remembering places. What the training does not do is reach across distance. Treat the discipline as a visualisation method and the record is defensible; treat it as a way of seeing somewhere else and it is not.

The four weeks, and what each one is for

Week one is baseline. Journal only, no technique, no expectation. You are establishing how much you normally remember, and without that number every later improvement is unverifiable. Week two adds the reality check during waking hours, twice a day, same check. Week three adds the fixed morning waking time, which is the change most likely to produce results.

Week four is where most people get their first clear event, and it is also where most people stop logging. Do not stop logging. The event is not the deliverable; the four-week record is. The record tells you which of the three interventions was carrying the effect, and that answer is what lets you build a practice you can keep for a decade instead of one you abandon in a month.

What to stop doing if nothing happens

Change one variable. The most common failure is running three interventions at once, waking early, checking obsessively, and journaling, then concluding after two quiet weeks that none of them work. The evidence cannot distinguish those outcomes and neither can you.

If after a full month of consistent journalling you have no event you would defend as lucid, the correct next move is to keep the journal and drop the technique, because the journal is the part that reliably improves recall and recall is the foundation. A practice that consists of writing down what you remember every morning is worth having on its own, and it is the part that will still be there in a year.

The practice after the first success

Once lucid recall is reliable, the interesting question stops being whether it happens and becomes what you do in the window. That is the natural place to stop expanding scope, and the natural place to bring in the parts of the tradition that assume lucidity is the entry condition rather than the goal: stabilising the dream state, extending the window of clear awareness, working deliberately inside it.

All of that is downstream of the four-week practice described above, and all of it is more likely to produce a record you have inflated than one you have not. The discipline to bring forward is the journalling discipline: raw entries, the knowing written down at the time, no interpretation until the month is closed. A person who keeps that standard for a year knows more about their own dreaming than one who has read everything that has been written about the subject.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is lucid dreaming scientifically real?

Yes, and it is the best-supported phenomenon in this whole area. It has a measurable population base, reliable induction techniques, and a replication literature, which is more than can be said of almost anything else in dream research. The reason it is sometimes treated as fringe is a conflation with out of body experiences, which have a very different and much weaker evidence base.

What is the best single technique for having a lucid dream?

A fixed morning waking time, combined with a reality check used consistently while awake. The waking time matters because rapid eye movement periods cluster in the last third of the night, and the check matters because it creates a brief return of awareness that the dream can then repeat. Most people who get results combine both, and most people who do not have only tried one of them.

Can you learn to have out of body experiences on demand?

The evidence does not support it, and the pattern of when they occur argues against it. They are reported by a small percentage of people and cluster near sleep onset, during illness, and during deep meditation, all of which are states you cannot schedule deliberately. A practice aimed at deliberate projection is therefore a practice waiting for an event, and that is worth knowing before you commit a year to it.

What is a tulpa and does creating one produce a real being?

Tulpa practice comes from Tibetan Buddhist traditions, where it is used for visualisation training and deity yoga, and its purpose is disciplined concentration. Western descriptions of tulpas as independent entities are an addition made by the receiving tradition. The training is genuinely useful for building steady attention on a complex image; the claim that something separate comes out of it is not supported.

Does remote viewing work?

The prospective results did not replicate reliably, which is what ended the funded programmes. A narrower and more interesting finding is that the training improves people's ability to describe visual scenes in vivid, specific detail. Treat the discipline as visualisation practice, where it has real value, rather than as a way of seeing a distant sealed location, where the record does not support it.
