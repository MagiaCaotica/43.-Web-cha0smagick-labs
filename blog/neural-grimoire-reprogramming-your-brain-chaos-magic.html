The Neural Grimoire: What Chaos Magick Can and Cannot Do to a Brain

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 12 min read

The neural grimoire is a genre rather than a single text, and the genre has one structural feature worth naming before anything else. It borrows two vocabularies, one from software and one from neuroscience, and it combines them in a way neither of the source fields licenses. Software gives you source code, version control, and the ability to read a program before you change it. Neuroscience gives you plasticity, which is real, and the ability to strengthen the circuits you use. Put the two together and you get something that sounds obviously true and is not: a readable editable program, sitting somewhere, running, waiting to be rewritten. Nothing in either discipline puts a program anywhere, and nothing in either discipline makes it readable. What follows is an attempt to say what is left after the metaphor is taken apart, because the residue is worth having and the metaphor actively gets in the way of it.

The software half of the metaphor is the part that breaks

A program is a text. That single fact carries most of what people mean when they talk about reprogramming, and it is the part with no counterpart. A program's instructions are legible to somebody, can be printed, diffed, and compared between versions. A programmer debugging a system can name the line.

The brain has none of those properties. There is no access to a running brain that would let you read the current implementation, and the folk neuroplasticity literature is unusually confident about the thing it is least able to justify, which is that old patterns get overwritten by new ones. They usually do not get overwritten. They get outcompeted under a particular condition, which is a different mechanism with a different implication, and the difference matters for anything you would want to do deliberately.

The outcompeting condition is roughly this: a circuit that fires on a given cue grows when the cue is followed by the same response, and shrinks when the cue is followed by a different one, and a circuit that has shrunk will re-strengthen readily. This is a learning rule, not an editing rule. What it means practically is that changing a habit is not overwriting a file, it is arranging for one response to be reliably followed by another, enough times, in the situations where the old one used to fire. The old one remains available. People who have changed habits and then gone on holiday sometimes find it waiting for them, and that is not a failure of their technique, it is the design.

So the honest version of reprogramming is not editing. It is scheduling a replacement in the specific contexts where the current behaviour runs, and accepting that it will run again when the schedule does not.

The neuroscience half is narrower than the genre suggests

Plasticity is real and it is specific. Repeated use strengthens a circuit, disuse weakens it, and the effect is most pronounced in the first weeks and plateaus after. That is the whole useful content, and it is more than the genre usually admits, because a mechanism that strengthens with repetition and weakens with disuse is exactly the mechanism a sigil practice needs.

Where the genre overreaches is in the direction of precision. The claim that you can target a specific belief and write to it implies an address, and there is no address. Repeating a phrase to yourself does not write to the part of you that holds the belief about your own competence. It uses whatever circuitry happens to be active while you say it, which may be the circuitry involved in the situation you are thinking about, and which is not the same thing.

The practical version of this is that a sigil practice works better when it is tied to a specific situation than when it is tied to a general aspiration. Not because a situation is a more meaningful target, but because a specific situation has specific cues, and cues are what the learning rule responds to. A daily sigil for discipline has nothing to attach to. A sigil used in the ten minutes before you open your inbox has something to attach to, and that is a difference in the mechanism rather than in the symbolism.

What a sigil actually does to a nervous system

Three things, and none of them require the intervention of anything external.

The first is a compression. A sigil takes a statement you have drafted and reduces it to a mark, and the reduction is the operation. You spent time deciding what the statement should say, worded so that it specified a number, a date, and an action, and the sigil is the residue of that deciding. Whatever the mark does afterwards, the deciding already happened, and the deciding is where the specification got sharper.

The second is a retrieval cue. A mark you can recognise instantly is a better trigger than a phrase, because it occupies less and survives longer. This is the part that survives contact with the learning rule above, and it is genuinely useful: a cue that is cheap to detect and hard to reconstruct does the job better than a cue you have to consciously recall.

The third is a commitment with a date on it. Writing a review date and an acceptable conclusion before the release is a piece of apparatus, not a sigil, and it is the piece that most reliably changes behaviour, because a pre-committed decision rule is the one thing in the whole apparatus that constrains the interpretation afterwards. The sigil is the memorable part. The dated decision rule is the effective part, and the two get swapped in conversation far more often than they should.

The genre's best idea, stated without the metaphor

Strip the vocabulary and there is one claim in this material worth keeping, and it is a good one. It is that a symbol can be a tool for holding a position, in the sense that it can let you act as though a thing were already settled in order to bring about its being settled.

That is a real technique and it does not require a brain metaphor. It is closer to how a courtroom works than to how a computer works: a thing treated as already decided behaves differently, and the behaviour changes what others do, and the changed situation is then the actual evidence. A sigil used before a difficult conversation is a device for being the kind of person who has already decided, and being that person is a performance that can change the outcome, and the changed outcome is not a mystery.

The performance does not have to be believed, and this is the useful part of the deflationary version. You do not have to believe the sigil is charged or the position is real. You only have to behave as though it were, long enough for the behaviour to be noticed. The technique is that the acting is easier to do than the deciding, so the acting can be used as a way of getting to the deciding.

What does not survive is the claim that this reaches further than the room. Once a sigil practice is said to alter brain tissue, it becomes unfalsifiable, because the measurable version of it is a change in behaviour in specific situations, and the measurable version is a much smaller claim than people are making when they use the word neural.

A four-week structure that is not a reprogramming schedule

For four weeks, one sigil, drawn once and released once, and then the work is entirely in the using. This is deliberately smaller than the genre's protocols and it is the version that produces something you can look at afterwards.

Week one is specification. Write the statement in your own words, the observable a stranger could check, the action you will take regardless, the review date, the acceptable conclusion, and what you are not asking for. This is roughly twenty minutes and it is the part everything else depends on. If you are doing it in less than fifteen minutes you are not being specific.

Week two is attachment. Use the sigil in one specific recurring situation, ideally something that happens at least five times, and note the time you used it and what you were about to do next. The point is not the sigil. The point is that a fixed situation plus a fixed cue plus a recorded action is the only arrangement that the learning rule has anything to work with.

Week three is continuation. Same situation, same cue, same action, and one addition: a second use in a second situation that is similar but not identical, so you can see whether the effect travelled or was situational. Most of the time it will be situational, and that is the useful result.

Week four is the count. How many times the situation arose. How many times you used the sigil. How many times you did the thing. And, written before you go looking for an explanation, one sentence on what you think happened. The sentence is the only part of the four weeks that teaches anything transferable, and the reason to write it before explaining is that a reason written afterwards is a reason that fits whatever you found.

Four things the genre gets right by accident

It is right that habits change in roughly thirty days, and it is right that thirty days is a boundary of convenience rather than a mechanism. The first two weeks carry most of the change and the rest is consolidation. Expecting a linear thirty-day curve is a reasonable error that produces bad calendar planning.

It is right that you cannot will a belief into place directly. The neural framing is wrong but the observation underneath it is correct, and the reason is the outcompeting rule: a belief that has not been contradicted by behaviour in a particular situation will not change because it was asserted. What changes it is a series of situations in which the old belief was not the useful one.

It is right that the practice should be small enough to do on a bad day. This is a real constraint, not a virtue, and the reason is mechanical: a practice that requires a good state cannot be performed in exactly the states where the old pattern is most likely to run.

It is right that other people can be part of the mechanism. The material about a sigil working through the people around you is usually stated as though this were spooky, and it is much more ordinary than that. If you behave differently, people respond differently, and that is not a channel, it is the most reliable part of the whole thing.

Five ways the reprogramming framing goes wrong

Reading a metaphor as a mechanism. Software metaphors imply inspection, and nothing here is inspectable. A practitioner waiting to read the code before changing the code is waiting indefinitely, and this is the single most common reason the genre stalls.

Targeting a general belief instead of a specific situation. A belief about your own competence has no cues, so the practice has nothing to attach to, and a general aspiration is not something the learning rule can strengthen. A specific recurring situation is.

Measuring on the wrong scale. Behaviour change in specific situations is the only measurable thing here, and it will look small and inconsistent. People expecting a general state change get a real result and do not recognise it as one, which is a measurement failure rather than a practice failure.

Upgrading the apparatus when the result stalls. The apparatus is the comfortable part and it produces the feeling of progress without producing evidence, and in this genre the temptation is stronger than usual because the metaphor is explicitly technological, so a new tool reads as an upgrade rather than as a change of subject.

Reading the absence of a result as a defect in the practice. It is usually a defect in the specification, and the way to tell the two apart is to check whether the observable was something a stranger could have checked. If it was not, the problem was the statement and not the working.

What is left, and why it is enough

What is left after the metaphor is removed is a small toolkit of about four operations. Compress a decision you have already made into a mark that is cheap to recognise. Attach the mark to one specific recurring situation. Pre-commit a date and an acceptable conclusion before you release it. Record what happened afterwards, in one sentence, before explaining it.

That is a practice. It is unglamorous, it produces modest and consistent results in the situations it was aimed at and no results outside them, and it has the enormous advantage of being checkable by a stranger reading your notes, which the neural version is not.

The thing the metaphor was reaching for is real and worth wanting. There is a version of the practice where the work seems to happen in a place you do not have to attend to, where the deciding is done once and then left running, and people do report things like that. The genre is not wrong to want it. It is wrong to claim that wanting it is evidence, because the only observable version of the claim is behavioural, and the behavioural version is available to you without any of the neural framing at all.

So keep the four operations, drop the vocabulary, and let the results be what they are. The people who get the most out of this material are the ones who never believed the interesting version was true, and who therefore did the boring part carefully, and who found the boring part sufficient.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can sigils reprogram my brain?

No, and the honest version is more useful than the claim. A sigil does three things that have nothing to do with rewriting anything: it compresses a decision you have already drafted into a mark that is cheap to recognise, it gives you a retrieval cue, and it marks an interval so that a release is unambiguous. The third of those, combined with a pre-written review date and acceptable conclusion, is what actually changes behaviour. Brains do not have source code, nothing here is inspectable, and the measurable outcome of a sigil practice is a change in what you do in specific situations, which is a much smaller claim than reprogramming and is available without any of the neural framing.

Is neuroplasticity real?

Yes, and it is narrower than the occult literature usually implies. Repeated use strengthens a circuit, disuse weakens it, and the effect is strongest in the first weeks before plateauing. What plasticity does not do is overwrite. A circuit that stops being used becomes weak and readily available again, which is why a habit that seemed gone can return intact after a break. The practical implication is that changing a behaviour is not editing a file, it is arranging for one response to be reliably followed by another in the situations where the old one used to run, and the old one stays available.

How long does it take for a sigil to change your habits?

The first two weeks carry most of the change and the rest is consolidation, so a thirty-day figure is a boundary of convenience rather than a mechanism. Expecting a linear curve across the month leads to bad calendar planning, because the visible effect arrives early and then the practice has to be continued for a fortnight before anything further seems to happen. A more useful measure is per-situation: attach the sigil to one specific recurring situation that happens at least five times a week, and count whether the action followed. That number is small, it moves, and it is honest.

Do I need to believe the sigil is charged for it to work?

No, and the version that works least well is the one that requires belief. The technique that survives contact with a deflationary account is behaving as though a thing were already settled, for long enough for the behaviour to be noticed and for other people to respond to it. That is a performance, and performances do not require conviction, they require repetition in the situation where the performance would go. Belief matters upstream of the practice because it changes what you attempt, not because it supplies anything the practice is missing.

Why does my sigil practice feel like it worked at first and then stopped?

Two likely reasons, and both are worth checking before buying anything. The first is that the effect was situational and you moved out of the situation, which is the expected behaviour of a learning rule rather than a failure of it. The second is that the apparatus became the practice: a daily sigil for a general aspiration has no specific cues for the learning rule to attach to, so there was never much for it to work on, and what you felt early on was the novelty of the new routine rather than a change in behaviour. The fix in both cases is the same, which is to pick one recurring situation, one cue, one recorded action, and a date.
