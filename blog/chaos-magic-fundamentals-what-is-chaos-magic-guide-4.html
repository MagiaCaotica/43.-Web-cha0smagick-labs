Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 6 min read

This is the report nobody writes: a year of chaos magic practice kept properly, with the wins in the first paragraph and the failures occupying considerably more space. The intention is not to discourage anybody. It is to show what the practice actually looks like from inside a schedule that has to keep running alongside a job, some bad weeks, and a stretch of five weeks when nothing was written down at all. The three conditions for the year were fixed in week one and never revised, and there is a verdict at the end which is much less dramatic than the ones usually published. If you are deciding whether the method is worth the trouble, this is more use to you than any description of it, including the good descriptions.

The rules I set at the start, and why I set them

Three conditions, written in week one and never revised. One sigil per fortnight. A deadline of six weeks from the first working. A log entry on the day, including the ones where nothing happened.

The rules exist so that the question at the end has an answer. Without them, the honest answer is that I cannot tell, and a year of practice produces exactly the same amount of usable information as a fortnight of it. Deciding in advance is the only part of the whole enterprise that is genuinely difficult.

The first run, and the mistake inside it

The first four weeks I used one sigil repeatedly, which is the recommended practice and also the trap. By week five the shape had become decorative. I would glance at it, feel nothing, and the fortnightly charge became a small ceremony attached to no particular problem.

The fix was to change the statement every second run, even slightly, and to write the change down. Two related intents do not become one. This is the single most useful thing I got wrong and it took four cycles to notice, which is a reasonable estimate for the kind of mistake that feels fine.

What worked, ranked by how much it helped

Not the workings. The record. The practice of writing an entry on the day, including the failures, changed how I approached the next one more than any ritual technique ever did.

Second on the list, and unexpectedly: reducing the intent to a single imperative sentence before anything else. Roughly half the sigils I made in the first month encoded a wish rather than a statement, and the ones that encoded a wish produced results I could not evaluate, because I had not said what a result would be.

  1. Writing the log entry on the day, which changed the next run more than any technique
  2. Reducing the intent to one checkable imperative sentence before making the glyph
  3. Timing the charge with a kitchen timer, purely to give it a stopping point
  4. Deleting the file afterwards, which stopped the re-opening habit within a week

The five-week gap

Around week nine a work deadline consumed the practice entirely. When it ended I had two choices, and the choice I made is the one most people make, which is to conclude the streak has broken and start again properly on Monday.

I re-registered the same condition and continued, with the gap written into the log as a gap. Nothing was lost except the illusion of a streak, which had never been the point. Writing the gap down took thirty seconds and turned a failure into a data point, and the difference in how the following six weeks went was larger than any difference between ritual styles I have tried.

The results, stated at the level of confidence they deserve

Two of the twenty-six conditions were met in a way I would call attributable, and in both cases the mechanism was that I had scheduled something I would otherwise have delayed indefinitely. Neither was mysterious and neither was about the sigil.

Four conditions were met in a way that would probably have happened anyway. Six produced a partial result, which in this context means the condition was worded loosely enough to be satisfied by something adjacent. The remaining fourteen were null, and the majority of those had an obvious ordinary cause, usually that the condition was never checked.

What the nulls had in common

The nulls cluster around a single pattern. In most of them the condition was phrased in a way that could not actually be checked, which meant I noticed a result or its absence weeks later while reconstructing what I had been hoping for.

The four conditions I had phrased most precisely are the four that produced results I could write down cleanly. That correlation is not evidence for the sigil. It is fairly strong evidence for writing conditions somebody else could check, and it is the most transferable thing in this report.

The practice that almost ended the year

One sigil concerned getting a specific person to change their mind about something, and I ran it for three cycles before noticing that the practice had started organising my entire relationship with that person around a pending outcome.

I stopped it and wrote the entry honestly, which is the only reason the habit survived. The entry read: this is an intervention in someone else's decisions and I did not want to be the kind of person who does that. That is a claim about ethics, it is checkable by anyone who knows the situation, and it is a much better use of a log than recording whether anything came of it.

What I would tell someone starting now

Do not try to keep a streak. Write the gap into the record instead. Make the condition checkable before you make the sigil, because that step does more for your results than any change to the ritual itself.

Log the same day. Expect a low rate of attributable results and plan your practice around that expectation, because a person expecting dramatic results will read noise as signal, and a person expecting two clear hits in a year will read the same data correctly.

The tools, judged after a year

The three that earned their place were a timer, a note-taking app with a timestamp, and a spreadsheet that plots the entries. All three removed friction from recording, which is the part that has to happen consistently.

The ones that did not were anything offering interpretation, and one that offered scoring. Scoring is the specific failure worth naming, because a score gives the practice a visible number, and once there is a number to improve, the practice starts optimising the number rather than the thing the number stands for.

What I have no idea about

Whether the method works, in the sense the literature uses the word. A year of one person's practice cannot address that and I have stopped trying to make it.

What I can say is narrower and more useful. Writing conditions somebody else could check produced more usable results than any ritual variation. Logging on the day produced better data than logging from memory. And expecting a low hit rate made the whole enterprise considerably less enjoyable and considerably more honest, which is the trade I would make again.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is chaos magic dangerous, and how do I know if I have gone too far?

The risks are behavioural and they have clear early signs. Watch for the practice crowding out sleep, for the narrowing of concerns until symbolic questions start making ordinary decisions, and for isolation from people who find it strange. None of these need supernatural interpretation. The practical check is whether obligations outside the practice still get met on a normal week, and if they have stopped, the practice has become a problem regardless of what you believe about it.

What happens when a working produces nothing at all?

You audit the procedure before you audit the claim. Check four things in order: whether the intent was checkable by somebody else, whether the glyph was legible at the size you viewed it, whether the charge had a stated end, and whether the release was completed. A working that fails any of those was attempted rather than run, and running it again with that fixed is more informative than concluding anything about magic.

How many sigils should I make before I know if this works for me?

Fewer than people think, and spread over longer than feels efficient. Around twenty conditions, phrased so that a stranger could check them, run at a low frequency over several months will tell you more than a hundred made in three weeks. The variable that matters is how precisely the condition was written, not how many you produced. A month of careful work beats a fortnight of anything.

Is a paid tracking tool worth it for a year of practice?

Eventually, yes, and specifically for one feature: an export you own. A year of entries is a genuinely valuable artefact and it should not be held somewhere that can raise a price, delete an account or change its format. Beyond that, a free spreadsheet does everything a paid journal does. The tools I would pay for are about durability and search, not about ritual content, and the content has been free since 1976.

Should I keep going when a streak breaks?

Write the gap into the record and continue. Abandoning and restarting costs about forty seconds of re-establishment, which feels substantial at the time and is the reason people abandon repeatedly over several years. A logged gap is a data point about your schedule. An abandoned run is nothing at all, and the difference in what you learn from the following six weeks is much larger than it feels in the moment.
