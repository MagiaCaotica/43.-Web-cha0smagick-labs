History of the Occult

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

Occult history is usually told as a procession of revelations, and the trouble with that shape is that it hides the transport. What actually happened is a chain of translation, printing, borrowing and synthesis, and every link in it is checkable. This guide walks that chain in the order the material arrived, from a Latin verb in the sixteenth century to a British order founded in 1888 whose initiatory material shaped most of the ceremonial practice in use now. Along the way it marks where a link is solid, where it is contested, and where a modern claim has been retrofitted onto older text. The point is not to debunk anything. It is to give you a route through the sources that tells you which parts of a claim are old, which are nineteenth century, and which arrived last Tuesday.

Begin with the word, which arrives in 1560

Occult entered English from the Latin occultare, meaning to hide, and the same root gives the phrase occult science. The word originally described concealment rather than mystery, which is a useful correction to the modern sense of something forbidden. A hidden art and a lost art are different claims, and only one of them is what the sixteenth-century usage implies.

Keeping this etymology in view does the work of an early filter. Any text claiming that an unbroken initiatory tradition has guarded a secret art since antiquity is making a claim about concealment that the word itself contradicts, since a concealment that produced printed grimoires was never much of a concealment.

The grimoire layer, arriving from three directions

The Ars Notoria, the Ars Almadel and the Lemuria sit among the earliest grimoires, compiled in medieval and early-modern Western Europe from Arabic, Jewish and Latin sources. Three traditions fed in, none of which is the tradition that later claimed them, and the compilation step is where a great deal of invention happened.

So when a modern text presents one of these as the pure teaching of a single school, the claim is anachronistic by several centuries and several hundred miles. The grimoire is a portmanteau, and reading it as a coherent document from one tradition is a category error rather than a matter of taste.

  • Arabic astronomical and astrological tables entered through Iberian and Sicilian translation.
  • Jewish demonological and naming material was mined and reshaped by Christian compilers.
  • Latin ritual manuals were adapted, expanded and given coherent structure in print.

The Hermetic Corpus and the fifteenth century

The Hermetic Corpus was brought into Latin in the fifteenth century and then read alongside Judaic and Christian material by people who did not read the Greek. What emerged from that overlap is the Renaissance magician as we understand the figure, and the interesting historical claim is not that these texts are ancient wisdom but that medieval European readers found them worth translating at all.

Later occultists inherited the results and dropped the conditions, which is how a set of late-antique philosophical dialogues and fragments became a foundation for systems of practice invented six hundred years afterwards. The borrowing is real. The continuity it is used to prove is not.

The 1888 order and the material that outlived it

The Golden Dawn, founded in 1888, was the most significant British occult order of the nineteenth century, and its initiatory documents shaped most of the ceremonial practice in circulation since. Almost every temple-derived element you meet in a modern tradition, including the grade structure and much of the elemental vocabulary, arrives through that nineteenth-century body of material rather than from the sources it claims.

This is not an accusation of forgery. It is an observation about transmission. A coherent system built in 1888 is still worth studying on its own terms, and it is considerably more interesting once you stop treating it as a recovered tradition and start treating it as a very accomplished nineteenth-century design.

The twentieth century, where most modern practice comes from

Crowley, Austin Osman Spare with his reduction of statements to glyphs, the reconstruction of a nature religion in the 1950s by occultists including Gerard Gardner, and the chaos magicians of the 1970s are all twentieth-century events. That list is not a slur on any of them. It is a description of where the practice that most people actually perform was assembled.

Gardner's reconstruction is the case that most needs the distinction, because a craft presented as revival is easier to practise honestly when the reconstruction is acknowledged than when it is defended. Its own history remains under active scholarly dispute, and the dispute is about exactly this.

How to read the chain without inheriting its claims

Read each text as a document with a date, an audience and a purpose, and ask what it was trying to accomplish for the person reading it at the time. A compilation made to be used by a practitioner in a hurry is not a historical source, and it was not written to be one.

Then separate three things that get collapsed together: what the text claims, what the text was written to support, and what actually happened to the people who acted on it. Only the third is evidence about the world. The first two are evidence about a time and a place, which is a different and more tractable question.

A source-checking routine you can reuse

Four questions, applied in order. When was the earliest copy printed. What did the compiler have to hand. What was the stated purpose of the manual. Which later author is the source of the claim you are reading. Most confident assertions about ancient continuity collapse once the fourth question is answered, because the author doing the asserting is usually recent.

Run those four on any passage that feels authoritative and most of the feeling evaporates, which is disorienting at first and then useful. What is left is a smaller set of claims with real provenance, and those are the ones worth building a practice on.

  1. Find the earliest printed edition rather than the earliest claimed origin.
  2. Identify the languages and traditions the compiler was working from.
  3. Note the stated purpose: teaching, apologetics, entertainment or fundraising.
  4. Trace the claim to the modern author who most recently asserted it.

What survives the chain, and what does not

The claims that survive contact with the transmission chain are the practical ones. Ritual attention changes physiological state reliably enough that it has been used in clinical contexts. Structured divination formats a decision by forcing it into language. Symbolic systems give memory somewhere to attach. None of these require any particular cosmology, and all of them are available to a sceptic.

The claims that do not survive are the ones about unbroken custody of a secret body of knowledge, about lineages stretching back without a break, and about results that have never been tested. These are not unusual beliefs. They are what you would expect a tradition to say about itself, which is precisely why they should be examined with more suspicion rather than less.

The two errors that recur, in both directions

The first error is credulity: treating a nineteenth-century composite as a millennia-old inheritance. The second is the mirror image, which is more common among people who have read a sceptical source and now treat every claim as a hoax. That second error is easy to miss because it feels rigorous.

The middle position is duller and more useful. A reconstructed system can be internally coherent and worth studying. Its practitioners can report experiences that are real to them. Its origin story can still be false. Holding all three of those at once is uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the clearest sign that you are not simply defending a preference.

The printing press as the real turning point

Grimoires are print artefacts, and print changes what a tradition can be. A manuscript can be unique, initiatory and controlled by one teacher. A printed book can be copied cheaply, bought by people outside any lineage, and read without permission, which is precisely the situation in which a body of practice becomes a body of claims.

So the period that matters most is the one where printing, literacy and cheap paper combined to put ritual manuals into the hands of readers who had no way to check them. Almost every claim about an unbroken lineage that you will read in a modern text runs into that period and does not survive the contact.

Three claims worth checking against the chain

The first is antiquity: a practice described as continuous since a remote civilis-ation. Check the earliest printed edition. If the practice is a modern synthesis of sources from different traditions, the antiquity claim is decorative rather than descriptive, and nothing else in the text depends on it.

The second is custody: a single unbroken line of teachers. Check whether the supposed lineage has documented names, dates and places. Where those are missing, the chain is being asserted rather than shown, and the difference is worth holding onto because the practice may still be useful.

The third is result: a claim of a specific reliable outcome. This one you can test directly, without any historical argument at all, and it is the only one of the three where running the experiment settles the question for you.

  1. Antiquity: find the first printed edition, not the first claimed origin.
  2. Custody: look for names, dates and places, and treat absence as an unanswered question.
  3. Result: this is the only claim you can settle by running the test yourself.

A reading order for someone starting now

Start with transmission, not with practice. A year of reading about methods before understanding where the methods came from produces a practitioner who can repeat instructions and cannot evaluate a claim, which is the least useful position there is.

Then read one primary source closely, in a modern scholarly edition rather than a reprint, and note where the text is internally inconsistent. The inconsistencies are more informative than the claims, because they show you the shape of the compilation.

Only then read the twentieth-century syntheses, and read them as creative work by identifiable people. They are the most influential material you will encounter and the least in need of defending, and treating them that way frees up the attention you would otherwise spend on their genealogies.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which grimoires actually matter?

The Ars Notoria, the Ars Almadel and the Lemuria matter as early witnesses to the European compilation process, and the Ars Goetia matters as the seventeenth-century printed source for the seventy-two spirits. Each is more informative about the compilers and their era than about a preserved tradition, which is not a criticism of the sources so much as a description of what kind of documents they are.

Is occult history mostly fraud?

Mostly it is a record of borrowing, adaptation and enthusiastic synthesis, which is not the same as fraud. Where claims of ancient continuity cannot survive a date check, that is a problem for the claim and not for the study. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries produced a great deal of genuinely coherent work that does not need a false pedigree.

What is a grimoire, in plain terms?

A practical manual assembled for use rather than for reading, usually organised as timed sequences with instructions rather than as argument. Because they were written to be used quickly, they tend to be internally inconsistent and full of borrowed material, which is exactly what makes them good evidence for how a period actually practised.

If so much was reconstructed, why study it at all?

Because reconstruction is not a disqualification. The Golden Dawn and the 1950s nature religions are internally coherent systems built by identifiable people with stated aims, and you can evaluate them on that basis. What does not survive scrutiny is the claim that they deliver something inherited intact from a lost civilisation, and dropping that claim makes the rest easier to study, not harder.

Is a paid history course worth taking over free reading?

The primary material and the scholarly critiques are freely available, and the transmission chain above can be checked with a library and an hour. A paid course is worth it when you want the chronology organised for you or want to be corrected while you work, since the value is in the structure and the correction rather than in access to anything secret.
