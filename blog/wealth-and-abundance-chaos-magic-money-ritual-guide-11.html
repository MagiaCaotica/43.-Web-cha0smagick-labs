Wealth and Abundance

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Money is the most popular target in modern Western occult practice and the most consistently misread one. The cause is not secrecy. It is that wealth is a strongly symbolic quantity, universal, quantifiable, and legible to anyone who is watching, which makes it easy to confuse an emotional stake with a magical claim. The arrangement below fits on one page. It takes an hour, produces a single glyph, and then asks you to do the least interesting part, which is to leave the paper alone and write down what you expect before you begin. Everything here is arranged so that an unfriendly reader could check the result. If the working fails, the record will show that it failed, and that asymmetry is the entire design.

The Sigil Is a Contract With Your Future Self

A sigil is not a wish. It is a reduction of an imperative statement to a glyph, and the imperative form matters more than most people realise. I will have more money is passive and unfalsifiable. I will send the invoice that has been overdue for forty days by Friday is neither. The method was formalised by Austin Osman Spare in the early 1900s: take the sentence you would actually say out loud, remove the vowels, then fold the remaining letters until nothing recognisable survives. What remains is a mark that only you can expand back into the sentence. The mark is memory technology, and its entire function is to stay legible to one person at one moment, months later, in a distracted state.

That is the whole mechanism, and it is worth stating plainly because the alternative invites bad engineering. A glyph does not hold power the way a stone holds heat. It holds a compressed instruction, in roughly the way a bookmark holds a place in a book you will not reopen for a year. When you charge it you are not persuading the universe of anything. You are making the instruction harder to lose.

Why 741 Appears and What It Does Not Claim

You will meet 741 in abundance material more often than any other number. The arithmetic is easy to state: seven plus four plus one equals twelve, and twelve recurs across temperament tables, zodiac divisions, and numerological columns that people have cross-referenced for centuries. Some practitioners add a second layer, reading the individual digits as a symbolic vocabulary, with seven standing for the classical planets and four for the elements. None of this is physics, and the literature that presents it as physics deserves suspicion.

The defensible position is that numerology here operates as a selection device rather than a mechanism. Printing 741 gives the page a focus, which is functionally useful, because an unnumbered intention tends to dissolve in transit. If you would rather use another number, the choice costs you nothing at all. What would cost you something is claiming that the digits vibrate at a frequency which pulls currency into a bank account. That claim has no support, and stating it makes the practice harder to test, because you now have to believe something false before you are allowed to begin.

The Target Has to Survive a Skeptic

Write the target so that a person who dislikes you could read it and say whether it happened. This sounds severe, and it is the whole test. A target that specifies a number, a date, and a verifiable event can fail. A target phrased as an improvement in wellbeing or a shift in circumstances cannot fail, because no observation you could make would count as disconfirmation. When a target is unfalsifiable the practice becomes unfalsifiable with it, and an unfalsifiable practice is a mood rather than a method.

The practical test is grammatical. Ask whether the sentence has a subject, a verb, and a completion condition. Income reaches a figure by a date. The invoice is sent. The account is opened. The second interview is booked. If you cannot put a date on the completion then the target is a direction and not a destination, and sigilising it will produce a satisfying sense of having done something without producing the thing.

One Statement, One Glyph, One Window

A money sigil is not a spell to be repeated. It is a single deployment with a start date, an end date, and a standing prohibition on refreshment. Refreshment is the commonest way abundance practice fails, and it fails quietly, because a refreshed sigil still feels like effort. The moment you redraw, the compression restarts, and the instruction you have been carrying since the first charge is quietly replaced by one you have not yet rehearsed.

Take a window of sixty days as a default. Write the start and the end on the same sheet as the glyph. When the window closes you make one of three decisions: the target was met, the target was not met, or the target was badly specified and needs rewriting before any further charge. There is no fourth option in which you simply draw it again and keep the earlier sheets in a drawer.

The Sixty-Minute Charge, Minute by Minute

  1. Ten minutes on the statement. Write the imperative sentence in plain language and read it aloud once. If you would not say it out loud to another person, it is not finished.
  2. Ten minutes on the glyph. Drop the vowels, fold the consonants, and redraw until the shape no longer suggests words. Colour is optional and carries no meaning of its own.
  3. Ten minutes on the number. Print 741 or your own choice, small, in a corner. Its job is to occupy the space where a date would sit if the sheet were wider.
  4. Fifteen minutes of charge. Trance by whatever route already works for you, holding the statement rather than the drawing. The state matters more than the technique, and a bored practitioner charging a clean sigil beats an ecstatic one charging a vague one.
  5. Fifteen minutes of sealing. Read the statement, glance at the glyph, and close the sheet. Do not re-read the statement after this, and do not add anything you did not write at the start.

A Pre-Registered Prediction, Written and Sealed

Before the charge, write a second sheet. It carries three things: the date you expect the first movement, the date you expect completion, and the specific observation that would count as failure. Seal it, or put it somewhere you will not look before the window closes. The value of the exercise lies entirely in the sealing. A prediction written and then reread every day is a prompt for motivated reasoning, because each day supplies a fresh chance to reinterpret the latest news in the direction you already wanted.

The most useful line on that sheet is the disconfirming one. What would I see if this were not working? Write the answer while you are still in a state of doubt rather than a week after hope set in. Later, when something ambiguous happens, the pre-written answer will tell you whether to count it, and you will not be constructing the count in the moment.

The Refresh Problem

The costliest habit in this material is the urge to redraw. It feels productive and it destroys the mechanism, because the compressed statement was the only part that was ever doing work. Every refresh returns you to the start and costs you the recognition you had accumulated. The sheet from the first charge has been handled, glanced at, and slept beside for sixty days. A fresh one has none of that history.

If the window closes with nothing, the correct response is a new target and a new statement rather than a new drawing of the old one. If the window closes with partial movement, write the next target in terms of what actually moved instead of what you originally wanted. Partial movement is information about which half of the sentence was actionable and which half was decoration.

Reading the Result Without Narrating It

When the window closes, read the sealed sheet first, then the outcome, and score them in that order. Reading the outcome first means you will fit the prediction to whatever happened, which is the exact mechanism this arrangement was built to prevent. Read the prediction, note the dates against it, and only then go and look at what actually occurred.

Report the result in a form somebody else could check. The money arrived. The money did not arrive. A partial amount arrived inside the window. Those are the available categories. If you find yourself writing that the atmosphere felt different, or that something shifted in the surroundings, you have stepped outside the measurement, and the honest move at that point is to log the attempt as inconclusive rather than as a partial success.

Where the Practice Actually Breaks

  • Target too broad. If no observation could count as failure then nothing can count as success either.
  • Two statements on one sheet. Folding them into a single glyph guarantees that neither will be legible when you need it.
  • Refresh at day twenty. The commonest break, and the one that feels most like diligence.
  • Confirmation-only logging. Recording hits while skipping near-misses manufactures a track record that will not survive a friend reading the diary.
  • Attaching your worth to the outcome. Treated in its own section below, and the most expensive of the five.

What to Do When Nothing Arrives

A null is a result, and it is the one most likely to be discarded. When the window closes with nothing, the useful move is a single paragraph on what was different between your circumstances and the circumstances the target quietly assumed. Money targets fail for ordinary reasons. The amount depended on a decision that belonged to somebody else. The deadline fell in a bad month. The skill the target required was not the skill you currently have.

Then choose between two responses. Specify the target more narrowly and run a second window, or set the practice aside for a season and work on the underlying material instead. The second option is underrated. A sigil aimed at a gap in your income is a way of asking a question you would rather not put directly, and the glyph will not close the gap for you.

The Ethics of Attaching Value to a Glyph

The risk here is psychological rather than supernatural, and it is worth naming precisely. Money work ties your sense of self-worth to an outcome that is mostly outside your control, and a practice that supplies daily evidence about that outcome can make the attachment worse rather than better. People who run these protocols badly end up anxious, checking statements compulsively, and making financial decisions out of panic.

The safeguard is unglamorous. Keep the practice off days you cannot afford to be disappointed on. Do not charge a sigil while unemployed and holding rent money. Do not read an outcome into every bank notification that arrives. If the working has begun to make money more frightening rather than more legible, then the plain reading is that it is doing harm, and the correct action is to stop.

A Card Worth Carrying

Write the following on an index card and keep it wherever you work. The target as one imperative sentence, carrying a number and a date. The window, with its start and its end. The pre-registered first-movement date and completion date. The sealed disconfirming observation. And three rules in a row: one glyph, no refreshment, outcome recorded before interpretation.

Everything else in abundance practice is decoration around that card. Tools that help you write the sentence. Correspondences that help you choose a colour. Timing tables that help you feel tidy on a particular evening. None of them are wrong, and not one of them is load-bearing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long before a money sigil shows anything at all?

Treat the first sixty days as the working window and the first fortnight as too early to read. Anything that resembles a result in week one is usually the ordinary consequence of having written the target down, which is a real effect and not the one you were testing. If the window closes without movement, that is a null and it belongs in the record rather than in a drawer.

Does the number printed on the sheet change anything?

No measurable amount. The digits give the page a focus and that is the whole of the job, so a number chosen for its arithmetic and a number chosen for its sound are equally serviceable. What damages the practice is promoting the digits into a force that draws money inward, because that turns a piece of paper into an object of belief, and belief you cannot check is belief you will eventually be caught holding badly.

What if I need to redraw the glyph halfway through?

Do not. Redrawing restarts the compression and swaps a statement you have been carrying for a fresh one you have not rehearsed, which is why the urge feels productive while making the outcome rather less likely. If the glyph has genuinely stopped being legible to you, write the statement again from the original sentence, keep the same window, and note the substitution in the record so the sequence stays honest.

Is it a problem to have charged several money sigils over the years?

Not in itself, since most of them were aimed at overlapping targets and the earlier ones quietly lost their meaning. The trouble shows up when the practice becomes a habit that produces a feeling without producing a record, because then you have no way of telling repetition apart from effectiveness. Keeping every statement and every window on the same sheet of paper makes the question answerable at no cost.

Should I tell anybody what I am doing?

Only to people who will treat it as a hobby rather than as a cry for help, which in practice shortens the list considerably. There is no traditional requirement to disclose and a sigil carries no obligation you took on by drawing it. The one case worth considering is money work inside a partnership or a shared household, because the target may touch accounts the other person has a legitimate interest in.
