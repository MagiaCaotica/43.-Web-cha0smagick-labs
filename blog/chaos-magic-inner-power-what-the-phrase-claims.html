Inner Power in Chaos Magick: What the Phrase Actually Claims

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

You will hear the phrase inner power in almost every piece of chaos magick marketing, and the people using it are rarely talking about the same thing. Across the material out there the phrase carries at least four distinct claims, and they differ enormously in what evidence would support them. One is a claim about attention, one is a claim about knowledge, one is a claim about capacity, and one is a claim that cannot be checked at all. This page is a reference: it names each version, says what would count as evidence, and gives you the question that separates them. You will also notice that the popular version of the phrase is the one with the least support behind it, and that the best-supported version is the least exciting one to sell.

Why one phrase ended up meaning four different things

Inner power has no technical definition in the primary sources, and that absence is the whole problem. The chaos magick literature is remarkably disciplined about terms that do work: sigil, servitor, egregore, gnosis, paradigm shift. When a text uses a word it usually means one thing by it. Inner power is not a term from Carroll or Spare. It is a phrase from the popular layer, the part of the tradition that arrives through video thumbnails and course sales rather than through books.

The consequence is that the phrase has no owner. Four different groups use it, each attached to a different underlying theory, and because none of them is wrong enough to be shut down, all four circulate. What follows is a taxonomy, not a debunking. Each version gets the same treatment: what it claims, what would support it, what would undermine it, and who benefits.

Version one: inner power as sustained attention

This is the version with the best evidence, and it is the least mystical. The claim is that people who can hold a single narrow object of attention for a sustained period gain access to information they would otherwise miss, and that the capacity is trainable. That is a claim about cognition, it is broadly supported by what is known about sustained attention and about the cost of task-switching, and it does not require any belief about the nature of reality.

What would support it: measurable improvement in the accuracy or speed of judgements that require sustained focus, with training. What would undermine it: no change after months of deliberate practice, or improvement only on tasks that were already easy to focus on. The training is unglamorous and it works. Deliberate practice on a narrow discriminations task, scored honestly, is the entire method. If your inner power is this version, the fastest route to improving it is a scored test where you cannot see the answers in advance.

Who benefits from this version: teachers who sell attention training, which is a real product with real results. They are not usually lying, and they are usually overselling.

The risk specific to this version is misattribution. Everything that requires concentration looks like proof of inner power, including things that have nothing to do with the occult. If you improve at a task after practicing magic, the likeliest cause is that you practiced the task.

Version two: inner power as knowledge you have not organised yet

This is the second version and it is the one chaos magick actually teaches. The claim is that a person knows more than they can retrieve on demand, and that certain procedures can move the unorganised part into retrievable form. The classic example is a practitioner who knows a great deal and can recall almost none of it in conversation, then does a month of written practice and can suddenly discuss it.

What would support it: measurable increases in recall or in the number of usable connections made under time pressure, in a specific domain, after a specific practice. What would undermine it: improvement that does not survive a delay, or improvement only on material the practitioner already valued. The honest reading is that this is a well-known effect of writing and spaced retrieval that does not require a supernatural explanation, and that the practices which produce it are the same practices that produce it in any other field.

The reason this version matters for a chaos magician is not that it is supernatural. It is that the belief that this is possible changes what you are willing to attempt. A person who expects to know more than they currently know will attempt harder problems. Whatever produced it, the effect itself is real and worth having.

Who benefits: course authors selling memory systems. Mostly fine, sometimes marketing the phenomenon as proof of something stronger than it is.

Version three: inner power as capacity to be wrong

This is the least popular version and the most useful. The claim is that the most valuable thing a practitioner can develop is not accuracy, confidence, or intensity, but a reliable capacity to discover that they were wrong. It is not power over anything. It is power over your own error rate.

What would support it: a practitioner who can state in advance what would change their mind, who writes down predictions before working, and who revises belief when the record says they should. What would undermine it: a practice where nobody ever records a failure, where a disappointing result is always explained by timing, and where the practitioner's position on a disputed question has not changed in five years.

This version is measurable in a way the others are not. Count the predictions written in advance, the outcomes recorded at a fixed review date, and the number of beliefs revised because of them. A practitioner with a high revision rate is not a failed practitioner. They are the only kind who is learning.

Who benefits: nobody sells this. There is no product that delivers a capacity to be wrong. That absence is itself evidence about how the other three versions got popular.

Version four: inner power as an unfalsifiable claim

This is the version you are most often sold. It states that a practitioner has accessed a power or a state that is unavailable to others, and that the access is real and permanent. Its structure is: something happened to me, I cannot show you what, therefore it worked.

There is no observation that would count against this claim, which is not a virtue. A claim immune to disconfirmation carries no information. It cannot be wrong, so it cannot be right. If a belief can explain any outcome, it explains nothing in particular.

What would support it: nothing available from inside the practice. The only available evidence would be third party observation of an outcome that the practitioner did not produce by ordinary means, which is a very high bar and is essentially never met.

Who benefits: everyone selling access to it. The phrase is a reliable indicator of the product rather than the practice, and that is a useful thing to be able to notice early.

The question that separates the four versions

If someone tells you they have unlocked inner power, ask one question: what would have shown you that you had not? A good answer names an observation made in advance, a date, and a conclusion that would have followed from the opposite result. A bad answer describes a feeling, a coincidence, a change in circumstances, or the advice of another person.

This single question does most of the work. It is not hostile. Most people offering inner power are sincere and will pause when asked, because the question is one they have not been asked. A practitioner who has written down a falsifiable prediction and a date has, whether or not they would have used the phrase inner power, done the thing that versions one through three are describing.

The reverse test is also useful. Ask what they would do if the practice had produced nothing at all. A practitioner working with versions one through three has an answer, because their version was never supposed to guarantee results. A practitioner working with version four does not, and the absence of an answer is the information.

What the popular version gets right by accident

The fourth version is unsupportable as a claim about reality and it is not worthless as a piece of instruction. The idea that a person has more capacity than their habits allow is functionally accurate for almost everyone. Most people underuse what they can do. The error is in the mechanism and the certainty, not in the direction of the advice.

There is a version of this that survives: treat your own attention and knowledge as resources you are underusing, train them in the boring ways that train them, and be honest in your bookkeeping. That person will get most of what the promise was selling and will be able to say which part worked.

The version that does not survive is the one where the practitioner concludes that the access is special, permanent, and not available to those who have not paid. That conclusion does no work. It cannot be acted on. It only makes the next purchase easier to justify.

A thirty-day version anyone can run

Take the useful residue of all three defensible versions and you get a specific, boring, thirty-day protocol. It requires no belief and produces a number at the end.

Days one through ten, train version one. Pick one narrow discrimination task you cannot see the answers to. Run a fixed number of trials per day. Write down your score every session including the bad ones. No self-built random source, no shuffling a deck you own, no answers visible during the run.

Days eleven through twenty, train version two. Write one page a day about a subject you already know something about and cannot talk about fluently. At the end of each page, write a question you now know how to answer that you could not have answered on day one. The count of those questions is the measurement, and the exercise is closer to recall practice than to magic.

Days twenty-one through thirty, run version three. Make ten dated predictions about your own work, ordinary and low stakes. Write down what would show each wrong. At the end of the month, count how many resolved, how many were wrong, and how many you revised because of the record. That count is the only honest measure of version three in the entire article.

Five ways people get this wrong

The most common error is treating a version-one improvement as proof of version four. Concentration got better, and now there is a story about the concentration.

The second is using version two to launder version four. Real gains in recall are a genuine effect and they get used to make an unrelated unfalsifiable claim feel grounded.

The third is skipping version three. A practitioner who never writes a prediction in advance cannot distinguish learning from confirmation, and will report a high hit rate for anything they attempt. This is not deception. It is a bookkeeping omission, and it is the most common one.

The fourth is buying the product that resolves the ambiguity. Any course that promises all four versions at once is selling you a package and you will not be able to tell which part you got. This is why the phrase feels empowering and stays unresolvable.

The fifth and worst is the reverse of the usual failure: treating the phrase as nonsense and skipping the three defensible versions along with it. The attention claim is true, the retrieval claim is largely true, the error-correction claim is the most valuable thing in the field, and all three are available to anyone willing to do the unglamorous bookkeeping.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is inner power a real thing in chaos magick?

No single thing. The phrase is a popular umbrella covering at least four separate claims, and they have very different standing. The attention claim and the knowledge-retrieval claim are defensible without any supernatural assumption. The claim about being able to discover you were wrong is the most valuable of the four and is the one nobody sells. The claim that a practitioner has accessed something special and permanent cannot be checked, and carries no information.

How do I tell which version a teacher is selling?

Ask what would have shown them they had not succeeded, with the observation named in advance. Sincere practitioners working with attention, knowledge, or error-correction will have a real answer. Marketing built on the unfalsifiable version will shift to describing a feeling, a coincidence, or a change in circumstances, because those are the only things they can point at. The question is not hostile and most people pause when asked it.

Does belief make magic work?

Belief reliably changes behaviour, which is a mechanism worth having on its own. A person who expects a practice to work will attempt harder, will follow through on the follow-through steps, and will notice openings they would otherwise have dismissed. None of that requires the practice to be supernatural. What belief does not do is remove the need for an honest record, and the practitioners who skip the record are the ones who most loudly claim belief as the explanation.

Can I train inner power at home?

Yes, for the three defensible versions, with a notebook and one uncontrollable source of randomness. A scored test with fixed trial counts trains sustained attention. Daily writing on a subject you know trains retrieval. Dated predictions with pre-written reversal conditions train error-correction. None of it requires initiation, lineage, or an expensive tool, and thirty days of it will produce a number you can look at.

Why is the phrase so popular if it is so vague?

Vagueness is commercially useful. A precise claim can be checked and can fail in public. A vague one cannot be checked, so every practitioner can describe their experience in its terms without contradiction, and no consumer can compare offerings. The four versions got blended because the ambiguity lets material aimed at version one also be sold to people hoping for version four.
