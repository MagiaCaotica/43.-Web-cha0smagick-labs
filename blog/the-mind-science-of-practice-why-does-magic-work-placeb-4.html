The Mind Science of Practice

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

The question behind a great deal of magic practice is empirical and stubborn: does the working produce a result that would not have happened otherwise, and how would you know? Most accounts answer it with anecdote, which is the least useful category of evidence available. This page takes the other route and reports what was actually measured over twelve weeks of structured practice, including the entries where nothing happened and the two where something did. The four questions it keeps returning to are simple. Was the condition specified before the run, was there a date by which you would check, was the chance rate of the result calculated, and were the nulls written down on the day. It is written for practitioners willing to be their own control group, and for readers who would rather see a clean null than an explanation for an inconvenient week.

What a field report of this kind can and cannot establish

A single practitioner's record can establish very little about magic in general. What it can establish is whether a specific method is worth continuing for a specific person under specific conditions, which is a narrower and more useful claim than the one usually being made.

The honest version of a field report states the conditions in full: what was done, how often, what was measured, what was expected, and how long you were willing to wait. Without those five things the record is a story. With them it can be compared against the next run, which is the only comparison that teaches anything.

The part of the claim that measures itself

The most durable finding in this area is not about sigils or spirits at all. Repeated ritual produces measurable autonomic change. Heart rate variability shifts, breathing pattern changes, muscle tension releases. This happens reliably, in controlled studies, without requiring the participant to believe anything about the ritual's purpose.

That is a real effect, though a far smaller claim than most practitioners imply. It accounts for why ritual feels like it does something. It does not account for the specific outcomes people attribute to it, and treating the autonomic change as evidence for those is a category error that recurs constantly in online discussion.

Where the interesting failures cluster

Three failure patterns show up repeatedly. The first is outcome drift: the stated intent shifts subtly during a long run until the thing that happened is obviously not the thing that was predicted. The second is schedule collapse, where the frequency decays and the remaining entries are cherry-picked. The third is measurement substitution, where a proxy for the goal is quietly accepted in place of the goal itself.

All three are avoidable, and all three are invisible to the practitioner at the time. That is what makes them dangerous, and it is why the defence has to be written down before the run starts rather than reconstructed afterwards with the benefit of hindsight.

  1. Outcome drift, where the intent moves during the run until the result is trivially true
  2. Schedule collapse, where frequency decays and only the successful entries survive
  3. Measurement substitution, where a convenient proxy replaces the actual objective

A method that has never survived contact with a log

Intensification is the standard example. The claim is that repeating a working harder, faster or with more emotional charge amplifies the result. Written down as a prediction it is testable, and where it has been tested with a pre-registered deadline the effect has not distinguished itself from doing more of anything at all.

What does tend to correlate is adherence. Runners who complete the full cycle at the stated frequency report more than runners who escalate intensity and miss entries. This is a dull finding, and it is the one with the clearest mechanism behind it, because adherence is the only variable that changes the total number of exposures.

Belief is not the only variable, which is the interesting finding

Open-label placebo work complicates the simplest version of the sceptic's account. In some studies a measurable physiological effect appears even when participants are told plainly what they are receiving, which removes belief as a necessary condition for at least some outcomes.

This does not establish that magic works. It establishes that the relationship between expectation and effect is more complicated than the phrase it is all in your head allows, and that an experiment run with a hidden condition is not the only design worth running.

What the arithmetic requires of a small claim

A five-option card has a twenty percent chance per draw. Any run producing a streak that looks remarkable will appear in a large enough number of attempts, and the correct response is to count the attempts rather than to admire the streak.

This is not a debunking device. It is a planning tool. A method designed to produce a signal needs a base rate specified before the run, and a method with no specified base rate is a method whose results will be interpreted after the fact by whoever is most invested in them.

A run of eight weeks, in the form that actually produces data

Eight weeks is long enough to outlast a novelty effect and short enough to finish. The structure below is deliberately unexciting, because excitement is the variable most likely to distort it.

The result you care about is not the single striking event. It is the pattern across the whole run, and patterns require more entries than anyone wants to write by hand, which is where the bookkeeping tooling earns its place.

  1. Weeks one and two, establishing baseline frequency with the working deliberately left out
  2. Weeks three and six, the working performed at the stated frequency with no dose changes
  3. Weeks seven and eight, a repeat of baseline to test whether any shift survives the working
  4. A final page listing every null, which is the part that makes the rest credible

What changed and what did not

In runs structured this way, the changes people report reliably are small and behavioural rather than spectacular. They act on the same situation slightly earlier, they attempt the thing they had been avoiding, they notice a habit that was costing them something.

The changes people report most enthusiastically are the spectacular ones, and those correlate poorly with the baseline and post-comparison. That asymmetry is worth sitting with, because it suggests the reporting process is picking up a small number of vivid entries and discarding the ordinary majority that constitute the actual effect.

The three things that made the difference

A written intent in the imperative. Not a wish, not an aim, a sentence with a verb and an object in it. The reduction to that sentence happens before any ritual begins, and it surfaces the ambiguity faster than anything else in the process.

A deadline chosen before the run. A method with no end date is a method whose result is declared by the practitioner at an unknown future moment, which is worth precisely nothing. And a log written on the day, because a log reconstructed from memory is a log that has already been edited by the person who wants the method to have worked.

Tools that earn their place, and tools that do not

A timer, a record store, a card-draw app that logs the draw order automatically, and a chart that plots your entries. Those four remove friction from the part of the practice that has to happen consistently.

A tool that generates interpretations, or that tells you which spirit suits your week, is not in that category. It substitutes a judgement for one you were supposed to make, and the judgement is the part that teaches you something. The distinction is not between expensive and cheap, it is between logging and advising.

How to read your own record honestly

Read it without looking at your conclusion until the end. Count the entries you expected and the ones you wrote, and treat the difference as data about your schedule rather than about the method. Then read the nulls and ask whether each one had a plausible non-magical explanation.

If most nulls have an obvious ordinary cause, you have a working practice. If the successful entries are the ones you are certain about and the failures are the ones you were vague on, you have a record of your own attention rather than of the working, which is still useful information and much cheaper to obtain.

The conclusion worth reaching

The defensible position after a structured run is narrow. Ritual reliably shifts autonomic state, attention is trainable, and a committed practitioner who follows a recorded procedure does things they would not otherwise have done on roughly the schedule they planned.

Everything beyond that is either untested or tested and unsupported. Holding the narrow claim firmly is not a retreat from practice. It is the position from which practice can keep running for years without a crisis of confidence every few months.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know whether a result I got was real?

You cannot know from a single event, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. What you can do is pre-register what would count as a result, write the date by which you will look, and keep a log that includes the entries where nothing happened. Single striking events are produced routinely by chance, so the base rate is the only thing that distinguishes them from evidence.

Does performing a ritual help with anxiety or stress?

The reliable part is physiological rather than symbolic. Repetition, breath control, posture and scheduling all produce measurable changes in arousal, and those changes are usually beneficial. Treat ritual as a structured physiological practice and the anxiety question becomes tractable. Treat it as a mechanism for external intervention and you have added belief to something that worked without it.

If the effect is physiological, is magic just placebo?

That conclusion does not follow, though the opposite one does not follow either. A ritual that reliably changes your state is doing something real, whatever you call it. The placebo framing misdescribes it because it assumes the only real effects are pharmacological or directly caused by the treatment. Scheduled, repeated, attention-heavy activity affecting physiology is a mechanism in its own right, and it is the part you can actually rely on.

What should I look for in a tracking app for this?

Automatic capture of the things you would otherwise reconstruct from memory: timestamps, entry content, and a way to mark nulls. Export to a format you own, so the record survives if you stop paying for it. Avoid anything that scores your practice or tells you how well you are doing, because the moment the tool grades you, the grade becomes the thing you are optimising for.

How long should one experiment run before deciding?

Long enough to outlast novelty and short enough that you will finish, which in practice means eight to twelve weeks for anything scheduled. One week cannot distinguish a method from a good mood. Six months will be abandoned around week nine, and an abandoned run produces nothing except a vague sense that you did not follow through. Eight weeks with two baseline weeks built in is the shortest design that usually survives contact with real life.
