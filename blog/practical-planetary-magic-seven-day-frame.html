Practical Planetary Magic: Getting Something Out of the Week

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Almost nobody who starts planetary magic is learning something new. They are learning to name a frame they have been living inside for thirty years. The seven days already mean seven things, and if you have read any astrology you already know which. The work is converting a naming system into a scheduling system, and the honest reason that works is not that Mars is a warrior. It is that a week is the shortest horizon on which almost nothing is genuinely impossible, and having one fixed question per day is a planning device most people have never had.

The seven are not seven arbitrary labels stuck to seven arbitrary days

The day names are planetary names. Sunday belongs to the Sun, Monday to the Moon, Tuesday to Mars, Wednesday to Mercury, Thursday to Jupiter, Friday to Venus, Saturday to Saturn. This is not a modern invention layered on top — the Romance forms are the planet names, which is why they survived the spread of the language that carried the system.

The pairing is not arbitrary either. The seven classical planets are the visible wandering lights, and a week is one seventh of the year, which is roughly how long a given planet takes to move through one sign. The traditional week was not a scheduling convenience. It was a low-resolution planetary cycle, and it is the only piece of timing practice in the whole occult field that is both old and self-consistent.

What each day is actually usable for

Rather than listing dignities, the useful version is what kind of work each day supports. The Sun is for things that need to be visible and sustained: publishing, showing up, a maintenance task that requires you to be seen doing it. The Moon is for memory, reflection, anything involving the body or a mood, and it moves fastest through the week so it is the most flexible.

Mars is for the thing you have been avoiding, which is the single most reliable use of the day in practice. Wednesday is for information: writing, research, a conversation you need facts for. Jupiter is for expansion and expense, and the traditional advice not to buy anything on Thursday is a joke with a real point in it, because Thursday is the day most likely to spend. Friday is for the arts, the body, and anything about value. Saturday is for structure, labour, and the things with a deadline.

The mechanism that survives a deflationary account

Strip the correspondences out and one thing is left, and it is not small: a week with one fixed question attached to each day is a planning frame. Most people do not have one. Most people's weeks are a list that grows and no horizon at all.

The frame works because it does three things. It forces a decision about what a day is for, which is a decision people avoid. It limits you to one category at a time, so the categories do not compete. And it converts vague intentions into dated ones, which is the same mechanism the mechanism audit identifies as the load-bearing part of a money working.

So the useful question is not whether Mars is a warrior. It is whether the thing you have been avoiding got a day. If the frame moved it from your list to a date, the frame did its job, whatever the planet is doing.

Where planetary hours are worth the effort, and where they are not

The classical system divides each day into twelve planetary hours, so each of the seven planets rules one hour in rotation, and a full day and night is seventy-two hours of the week. The arithmetic is neat and the claim it encodes is that a specific planet governs a specific hour of a specific day.

This is the part of planetary magic where the enthusiasm and the practicality part company. To use planetary hours properly you need to know which hour of which day is currently on offer, which means an ephemeris or a table, at the moment of the working. Most of the traditional schedules are given in terms of planetary day order rather than clock hours, which is a different system and often confused with it in modern material.

The honest advice is that planetary hours are worth it for one specific use, which is choosing between two available windows when you are choosing anyway. They are not worth it as a reason to wait, because the ratio of lookup time to a twenty-minute working is severe, and the tradition itself is thin here — the sources that give the full chart are late and the sources that give the theory are vaguer still.

The Moon's twenty-eight mansions is the strongest claim in the system

If you want one part of traditional timing to take seriously, this is it. The lunar mansions divide the Moon's roughly twenty-eight-day cycle into twenty-eight segments, each about one day, each with a name, a set of qualities, and a list of activities considered appropriate or inappropriate.

The reason this one holds up better than the others is that it is computable. Given a date you can determine the mansion, and the criteria are specific enough to disagree about, which is what makes them useful. Other timing systems in the field are either too general to fail or too particular to check. The mansions are in the narrow band.

They are also the one part of lunar timing most modern astrology software handles correctly, since it needs an ephemeris anyway, which makes them far cheaper to use than the classical hour charts.

How to run a week without a system

You need three things and none of them are expensive. A paper or screen list of the seven days with a blank next to each. One intention per day, written as a thing that either happened or did not, because an intention you cannot tick is an intention you cannot review. And a marker for whether it landed.

The discipline is that you assign on Sunday and do not reassign mid-week. A frame you renegotiate daily is not a frame, it is a list, and the list is what you already had. The value of the planetary day is precisely that it was chosen in advance and cannot be gamed by your mood on a Tuesday.

The review is the whole practice. At week's end you have seven rows with two states each, which is the first genuinely useful piece of data about how your timing intuition works, and it is the only one you cannot get by thinking about it.

The trap: the frame gets used to avoid the frame

A timing system is a tool for moving a decision from the abstract to the dated, and the most common way it fails is that it becomes a way of not having the decision. Choosing the right day becomes a substitute for choosing the thing, and researching the right hour becomes a legitimate afternoon's work that produces no result and feels productive.

The second trap is fidelity creep. The tradition is not internally consistent about much, and a practitioner who tries to honour every source ends up with a system that requires the lunar mansion, the planetary hour, the day, the sign, the decan and a specific aspect, and by the time all six agree the working has moved a fortnight into the future or has quietly been abandoned.

The discipline that prevents both is a fixed complexity budget. Pick one system, the simplest one that fits, and use it for a season before you consider refining it. Every addition after the first system costs more in delay than it returns in specificity.

What the correspondence tradition actually offers you

The deeper use of the seven is not the days at all. It is as a set of seven distinct lenses, one per planet, for looking at a single situation. That is what the seven was originally a list of, and it is the version that keeps paying after the timing novelty wears off.

The Sun asks what is being displayed and who is meant to see it. The Moon asks what the actual feeling is, as opposed to the stated one. Mars asks what you are avoiding. Mercury asks what information you do not have. Jupiter asks what is being over-extended. Venus asks what you are calling value when you mean preference. Saturn asks what has been deferred for a year.

Seven questions, one situation, twenty minutes. It surfaces the thing that is blocking, it is the same operation as the paradigm shift protocol applied to a question rather than a belief, and it is the one use of the planetary set that does not require you to believe anything about planets.

Five ways planetary practice goes wrong

The most expensive is waiting for the right moment. A working deferred to a good day is a working not done, and the cost is not the ritual, it is the year. The second is the complexity budget, already described. The third is treating the correspondence as an instruction rather than a lens, which turns a question into a command and produces a plan you are supposed to execute instead of one you are supposed to examine.

The fourth is doing everything on its own day, which produces a beautifully distributed practice that never touches the specific thing you built the practice to deal with. The fifth, and the most common among people who read widely, is learning the dignity tables and never writing a single dated intention, which is study rather than practice and feels productive for years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is planetary magic just astrology with rituals added?

The correspondences come from the same tradition, but the two practices are not the same. Astrology here is most often a personality frame. Planetary magic uses the planets as a timing and prompt structure, and the useful half is the prompting. A planetary day is a day with one question attached, which is a planning device, and you can get that benefit whether or not Mars has anything to do with it.

Do I need an ephemeris to do planetary magic?

For the seven days, no. The day assignments are fixed and need no calculation, because the weekdays are the planets. You need an ephemeris only for the two more demanding layers, the planetary hours and the lunar mansions, and the mansions are cheap because any chart software will give you them. The hour charts are the expensive layer and are the one most practitioners can safely skip.

Is it worth waiting for the right planetary day to start something?

Not for a start, which is cheap and reversible. Waiting applies to the working itself, not to the decision, and getting that backwards is the main cost people incur. A beginning can happen on any day. The sigil can be made on Mars day. What is worth doing on a considered day is the charge and the release, because that is the part where the frame is doing work you will not do by accident.

Which single planetary practice gives the most return?

The seven-lens examination of one situation, not the calendar. It is twenty minutes, it needs nothing, it has no timing requirements, and it reliably finds the actual obstruction because the seven questions are chosen to cover the usual places. Most practitioners who adopt the day assignments first end up using the lenses more, because the lenses do not require them to believe anything.

What about the Moon's 28 mansions?

Worth more attention than they get, and the part of traditional timing I would take seriously first. The reason is that they are computable and their criteria are specific enough to disagree about, which puts them in the narrow band where a system can actually be tested against experience. They also cost nothing extra if you are already running a chart app, and they move fast enough to be a daily practice rather than a monthly one.
