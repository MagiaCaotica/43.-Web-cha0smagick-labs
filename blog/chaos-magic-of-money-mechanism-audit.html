How Does the Chaos Magic of Money Actually Work? A Mechanism Audit

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Somebody asks how money magic works and the honest answer is that the question has nine parts, and they do not have equal status. Four of them are ordinary psychology that would happen whether or not you drew anything. Two are decisions you make before the ritual that would have happened anyway. Two are claims that cannot be tested by the person making them. One is genuinely open. Sorting them is more useful than defending or mocking the whole practice, because the four ordinary parts are load-bearing in your results whether you believe in the rest or not.

Why the question is usually answered in one direction or the other

The money-magic question produces two confident answers and almost no analysis. One says it obviously works, and supports the claim with a story about a bill that cleared. The other says obviously it does not, and supports that with the observation that nobody who sells it is visibly wealthy. Both are doing the same thing: treating the practice as a single yes-or-no proposition when it is actually a bundle of separate claims with separate evidence.

There is a reason the debate stays stuck. Money is the one domain where a practitioner can tell a success story with plausible-looking details and no one can check it. A sigil for a toothache resolves in days and the resolution is visible to everyone. A sigil for a client paying an invoice might resolve in four weeks, and the invoice was going to be paid anyway, and nobody audits the counterfactual. The domain with the weakest verification available is the domain most saturated with claims.

Link one: the intention gets decided before the ritual

The first link is that a money working almost always follows a decision rather than producing one. The person asking for money help has usually already decided to do something about the money. They have already chosen to send the invoice, or to ask for the raise, or to look for the second client, or to cut the subscription. What they lack is not a decision but a starting point and the sense that it is real.

This is not a criticism of the practice. It is a description of where the effect is coming from. If the ritual supplies the start, the same result would have arrived from any other start. A phone call, a written email to yourself, a calendar entry with a date, a public statement to another person. These are not less magickal. They are the same mechanism wearing fewer layers.

Link two: the sigilisation compresses a decision into a handle

The second link is the one the tradition describes most accurately, and it survives deflation intact. Reducing a statement of intent to a mark is a compression operation. It takes a sentence that is easy to argue with, easy to soften, easy to reword later, and turns it into something you cannot argue with because you cannot read it.

That matters more than it sounds. An intention stated in words stays negotiable. Words can be reinterpreted tomorrow as having meant something slightly different, and you will not notice the reinterpretation happening. A compressed mark is fixed. When the review date arrives you can check a specific thing rather than a general mood, because the specific thing was fixed at the moment you drew.

The comparison that holds: a servitor specification and a sigil are the same operation at different scales. Both take a decision you have already made and package it so that maintaining it costs nothing. The traditional name for this is not power. It is a specification.

Links three and four: expectancy and the pre-commitment of a date

The third link is expectancy. Everyone who has ever tried to influence an outcome by expecting it has noticed that the expectation changes what they do. You check the bank balance more often. You notice an opportunity you would have scrolled past. You are more willing to send the awkward email because you are already braced for a no. None of this requires anything to be listening.

The fourth link is the one the ritual structure supplies most reliably. Writing down a date, a specific observable, and an acceptable conclusion before the work begins is a commitment device, and commitment devices are the most reliably effective thing in the entire field. Once you have written that if the observable has not happened by the fourteenth, you will treat the working as not having worked and take a specified alternative action, you have removed the option of quietly reinterpreting later.

Together these two links account for most of what people report. They are ordinary, they are well documented, and they would be happening anyway.

Links five and six: the two claims that cannot be tested by the person making them

The fifth link is the entity's intervention. If you hold that a spirit took an action on your behalf, you have made a claim that in principle someone else could check and in practice you cannot check, because you do not have access to the counterfactual ledger.

The sixth link is a subtler version. It is the claim that the working opened a door that would otherwise have stayed shut. This is nearly unfalsifiable from inside, because the counterfactual is by definition unobserved, and because a door that opens a month later looks exactly like a door that opened because you were watching.

Neither of these links has been demonstrated. The honest position is not that they are false. It is that the practice has no internal instrument for telling the difference between them and the ordinary links, which means a practitioner cannot use their own results as evidence either way.

Link seven: the pressure a fixed deadline puts on a decision you were avoiding

The seventh link is the most overlooked and the most reliably useful. A working with a date attached is a decision with a deadline, and a decision with a deadline is a different psychological object from a decision without one. The reason you have not done the thing about money is rarely that you lack the ability. It is that the thing has no date attached, so it never becomes urgent, and so it never gets done.

Attach a date to it and the arithmetic of your own behaviour changes without any external input. The date is often the entire active ingredient. This is why the tradition is emphatic about writing the review date down before the working and why it is right to be emphatic, just for a boring reason.

This link also explains the characteristic failure. A working with no date produces no deadline, produces no behaviour change, and produces the feeling that nothing happened, which is then interpreted as the spirit declining to act.

Link eight: the accounting artefact

The eighth link is the one people forget to count: money arrives in a system, and systems have throughput. A business with no pipeline has no revenue regardless of any sigil. A person with a full pipeline and no follow-up has revenue that stops at the first no. The reason abundance workings cluster around the same instruction in every tradition is that the instruction is correct and has nothing to do with magic.

Treat it as a link rather than a footnote. When someone asks whether the money magic worked, the most common honest answer is that the working produced a specific, named action, and the action happened to plug a specific, named gap in a system that would have leaked without it. Whether the plug would have been installed by some other route in some other week is genuinely unknown, and that unknown is the whole content of the dispute.

The useful move is to write down the gap before the working. Then the question stops being whether magic happened and becomes whether the gap closed, which is checkable.

Link nine: the only genuinely open question

The ninth link is the one that cannot be resolved from inside the practice and is the only one worth holding open honestly. Not whether you can change your own behaviour, which is settled. Not whether entities exist, which is not a question money magic can answer. But whether a specific, small, low-stakes, personally consequential outcome can be moved by a working whose ordinary links have been held constant.

This is answerable, and the design is straightforward. Pick something where the ordinary links are minimal: not money, where the behaviour links are overwhelming. A first move at that level has almost no behaviour-link contamination, which is exactly what makes it the right level to test at and the wrong level to bet anything on.

Hold the ordinary links constant: same intention formulated the same way, same build interval, same charge, same timing, same review date, same accounting. Two workings, one with the full practice and one with the practice minus the part you are testing. Fixed trial counts. Pre-written conclusions. This is the design of every good experiment and the reason the tradition has almost never run one, because the point of the ritual is the feeling, and the feeling is not in the control group.

What this audit says to do with the four ordinary links

The practical conclusion is not that the non-ordinary links are worthless. It is that the ordinary ones are free, reliable, and currently under-used. Most practitioners spend their attention on link five and none on link seven, which is the reverse of the correct allocation.

So: write the sentence before you draw, not after. Write the date and the acceptable conclusion before the working, not at the review. Name the gap in the system before the working, not after the result. If the result arrives, you will be able to say which link plausibly did it, and you will know what to build on. If the result does not arrive, you will know which link was missing, and the next working is better because of that.

The tradition's own strongest claim survives this audit intact. What the sigil does is take a decision you have already made and make it difficult to abandon. That is a real function. It is not a small one, and it does not require you to believe anything about the rest of the chain.

Five ways this audit gets misused

The first misuse is using it as a demolition. Sorting the claims does not make the ordinary links stop working, and a person who reads this and stops doing the boring parts loses real outcomes.

The second is using it as a shield. If every practice that succeeds is explained by expectancy and deadline, the position is unfalsifiable in the convenient direction. The audit cuts both ways: link nine is open, and the fact that nobody has closed it is a statement about the practice's method, not about the world.

The third is confusing plausible with sufficient. A mechanism that would account for the result is not thereby the mechanism. Every account in the list would produce the observed outcome, which is exactly the problem.

The fourth is claiming the audit proves the deflationary account. It does not. It shows that deflation has not been excluded, which is a different and much weaker statement.

The fifth is running link nine with a sample size of one and reporting it as settled. A single well-documented instance is a data point, not a result, and treating it as a result is how a practice ends up confident and wrong.

Frequently Asked Questions

So does money magic work or not?

Four of the nine links are ordinary and reliably produce results you will credit to the working: the decision, the compression, the expectancy shift, and the deadline. Two cannot be tested from inside the practice at all. One is genuinely open and cheap to test at a low-stakes level. The defensible position is that the ordinary links are real and you should use them, and that the rest has never been demonstrated either way.

Which of the nine links is the strongest explanation for a working that worked?

The deadline link, more often than practitioners expect. A money working almost always attaches a date and a specific observable to something you had already decided to do. The date changes your own behaviour in a way that is measurable without any metaphysical commitment. If you removed the date and kept everything else, the result would frequently disappear, and nothing would have been disproven about magic.

Does link nine mean I should stop doing workings on money?

No. Money is where you can most cheaply test a real outcome, because financial events are dated, quantified, and independently visible to other people. The contamination problem is that behaviour changes are large there, which makes it the worst place to isolate the thing you want to isolate. Test at a low-stakes consequential level and keep using the method at the level where it works.

Is expectancy really enough to explain a large financial change?

For a single working, usually. Expectancy plus a deadline plus a compressed specification is a strong package and it does not scale indefinitely, which is why link eight exists. If a person consistently produces large financial changes from small workings and no other change in their behaviour, that is a real anomaly worth investigating. One instance is not an anomaly. It is a Tuesday.

Why does nobody run the proper test?

Because the ritual is where the point of the practice is. The charge, the state, the moment of release is the thing practitioners value, and it cannot be in a control group without the group being obviously deprived. The scientific question and the existential question have different designs and the tradition has consistently chosen the second one. That is a legitimate choice. It does mean the first question stays open.
