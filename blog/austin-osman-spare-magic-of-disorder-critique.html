Austin Osman Spare and the Magic of Disorder: A Criticism

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 11 min read

Every sigil guide on the internet teaches you the same six moves: reduce each letter to its skeleton strokes, join them into one glyph, charge it, then forget it. Austin Osman Spare wrote the method in 1909 and most of what has been built on top of it since is his technique without his argument. He never claimed sigils cause events. He called the whole apparatus a focusing mechanism, wrote that the value lay in the forgetting rather than the making, and insisted the interesting thing was not the mark on the paper but the hole it opens in attention. Read the sources rather than the summaries and three of the standard teachings turn out to be wrong.

What Spare actually published, and when

Austin Osman Spare was born in 1886 and died in 1956, and in his working life he was a painter with a private system of notation. The system appears in <em>A Book of Art</em>, published in 1909, in <em>Sigil</em> from 1910, and in <em>The Ancestry of the Desire</em> from 1911. He was briefly an associate of Aleister Crowley, and Crowley's own <em>Liber ABA</em> also appeared in 1909, which is why the two names are permanently tangled in popular accounts even though their systems differ.

What Spare produced was not a grimoire. There was no pantheon, no temple, no initiatory hierarchy, no lineage of masters to obey. There was a set of drawing rules and a claim about what drawing them does to a person. That thinness is the point. The elaborate systems came later, mostly from Crowley and then from the twentieth-century ceremonialists who built rituals on top of him, and Spare is the odd source precisely because he refused to add the scaffolding.

Reduction is a transcription problem, not a magickal one

The mechanic everyone teaches is letter reduction. Take the word you want, write it out, strip each letter down to the strokes that carry its skeleton, and ligate what remains into a single symbol. The result is deliberately unreadable, which is the point: an unreadable mark cannot be picked up by your inner ear as a word, so it cannot be rehearsed. The reduction is therefore a mechanical, almost clerical operation. Almost any consistent scheme produces an acceptable glyph, which is a fact that troubles practitioners more than it should.

Here is the first place the summaries go wrong. Spare's reduction is not a single rule. It is a set of stylised letterforms he had already settled, and the reduction is applied to his own written hand rather than to a neutral typeface. Change the typeface and the skeleton changes. That means a sigil is a record of how a person writes, not a decoding of the language. Two practitioners can build entirely valid sigils for the same intention and share nothing beyond the intent, and that is fine, but it also means nobody can grade the aesthetic quality of someone else's sigil the way they can grade handwriting.

The distinction everyone drops: Seals are written, Sigils are forgotten

Spare drew a hard line between two objects that the modern literature has merged into one. A <strong>Seal</strong> is drawn, named, and then given away, left in a place, handed to a person. It persists. It is a message addressed to somebody. A <strong>Sigil</strong> is drawn, used, and then deliberately destroyed from memory. The forgetting is not a tidy-up step or a security measure. In Spare's account it is the operative event. The mark is a temporary scaffold for a concentration, and the moment the intention is released the scaffold should be gone.

The consequence is uncomfortable for anyone running a sigil archive. A stored sigil with a written key beside it is, in Spare's own vocabulary, a Seal pretending to be a Sigil. This is not pedantry. If the mark keeps its meaning in front of you, it functions as a reminder, and a reminder works by the mechanisms you already understand: recall, intention, anticipation. Nothing extra is happening. The version that requires you to reconstruct the charge from scratch each time you look at it is doing something different, and the cost of that reconstruction is exactly the thing Spare was building the sigil to avoid.

The 'infinity' claim is the part worth taking seriously

Spare's metaphysical claim is easy to skip and hard to dismiss. He treats the sigil as an aperture, an opening into what he calls the infinite or the divine, and the important sentence is the one about the mind supplying a place to stand. In plain terms: the mark is not the power. The mark gives a focused, narrow, deliberately anonymous object for attention to rest on, and what you then do with the attention is where anything real happens.

Read that way the method is a well-designed attentional device, and its design can be judged. A good attentional device does four things: it removes the audience, it removes the words, it is small enough to hold in one visual field, and it is specific enough that only one meaning can be attached to it. Reduction supplies the second and third. The naming supplies the fourth. The forgetting supplies the first, by guaranteeing that no witness, including yourself, can hold you to the charge. If you are assessing this on its own terms, those are the four things to test, and most of what gets sold as sigil technique is only supplying two of them.

Spare's scepticism is the most misquoted thing about him

Here is the problem with using Spare as a founding father. He did not think sigils made anything happen in the world. His position was closer to the psychophysical and he was explicit that the value was internal. When chaos magickers in the 1970s and after claimed him as a predecessor, what they took was the technique and quietly dropped the disclaimer. Peter Carroll's framing, which is the ancestor of nearly all modern practice, is causal in a way Spare's writing does not support.

This matters for a reason beyond historical accuracy. If sigils do not act on the world, then the burden of proof sits entirely on your own subsequent behaviour, and that is a harder standard than most people are willing to hold. It also explains why the honest practitioners in the tradition keep their workings small and behavioural: a sigil that commits you to a specific action at a specific time is doing something real regardless of your position on causation, whereas a sigil that asks the universe for money is doing nothing at all until you specify what you will do differently.

He anticipated the unconscious, and Jung said so

In <em>Psychology and Alchemy</em>, published in 1944, Carl Jung credits Spare with the concept of the subconscious and cites the 1909 work. That is a citable priority claim from a hostile-adjacent tradition, which is a rare kind of evidence. Jung was not being generous; he was tracking a genuine and now largely forgotten source in the English-language literature of the period.

The qualification matters. Spare's 'subconscious' is not Jung's. Jung's is a layered deposit of repressed material, and his is a developmental structure that shapes a person. Spare's is closer to a layer of mind that is not under conscious control and that responds to symbolic rather than verbal presentation, and that is nearer to a neurological description of implicit processing than to a psychoanalytic one. Reading him as an early psychologist rather than a mystic gets you further with modern attention research, and it also stops you from importing conclusions he never drew.

The biography is not decoration; it explains the method

Spare was confined in an asylum in the early 1910s after a series of attempts on his own life, was discharged, and continued producing work in a markedly altered register. He was poor for most of his life, sold a small number of drawings, and died in circumstances that were not dignified. Anyone treating him as a serene adept is reading a later myth rather than a person.

The method fits that biography better than a mystical one does. A man who could not reliably hold a stable sense of his own state developed a notation that removes the need to hold anything. The forgetting rule is not mystical discipline in his case, it is a plausible adaptation to a mind that would keep re-reading its own symbolic material and drain the charge. If you want to understand the technique rather than the legend, this is the most useful thing about him, and it is also a reason to expect the technique to work unevenly for people whose attention is stable.

Three standard teachings that do not survive the sources

First, the claim that sigils must be drawn in one unbroken stroke with no lifting of the pen. This appears in Spare's letterforms, which is a fact about the drawing of letters, not a rule about the sigil. The glyph is an assembly of letter parts; nothing in the sources requires a single line, and insisting on it is a later addition. Second, the claim that the charge happens by staring, drumming, or sexual arousal. Spare's accounts emphasise naming, consideration, and then dismissal. Sex enters some of the later ceremonial material around Crowley. The staring and drumming conventions are borrowed from entirely different systems and are usually presented as if Spare required them. Third, the claim that the forgetting must be total and immediate, or the working is void. The sources say the charge is released, not that you must blank your mind on cue. The practical requirement is that the meaning is not continuously rehearsed, and there is no evidence for a ritual of deliberate forgetting. Several otherwise careful teachers have turned this into a test of willpower, which is a different practice with the same vocabulary.

What survives as a usable method

Strip the metaphysics and four steps remain, and they are worth the trouble. Reduce the letters using one consistent scheme of your own, since the scheme itself carries no authority. Name the charge in plain language before you draw, so the sigil has one meaning and not five. Draw it, then do the specific physical thing the intention consists of. Then put the glyph away and do not look at it again until the review date you set in advance.

That is the whole method. It is roughly ten minutes of work. The reason it has a reputation for depth is not that it is complicated, it is that the fourth step is genuinely hard, and most people who claim to have done it have not. A free sigil generator will draw the glyph for you, which removes the only part that was ever doing psychological work, so if you use one, make yourself specify the reduction scheme rather than accepting the first glyph offered.

The test that would settle it

You cannot test causation, but you can test the attention claim, and the design is straightforward. Choose an intention that is behavioural and that has a binary outcome you can verify without interpretation. Draw two sigils on two different days for the same intention, then destroy each from memory. Write down, in advance, the two dates you will check and the specific observable you will record. Do not tell anyone which day was which until after both observations are written down.

The result that supports Spare's own account is that the usefulness of the sigil is inversely related to how much you look at it. If keeping the glyph in view keeps the intention alive, the mechanism is a reminder and you have proven the technique is ordinary. If the sigil works best precisely when you cannot reconstruct it, then something about the forgetting is doing the work and the interesting question becomes what that is. Either outcome is a real result, and the version of this practice where you never find out which is the case is the version that teaches nothing.

Where the standard retelling is useful anyway

None of this makes the method worthless or the man uninteresting, and it would be a different kind of error to swing the other way. Spare produced a working system with a specific theoretical claim, a clear mechanism, and a set of drawable artefacts, in a period when most occult writing was inherited theology. The reduction rules are good, the aperture idea is good, and the discipline of the released charge is good.

The failure is entirely in the transmission. What should have come down as a method with a stated internal claim became a technique in search of an external one, and then the external one was supplied retroactively by people who found it useful. Read the 1909 material rather than the modern handbook and you get a smaller, stranger, and more defensible practice. That is a better trade than the one on offer.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you actually build a sigil, step by step?

Write the intention out as a short phrase, then reduce each letter to the few strokes that carry its shape, joining the reduced letters into one glyph so the result cannot be read back as words. Name the charge in plain language before you draw, since the naming fixes the meaning and the glyph only holds it. Then carry out the specific physical action the intention describes, put the glyph out of sight, and leave it alone until the review date you set in advance.

What is the difference between a Seal and a Sigil in Spare's system?

A Seal is drawn and then given away, left somewhere, or handed to a person, and it persists as a message addressed to somebody. A Sigil is drawn, used, and then released from memory, and the forgetting is part of the operation rather than a tidy-up. A stored sigil with its key written beside it is functioning as a Seal, which means it is operating as a reminder using mechanisms you already understand.

Did Austin Osman Spare really discover the subconscious before Jung?

Jung credited him with the concept in Psychology and Alchemy in 1944, citing the 1909 work, so the priority claim has a citable source in a critical tradition. The two meanings differ substantially. Jung's subconscious is a layered deposit of repressed material that develops over a lifetime, and Spare's is a layer of mind outside conscious control that responds to symbolic rather than verbal presentation, which is closer to a description of implicit processing.

Did Spare believe sigils caused real events in the world?

Not in the way modern chaos magickers usually claim. He treated the apparatus as a focusing mechanism whose value was internal, with the mark supplying a narrow object for attention and the consequences happening in behaviour. That reading raises the bar rather than lowering it, because it puts the whole burden on what you then do, and it is why the honest practitioners in his tradition keep their workings behavioural and small.

Is it worth learning Spare's method directly instead of from modern guides?

Yes, and the reason is not mysticism. The sources are short, dated, publicly available, and they contain a mechanism rather than a set of rules, whereas the modern handbooks add three practices he did not teach. Reading the 1909 material gives you a smaller and stranger system with a stated claim you can actually test, which is a better position than holding a technique whose theory has been swapped out from under it.
