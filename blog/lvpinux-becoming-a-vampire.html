LVPINUX

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

The Liber Lvpinux is described online as a book about turning into a werewolf. It is not, and almost everything written about it online is describing a different book. What it actually contains is a short nineteenth century text concerned with lycanthropy as a spiritual and demonic subject, written by a man who believed in it and recorded what he thought was going on. This page works through the book itself, sets it against the other grimoires people reach for when they want to become something, and then asks the practical question: what can an honest practitioner take from a text whose central claim you have to reject while still respecting the author enough to use his method properly. The answer turns out to be narrower than most people want, and considerably more useful than most of what is sold under the same name.

What the Liber Lvpinux actually contains

Three parts, of uneven quality. The first is a polemic against the ordinary explanations of lycanthropy, arguing at length that the condition is demonic rather than medical. The second is closer to a grimoire, giving ritual instructions for the transformation. The third is a set of claims about how the transformed person behaves, drawn from a mix of trial, hearsay and folklore the author does not distinguish between.

That mixture is the honest description of most books in this corner of the tradition, and it is worth holding onto. When you read a text like this you are reading one man's certainty assembled from sources of very different reliability, written without any intention of being checked by a later reader. Treating that as a research finding is the first mistake, and treating it as worthless is the second.

Why the text has no werewolf transformation ritual in it

The book describes a werewolf condition and describes how to reach it. It does not contain the specific item that a generation of web posts attributes to it. What you will find described as the Liber Lvpinux ritual is almost always a modern construction, assembled from the general shape of ceremonial practice and from other books, then filed under this title because the title sounds right.

This is a small failure and it repeats constantly across the online grimoire scene, for the same reason it repeats in history: nobody checks, because checking is work and repetition is free. If a claim about a text would embarrass you if you had the text open, go and have the text open.

Where the manuscript sits in the grimoire tradition

Late nineteenth century, privately printed, tiny circulation, and effectively out of print for most of the twentieth. That placing matters more than the claims do. The book was not written to serve a lineage, and it was not transmitted through an order with a curriculum behind it. It is a private document that happened to be printed, which makes it a strange object to build a practice on.

Compare it with the ceremonial literature that does have a lineage, where instructions are given in a form that assumes an initiation behind them and a teacher in front of you. The Liber Lvpinux has neither. Reading it as though it were written for you personally is reading a Victorian man's private worries as though they were your own.

The three instructions the book does give

Stripped of rhetoric, the actionable content is small. There is a preparation period, a stated lunar condition for attempting the change, and a set of warnings about the state of mind the practitioner should be in. There is also a fairly clear statement of what happens afterwards, which is social rather than physical: the transformed person loses access to ordinary human society.

That last point is the one the modern retellings leave out, and it is the most important thing in the book. Whatever the author thought was happening, the consequence he described was total and irreversible isolation. A practice whose documented outcome includes losing your job, your family and your ability to be recognised is a different proposition from the one sold under its name.

  • A stated preparation period rather than an immediate attempt
  • A lunar condition attached to the timing, with a threshold for waiting
  • A requirement about mental state, described vaguely but treated as load-bearing

How the werewolf claim entered circulation

The book was obscure for a century. What made it famous was the internet, and what made it useful to the internet was a title that fits neatly into a list of things you can become. Once it was on those lists, the specific claims became less important than the association, and the association is still doing most of the work in search results today.

Nothing about this makes the book bad. It makes the book misread, and misreading is more expensive than a bad book because you can act on it. A bad grimoire tells you to do something pointless. A misread one tells you to do something pointless with a certainty you did not earn.

Reading the text as a document rather than a promise

Put it beside a medieval grimoire and the difference is instructive. The grimoire assumes a world in which its instructions will work, and it is not embarrassed about that. The Liber Lvpinux is arguing with a world that had started to explain lycanthropy medically, and it is losing. Read it as a losing argument written by a man who was sure, and it becomes far more interesting and far less instructive.

The instructive part is the structure of his certainty, because you can borrow the method without borrowing the conclusion. He made a claim, committed to it, recorded what he observed, and did not revise. That is honest self-report of a certain kind. It is not evidence, and the difference between those two sentences is the difference this article is trying to keep visible.

The one transformation the text does describe

Shift of identity, not of body. Everything in the book that can be taken seriously is about how a person relates to themselves once they believe something impossible about themselves. The physical details are folklore imported wholesale, and they are the part with the least support and the most coverage.

That makes the book less dangerous than it looks and more useful than it is given credit for. If you are drawn to the material, the identity question is the live one, and it is a question worth asking carefully rather than ceremonially.

What a modern practitioner can honestly take from it

Three things. A preparation period, which is good practice in any tradition. A timing condition, which at least acknowledges that not everything is done on demand. And a warning about isolation, which is the single most valuable sentence in the book, because the documented consequence of going all in is losing your ordinary life.

What you should not take from it is the certainty. The book offers no reason to believe its central claim and no way to check it, and a practice built on an unchecked claim inherits that weakness permanently. If you are serious, the correct response is to run the method with the claim set aside, and see what you can defend afterwards.

A working built on the book's actual method

If you want to work with the material without inheriting the claim, the structure is simple. Take the identity question and put it somewhere you can measure it. Not a question about whether you could transform, but a question about what you are already doing when you feel most unlike yourself, and whether you can make that state deliberate rather than accidental.

Then keep the parts of the book that are about timing, because timing is free and the tradition was right to include it. A lunar condition costs nothing to honour and gives the practice a shape that survives a bad week. Drop the parts about teeth.

  1. Write the identity question in a form you could answer in a month
  2. Keep the preparation period even though nothing in it is verifiable
  3. Honour the timing, since it costs nothing and the shape is the point

The danger of borrowing a claim the source refuses

Once a claim travels between texts it acquires authority from repetition. A modern ritual attributed to a nineteenth century book borrows the book's name and none of its context, and after two more repetitions it is treated as canonical, because nobody remembers there was a moment when somebody invented it.

You can avoid this cheaply. Before using anything attributed to a specific book, find the book. In this case it takes one search to discover that the central ritual everyone cites is not in it, and having discovered that once you will not unlearn it.

Where the material is honestly uncertain

The provenance is uncertain in ways nobody has resolved. The author was not a magician, the circulation was tiny, the sources are unlabelled, and the earliest printed material about it is late. A scholar would say the text is real, poorly sourced and impossible to check, which is a perfectly respectable condition for a book and a poor one for an instruction manual.

The uncertainty is the reason to be careful rather than the reason to dismiss it. Dismissal is a claim too, and it is the claim that stops you asking useful questions. The defensible position is narrower: this is a document, it is interesting, its instructions cannot be relied on, and its warnings are worth more than its promises.

The question to ask before you begin

Ask what would have to be true for the book's central claim to be true, and then ask whether you would still want the practice if you knew it was false. That second question is the one that sorts people. If the answer is yes, you have a workable practice and an honest frame. If the answer is no, the practice has taken up residence in your identity, and that is the failure the book itself predicted.

The material is not going anywhere. There are copies, the text is short, and the interesting questions about it are still open. You can spend a month with it on the table rather than a decade with it in your hands, and nothing is lost by taking the slower route.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the Liber Lvpinux actually contain a werewolf ritual?

Not the one people describe. The book discusses lycanthropy at length, argues it is demonic, and gives a set of instructions that are vaguer than the popular version suggests. The specific ritual attributed to it online was assembled later from other sources. If a text is telling you this book contains something it does not, that is a reason to distrust the source rather than the book.

Can you become a werewolf?

No, and no mechanism exists that would allow it. As an identity and a practice, yes, and that version is entirely available to anyone willing to live with the consequences the source itself describes, which include losing ordinary human society. Decide which of those two you are actually asking about before spending any time on it.

Is vampiric or lycanthropic practice harmful?

The documented risk is psychological rather than supernatural, and it concentrates in people with dissociation or psychotic vulnerability. Identity-absorbing practice, where you take a non-human identity seriously enough to organise your life around it, can destabilise a person who is already unstable. If that describes your situation, speak to a clinician before starting, not after.

What does an app cost you that a library loan does not?

Into indexing, search and cross-referencing, and those are genuine advantages when you own two hundred texts. What it cannot do is tell you which of them are worth reading, and that is the decision the app exists to appear to make for you. Buy the one that answers the question you actually have, which is usually finding a specific entry quickly.

Would a paid app beat pen and paper for this practice?

For finding a name inside a text, yes. For working out whether your understanding of a grimoire is correct, no, and no app can do that part, because the test is whether you can explain the argument to somebody else. Spend money on retrieval and keep the interpretation in your own hands, where it belongs.
