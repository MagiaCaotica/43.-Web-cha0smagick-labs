The Witch-King: Building a Servitor That Holds Authority Instead of Granting Wishes

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 14 min read

The title promises a servant that transforms your reality. That promise is doing three separate jobs at once, and only one of them is a decision you get to make. The Witch-King is also not one thing: a crowned demon from the grimoire tradition, a horned pagan deity reconstructed in the twentieth century, and a piece of literary folklore are three unrelated figures that share a crown and nothing else. Mixing them produces an instrument with no consistent behaviour, which is why so many people report a powerful servant and can never predict what it does. Strip the name apart, decide which figure you are actually building, and you get something you can specify, test, and dismiss. What follows is that separation, and then a design sheet for the one version of the Witch-King that is worth building.

What the title is actually promising

Read the claim as three separate assertions and you can see which ones survive contact with a working practice. First: that a servant exists and can be addressed. That is a design decision, and you make it. Second: that reality is transformed. That is a prediction about the world, and it has to be falsifiable or it teaches you nothing. Third: that the servant is a Witch-King. That is a specification question, and it is the one most videos skip.

The usual content on this subject spends its whole runtime on the second assertion, describing a feeling of power, a sense of the presence becoming real, a widening of possibility. None of that is wrong as an experience. It is just not a claim that can fail, and a claim that cannot fail cannot tell you whether your next working should be different from your last one.

So this article keeps the first assertion, replaces the second with a mechanism, and spends most of its length on the third. A Witch-King you have specified is buildable. A Witch-King you have inherited from three incompatible traditions is not, because he has no consistent behaviour to specify.

Three different figures share the name

The grimoire meaning is the least romantic and the most useful. In the Ars Goetia, twenty of the seventy-two are ranked King: crowned, territorial, and defined by the power to judge, punish and close a matter. Balam, Allocer and Uca are the ones most often worked. These are demon kings, and their function is adjudication. They do not grant; they rule. That is exactly the behaviour you would want if you were designing an instrument for decisions rather than for luck.

The pagan meaning is a twentieth-century reconstruction with a thin evidence base. The name Cernunnos comes from a single Gallo-Roman altar found at Pommerol, and even the identification of that altar's figure as a Celtic god is scholarly inference. The much-repeated pairing of a Horned God with a Great Mother comes from Robert Graves, whose The White Goddess appeared in 1948 and did more to shape modern pagan practice than any earlier text. Graves was a poet working from fragmentary sources and he knew it. What he produced is a beautiful and influential fiction that a century of practitioners then treated as recovered tradition.

The folklore meaning is a literary one. The witch-king of Victorian and Edwardian story is a figure of the old religion who returns when the Christian order weakens, and he is usually depicted as either benevolent or tyrannical depending on the author's politics. That is a narrative device. It has no ritual procedure attached to it anywhere.

These three do not agree about what a crown is for. One says judge, one says fertilise and lead, one says reclaim a world. A servant built from all three will do all three unpredictably, and the practitioner will conclude the servant is powerful rather than incoherent. It is more likely to be the second.

A servant you make, or a thoughtform you inherit

This is the distinction that decides the rest of the build, and it is the one that gets skipped most often. A servitor is a thoughtform you construct: you design its permissions, you design its address, you design its dissolution, and because you designed it you can predict it. You can also lose it, which is a real risk, but the risk comes from neglect rather than from other people using it.

An egregore is a shared thoughtform with a history and, usually, other contributors. Goetia itself is the standard example. Building into an existing egregore means drawing on something already shaped by centuries of use, which is genuinely more power in one sense and considerably less control in another. The person you are working with has an established temperament, established taboos and existing obligations you cannot see.

The title says Invocation, which implies the second. The title also says Magical Servant, which implies the first. If you build a private servitor and call it an invocation, you will find it behaves like the well-behaved tool you made, and you will wonder why it never feels like an ancient power. If you petition a Goetia King and expect a private tool, you will find it has moods you did not specify.

Decide now. The rest of this article builds the private servitor, because that is the version you can specify, test and dismiss. If you want the egregore route, the discipline is the same but the design sheet is replaced by a petition, and you give up authorship of the result.

What kingship is actually good for

Take the Goetia definition seriously and kingship becomes a genuinely useful design brief. A king adjudicates. He hears a matter, applies a rule, and returns a verdict that either stands or does not. He can refuse. He can end a dispute. He can say that a thing is not permitted, and that refusal is a feature rather than a failure.

Every other promise in the title is harder to deliver. A servant that grants wishes has no criteria, so it either says yes to everything or nothing ever happens. A servant that transforms reality has no definition of the transformation, so there is no way to know whether it worked. A servant that protects you has to decide what counts as a threat to you, which means it will eventually act in a way you disapprove of, and you will not have authorised that.

The adjudicator brief is the only one with a natural stopping condition. A matter is opened, a rule is stated, a verdict is returned, the matter closes. If it stays open, that is information. The instrument is behaving like a king, and kings do not leave cases open.

It is also the brief most likely to surprise you, because an adjudicator's first real act is usually a refusal, and refusals are hard to mistake for flattery.

The cat problem

Cats are the witch's familiar in nearly every version of the folklore, and the familiar's defining quality is not loyalty. It is independence. Familiar traditions describe contracts with a bound spirit: the cat observes, it agrees, it may withhold, and it leaves when the terms are broken. It does not take orders enthusiastically.

A Witch-King of cats is therefore a category error, and it is worth naming because the error is revealing. Kingship and familiarity are two different relationships to the same animal archetype. The familiar dispatches. The king adjudicates. Trying to build one construct that does both produces something that either chases tasks without judgement or judges without ever doing anything.

If both functions are useful, build two constructs with two names, two addresses and two dissolutions, and keep them separate. This sounds like extra work until you notice how much of the confusion in servitor practice comes from expecting one instrument to be a butler, a judge and a bodyguard at once.

The non-ritual version of the same structure is worth naming too, because it is the honest fallback. Dispatch is delegation: you write the message and somebody else sends it, and the sending is most of the work. Adjudication is a decision procedure: you write the criteria before you see the options, and you apply them. Both can be done with no occult framing at all, and doing them first means you know which of them the construct is actually contributing to.

The design sheet

A servitor specification needs five fields. Most attempts supply one and a name. The name is optional and the other four are what make the instrument predictable.

Permission: the enumerated list of things the construct may act on. Write it as a closed list, not an open one. A list of ten specific permitted actions produces predictable behaviour; a general grant of authority to help you produces a construct that decides for itself what helping means, which means you get a second problem to solve.

Refusal: what it must decline. Every usable construct needs at least one. A servant with no refusal condition is a mirror, and a mirror that answers affirmatively is worse than no servant because you will act on it.

Address: how you summon it. One fixed procedure, written out, identical every time. If the address changes from sitting to mood, the construct tracks your state rather than your intention, and you cannot tell which one is being contacted.

Term: the condition that ends it. Written before the build, not after the first worry. A servitor with no end condition becomes an ongoing obligation you feel vaguely guilty about, and the guilt is the real cost, not any supernatural consequence.

Cost: what you put in at the build. Something already spent, unrecoverable, and required by nobody else. This is not decoration. A construct built with nothing invested is a specification you have written down, and specifications do not get consulted when you are tired and the deadline is close.

Petition, not summons

The grammar of the request is where most of the usable effect sits, and it differs sharply from the grammar of a purchase. You are not ordering a servant. You are submitting a matter to something with standing, and the standing is what you are borrowing.

A petition has five parts. Title, naming the office rather than the name, because the office is the part with a consistent behaviour. The matter, stated as a specific question with an answer that can be yes or no. What you are offering, which for a private construct means what you will do with the verdict. What happens if refused, written before you submit. And what you will do regardless, which is the part that does the most work.

That last field is the mechanism. A construct that adjudicates a matter you have already decided to act on regardless has changed exactly one thing: it has forced you to write down what you will do, in advance, with a date attached. That is the part of the working which survives a deflationary account of magic entirely, because it is a written commitment device and commitment devices are known to change behaviour.

The refusal condition matters for the same reason. Pre-writing what you do if the verdict is no is the step that stops a ruling from becoming an excuse.

The test that most of these fail

Here is the discriminating question: does the construct ever say no? Everything that feels like a powerful servant and is not one can be characterised by a single property. It agrees. Across many matters, over weeks, it finds a way to agree, and each agreement is accompanied by a sense of expansion and possibility.

That feeling is real and it is worth having. It is also produced by any well-made request made with conviction, and conviction is the cheapest input in the practice. The construct that only ever agrees is not transmitting an authority, it is reflecting a mood.

Run the test before you build rather than after, because running it after is how you end up defending a mirror. Set up five matters of which three are ones you would prefer to lose. Not tests you are confident about. Matters where the answer you want is no. Submit them through the ordinary address and record the verdicts with dates.

If the record shows refusals, you have an adjudicator and the refusals are its most useful output. If it shows three agreements on matters you wanted to lose, the instrument is not adjudicating, and the honest move is to accept that you built a focus and use it as one. A focus is a useful thing. It is just not a king.

Signs it landed, signs it did not

Landed: you begin declining things. Not dramatic refusals, just the ordinary ones, an invitation you used to accept, a commitment you used to make, a message you used to send while irritated. A functioning adjudicator reduces the number of decisions you make while angry, because you now have a written criterion to check against.

Landed: the verdicts are uncomfortable and specific. Kings do not produce warm generalities, because a warm generality is not a verdict. If your log is full of encouraging generalities about your potential, you are reading your own reflection.

Did not: nothing ever gets refused. Check whether you actually submitted the three matters you wanted to lose, or whether you quietly rephrased them into ones you were happy to win. This is a common and almost invisible substitution.

Did not: the construct is more active when you are tired than when you are clear. That is an address problem, not a power problem. The construct is contacting your state instead of your intention, and the fix is to make the address physical and identical rather than more emotionally emphatic.

Did not: you cannot say what would make you dismiss it. Without a pre-written term you have not built a tool, you have started a relationship with an unclear exit, and the exit is where constructs of this kind usually fail.

Five ways this goes wrong, ranked by cost

First, and most expensive: the construct acquires authority over other people. You wanted a decision procedure and you built something whose function is to overrule other people's choices. That is a different act from helping you decide, it does not stay inside your own affairs, and it is the failure mode that has real consequences. Build adjudication over your own matters only.

Second: scope creep into general assistance. The general grant of authority to help you produces a construct that solves adjacent problems you had not asked about, and the first one it solves is one you would have preferred to stay unsolved.

Third: inheriting instead of building. Petitioning an existing egregore and expecting private-tool behaviour means every surprising result has two explanations and no way to tell them apart, so you learn nothing from any of it.

Fourth: the mirror. Covered above, and ranked fourth only because it is the most common and the least harmful. A focus is a legitimate instrument; mistaking it for a judge is an error of description rather than of conduct.

Fifth, and cheapest: the flattery trap. A construct built specifically to be reassuring will be reassuring, and the whole exercise becomes a mood. The three matters you wanted to lose cost you five minutes to set up and they are the only thing standing between a practice and a compliment.

What to build instead

Take the strongest part of the Witch-King image, which is the judgement, and attach it to the one function the tradition supports most consistently: settling a dispute you are already inside. That is a smaller claim than the title makes and it is the one with a real chance of being true.

The build is short. Write the four refusal conditions. Write the term. Set the fixed address. Submit three matters where you want to lose. Log the verdicts with dates. Review on a date you wrote down in advance, and if two of the three came back agreeable when they should not have, you have learned the useful thing, which is that you wanted permission and were asking the wrong instrument for it.

None of this requires a belief about whether anything supernatural is present. It requires that you write things down before you know how you feel, and that you check them on a date. That is the whole of the practice that survives every account of what magic is, and it is also the part that most people skip.

If you want the visual and the traditional frame, keep them. They are not the mechanism and they are not hurting anything. Just do not let them stand in for the four fields, because a crown on a piece of paper is a very effective way to feel that a specification has been met.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Witch-King a real spirit?

There is no evidence that any particular Witch-King exists as a being you could petition. What does exist is a category in Western grimoire literature: twenty of the seventy-two in the Ars Goetia are ranked King, and their described function is judgement and territory. Working with that category is a practice of design and attention. Whether the design is answered by anything is a separate question, and the honest test is the one above, not the feeling of presence.

Why not simply invoke Cernunnos or Pan?

Because you would be inheriting a twentieth-century literary construction rather than an attested ancient office. The name Cernunnos rests on a single first-century altar, and the famous Horned God and Great Mother pairing comes from Robert Graves in 1948, who assembled it knowingly from fragments. That is not a reason to avoid the symbol. It is a reason to know that you are building from a text, and that the text was written by a poet who told you he was guessing.

What is the difference between a servitor and an egregore?

A servitor is a thoughtform you construct, so you control its permissions, its address and its end condition, and you can predict and dismiss it. An egregore is a shared thoughtform with a history and usually other contributors, which gives it more accumulated force and much less predictability. The two are not rivals. Working with a Goetia egregore and building a private servitor are different tools for different jobs, and the commonest confusion in this area is building one and expecting the behaviour of the other.

How do I know if my servant is just telling me what I want to hear?

Submit three matters where the answer you want is no, through the ordinary address, and record the verdicts with dates before you submit them. An instrument that adjudicates will refuse at least some of them. An instrument that agrees with all three is reflecting your mood, and no amount of intensity on your part will change that, because intensity is the input that is cheapest to supply.

Can a servant hold authority over other people?

Do not build one that tries. Build adjudication over your own affairs and the construct stays inside a boundary you can actually hold. An instrument whose function is to overrule other people's decisions does not stay in your affairs, and it will produce the appearance of authority long before it produces the substance of it, which is the most dangerous combination available to a beginner. The useful version of kingship is deciding, not ruling.
