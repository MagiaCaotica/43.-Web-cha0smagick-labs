Community Blog | President Bifrons: Ruler of Death and Transformation

Introduction

President Bifrons governs the mysteries of death and transformation in the Goetia. He sits as the forty-sixth spirit in the Ars Goetia, the first book of the Lesser Key of Solomon, holding the dual rank of President and Earl. This is not a spirit you call casually. Bifrons deals in the currency of endings—physical death, the death of circumstances, the alchemical putrefaction that precedes every genuine rebirth. My own encounters with him have been blunt, humorless, and surprisingly practical. He does not trade in gothic aesthetics; he trades in the mechanics of how things fall apart and how they can be reassembled.

The classical sources describe him as a monster with two heads who appears as a man when commanded, teaching astronomy, geometry, and the virtues of herbs and precious stones. He moves dead bodies and buries them under candles, and he can change the location of corpses. That last part—moving corpses—sounds like stage magic until you understand what it actually means in practice: the relocation of influence, the shifting of ancestral patterns, the literal and metaphorical exhumation of what was buried. In chaos magick terms, Bifrons is the operator who manages the necrotic current—the entropy vector that makes transformation possible.

What distinguishes Bifrons from other death-adjacent spirits is his bureaucratic precision. He commands six legions. His sigil is not decorative; it is a functional key. When he appears in the fiery king form described in the grimoires, the visual phenomenon serves a purpose: the fire illuminates what the darkness concealed. Practitioners who approach him with theatrical expectations tend to leave disappointed. Those who approach with a specific technical problem—a stuck inheritance, a family curse needing exhumation, a physical space requiring cleansing of residual death-energy—often find him remarkably effective.

Bifrons' Domain

Bifrons rules over the passage of souls, the nature of death, and the alchemical transformation of matter and spirit. In the grimoire taxonomy, his portfolio is specific: he teaches the liberal sciences—astronomy, geometry, and the virtues of herbs and precious stones. That pedagogical function is not incidental. The liberal sciences in the medieval worldview were the tools for reading the book of nature; Bifrons grants the practitioner the literacy to read the signatures of death and decay in the material world. When you understand the geometry of a crystal lattice or the astronomy of planetary hours, you understand the architecture that binds spirit to matter—and how to loosen those bonds.

His authority over "moving dead bodies and burying them under candles" has been wildly misinterpreted by modern occultists who want necromancy to look like a horror film. The historical reality is more mundane and more useful. In the early modern period, "moving bodies" referred to the legal and ecclesiastical business of exhumation, reburial, and the translation of relics. Candles marked sacred ground. Bifrons governs the protocols of displacement: how a thing that has ended gets relocated, recontextualized, made useful again. This applies to ancestor work, to clearing haunted properties, to the psychological exhumation of buried trauma. The candles are the witness; the body is the vessel; the movement is the operation.

He also governs the transformation of bodies and souls—what the alchemists called solve et coagula applied to the animating principle. This is not reincarnation in the Eastern sense. It is the technical process by which a consciousness-pattern, stripped of its biological substrate, can be reshaped, redirected, or dissolved. Chaos magicians recognize this as the manipulation of the gnostic body or body of light. Bifrons provides the operating system for that manipulation. His six legions execute the micro-operations: severing silver cords, rewriting karmic imprints, managing the entropy gradient that allows a pattern to dissolve without scattering into noise.

The dual rank—President and Earl—signals his position in the infernal hierarchy. As President, he commands; as Earl, he administers territory. The territory is the threshold between living and dead, the liminal zone where the rules of biology suspend and the rules of spirit take over. Cross that threshold without authorization and you get lost. Cross it with Bifrons' seal and you get a guided tour with practical applications.

His Sigil and Symbolism

His sigil depicts a ghastly face with a serpent's body, symbolizing the merger of death and renewal. The classical description from the Ars Goetia and its antecedents in the Pseudomonarchia Daemonum gives us a two-headed monster—one head facing forward, one backward—who transforms into a man of noble bearing when commanded. The two heads are not decorative. They represent the binocular vision required for death work: one eye on what dies, one eye on what emerges. The serpent body is the kundalini current, the chthonic force that moves through the earth and the spine alike, connecting the underworld to the crown.

When Bifrons appears as the "fiery king" described in the grimoires, the fire is not punishment—it is revelation. The flames render visible the normally invisible architecture of the dead: the cords, the anchors, the residual charges that bind a spirit to a place or a lineage. In practical work, visualizing the sigil as a burning brand rather than a static line drawing changes the quality of contact. The sigil becomes a lens. I have found that inscribing it on black obsidian or jet (stones Bifrons himself governs) and charging it under a waning Saturn hour produces a tool that functions as a divinatory mirror for ancestor communication and a key for opening cemetery gates—literal and metaphorical.

The correspondence with precious stones and herbs is not a shopping list. Each stone and herb in Bifrons' materia medica functions as a specific frequency key. Jet for absorption and grounding. Obsidian for mirror-work and truth. Garnet for the vital heat that prevents dissolution from becoming dispersion. The herbs—typically those associated with Saturn and the underworld: cypress, yew, wormwood, asphodel—serve as the chemical analogues to the spiritual operations. A competent practitioner builds a materia kit tuned to Bifrons' current: stones for the structural work, herbs for the fluid work, candles for the witness, sigil for the authority.

Powers and Responsibilities

Bifrons teaches the art of gems and precious stones, and governs the transformation of bodies and souls. The grimoire listing is deceptively brief: astronomy, geometry, herbs, stones, moving corpses, changing corpse locations. Each item is a category header for a vast technical domain. Astronomy under Bifrons is not modern astrophysics; it is the stellar timing of necromantic operations—the heliacal risings, the planetary hours, the lunar mansions that govern when the veil thins and the dead can be reached. Geometry is the spatial logic of burial grounds, the sacred geometry of cemetery layouts, the precise measurements for protective circles and spirit traps.

The "art of gems and precious stones" is a complete lapidary science oriented toward the dead. Each stone has a correspondence to a specific type of spirit, a specific mode of death, a specific ancestral lineage. Diamonds for the violent dead. Pearls for the drowned. Opals for those who died in madness. Jet for the ancestors. The practitioner who masters this art can construct lapidary talismans that function as batteries for necromantic force, as anchors for specific spirits, as filters that allow only certain categories of the dead to approach. This is not folklore; it is a technology with repeatable results.

His governance of "transformation of bodies and souls" extends to the alchemical operations of calcination, dissolution, separation, conjunction, fermentation, distillation, and coagulation applied to the subtle anatomy. In the laboratory, these operations transmute lead to gold. In the necromantic laboratory, they transmute a trapped ghost into an ancestor ally, a curse into a blessing, a haunted house into a power spot. Bifrons' six legions are the laboratory technicians. They execute the micro-steps. The magician provides the intention and the protocol. The result is only as good as the protocol.

One power rarely discussed in modern summaries: Bifrons can reveal the location of hidden treasures—not only gold, but lost knowledge, forgotten graves, concealed family secrets. The "treasure" is whatever was buried and needs recovery. This makes him invaluable for genealogical research, for finding lost wills, for recovering suppressed family histories that manifest as generational curses. The mechanism is the same: he moves the earth that covers the secret.

Working with Bifrons

Work with Bifrons when undergoing major life transitions, death work, or transformation rituals. The standard grimoire approach—circle, triangle, license to depart, sigil, incense, planetary hour—works if you have the training and the discipline. For chaos magicians, the approach is different: we treat the grimoire as a user manual for a piece of software we're reverse-engineering. The parameters are the same (Saturn hour, waning moon, cemetery dirt, black candle, jet or obsidian), but the interface is flexible. A sigil charged with gnosis and fired into the subconscious can replace the physical circle if the operator's will is coherent. The license to depart becomes a mental command structure. The incense becomes a breathing pattern.

Practical protocol I've used: Prepare a necromantic kit—jet stone, cypress incense, black candle, graveyard dirt from a crossroads or ancestor's grave, a photograph or personal effect of the target (if working for another), the sigil drawn on parchment with dragon's blood ink. Open at the Saturn hour on a Saturday (waning moon preferred). State the objective in writing first—Bifrons responds to precision. "Move the body of X from location A to location B" translates to "Relocate the influence of ancestor X from the family pattern of addiction to the family pattern of recovery." The metaphor becomes the mechanism.

Offerings: not blood. Bifrons accepts the offering of work. The agreement is: you do the integration work after he does the extraction work. He moves the corpse; you rebury it properly. He reveals the treasure; you do the legal and emotional labor of claiming it. He teaches the geometry; you build the temple. Practitioners who skip their side of the bargain find the results reverse—entropy reasserts itself, the pattern scatters, the haunting returns worse. The oath in "oath-bound necromancer" goes both ways.

Safety protocols: Banish before and after (LBRP, Gnostic Pentagram, or your preferred method). Do not work in your living space—use a dedicated temple room or outdoor site. Keep a physical anchor (the jet stone) on your person for 72 hours after contact to ground the current. Dream journal mandatory—Bifrons communicates heavily through dreams in the week following contact. If you experience persistent death-dreams, intrusive thoughts of suicide, or physical cold spots in your home, cease work immediately and perform a full cleansing. This is not theatrical warning; it is technical specification for a high-voltage current.

Why Bifrons Is Called the Oath Spirit

The epithet "oath-bound necromancer" does not appear in the classical grimoires—it is a modern descriptor that emerged from the chaos magick current's engagement with Bifrons, and it captures something essential about his mode of operation. Every spirit in the Ars Goetia is bound by the oaths of Solomon, the seals, the divine names that constrain them. Bifrons, however, specializes in binding. His function is to bind the dead to their rest, to bind the living to their ancestors, to bind a spirit's word to its deed. When you work with him, you enter a reciprocal binding: he performs the operation, you perform the integration. The oath is the contract.

This is why Bifrons is dangerous for dilettantes. A hobbyist wants results without obligation. Bifrons delivers results that create obligation. The corpse he moves creates a vacancy that must be filled. The treasure he reveals creates a claim that must be pursued. The ancestor he contacts creates a relationship that must be maintained. The oath is not a moral judgment; it is a thermodynamic law. Energy moved from one configuration to another requires a new equilibrium. The practitioner who understands this designs the new equilibrium before calling the spirit. The practitioner who doesn't becomes the equilibrium—possessed by the very current they summoned to serve them.

In my own practice, the "oath" manifests as a geas: once you've used Bifrons to exhume a family pattern, you cannot un-know it. You are bound to act on the knowledge. The spirit holds you to the work. This is why experienced necromancers approach him with trepidation and precise contracts. We know the price. We pay it willingly because the alternative—leaving the corpse buried, the pattern unexamined, the ancestor unheard—is a slower, messier death.

Death Magick: Ethics and Alternatives

Death magick sits at the sharp end of ethical ambiguity. The grimoires do not discuss ethics; they discuss authority. The King commands, the spirit obeys. Modern practitioners, stripped of the medieval cosmology that justified that authority, must construct their own ethical frameworks. My framework is simple: consent and necessity. Do not work death magick on a living target without their informed consent. Do not disturb the dead without necessity—curiosity is not necessity. Necessity means: a haunting that threatens health, a family pattern that destroys generations, a legal matter requiring ancestor testimony, a land that needs cleansing for the living to thrive.

The alternatives to direct necromancy are often safer and equally effective. Ancestor veneration—regular offerings, memory-keeping, storytelling—builds the relationship without the high-voltage risk of evocation. Mediumship under trained supervision provides communication without the binding oath. Psychopomp work (guiding the dead onward) serves the dead rather than using them. Cemetery walking—mindful perambulation among graves with offerings of water and coin—opens the channel gently. These are not lesser practices; they are different voltages. Bifrons is the high-tension line. Most practitioners need household current.

If you must use the high-tension line, the ethical safeguards are contractual specificity and temporal limitation. Define the operation in writing: "Bifrons, move the influence of [specific ancestor] from [specific pattern] to [specific resolution] by [date]. I will perform [specific integration actions] within [timeframe]." Burn the contract after the operation closes. This prevents scope creep—the tendency of necromantic work to expand beyond its original mandate. The dead are patient. They have nothing but time. The living must enforce boundaries.

Comparisons with Other Death-Adjacent Spirits

Bifrons is often confused with Bune (Duke, 26th spirit) and Balam (King, 51st spirit), both of whom have death-adjacent portfolios. The distinctions are operational, not hierarchical. Bune governs the wealth of the dead—necromantic prosperity, the riches buried with ancestors, the financial settlements of estates. He moves money and eloquence. His current is Mercurial-Venusian: fluid, negotiable, transactional. Bifrons moves bodies and patterns. His current is Saturnian: structural, slow, irreversible. Use Bune when you need the estate settled. Use Bifrons when you need the ghost that haunts the estate exorcised.

Balam (sometimes spelled Balaam) appears as a three-headed king—man, bull, ram—riding a bear, with a hawk on his fist. His portfolio is invisibility, wit, and the "true answers" to past, present, and future. He is a divinatory king. His death-adjacency comes through the bear (chthonic) and the hawk (solar vision into the underworld). Balam tells you what happened. Bifrons lets you change what happened's consequences. Balam is the diagnostician; Bifrons is the surgeon. In a full necromantic protocol, you might call Balam for the reading, then Bifrons for the operation.

Other spirits worth distinguishing: Murmur (Duke, 54th) teaches philosophy and necromancy from the perspective of the dead themselves—he is the voice of the dead. Bifrons is the hand that moves them. Andrealphus (Marquis, 65th) transforms men into birds—metaphorically, he shifts consciousness between forms. His death-work is shapeshifting, not relocation. Phenex (Marquis, 37th) sings the sciences and hopes for return to the seventh throne after 1,200 years—he is the penitent dead. Bifrons has no penitence. He is the functionary. He does the job.

Historical Grimoire Context

The textual history of Bifrons traces from Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1577) through the Ars Goetia (17th century) to the modern editions of Mathers/Crowley (1904) and Peterson (2001). In Weyer, he is "Bifrous," a President and Earl with 6 legions, teaching astronomy, geometry, and the virtues of herbs and stones, moving bodies, and making candles under which they are buried. The Ars Goetia adds the two-headed monster appearance and the transformation into a man. The core data is stable across centuries—rare for grimoire spirits, who often mutate significantly between manuscripts.

This stability suggests Bifrons represents a functional archetype rather than a literary invention. The necromancer who moves bodies is a cross-cultural constant: the Greek psychopompos, the Egyptian Anubis, the Aztec Mictlantecuhtli, the Vodou Baron Samedi. Bifrons is the Goetic interface to that archetype—optimized for the Western ceremonial magician's toolkit. The six legions correspond to the six directions (four cardinal, up, down) or the six days of creation before the Sabbath rest. In either reading, the number signals completeness of spatial-temporal authority.

Modern editors have introduced errors. Some editions list him as the 47th spirit; the correct enumeration is 46th. Some give him 26 legions (confusing him with Bune). The Mathers/Crowley edition introduced the "fiery king" description that does not appear in Weyer—possibly a conflation with another spirit's manifestation. The Peterson critical edition is currently the most reliable. For practical work, I recommend cross-referencing at least two editions and testing the sigil from each. The version that produces consistent results is the correct one for your current.
