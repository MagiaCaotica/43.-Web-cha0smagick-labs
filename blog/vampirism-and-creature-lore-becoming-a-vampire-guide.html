Vampirism and Creature Lore

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

The vampire most people picture is a literary invention with a recent birthday, assembled across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, out of several older and quite separate traditions. Once that is clear, most arguments about vampirism become much easier, because they are arguments about different objects. Is the vampire real? Which vampire? The one in the folklore, the one in the novels, the one in modern esoteric practice, or the one that people mean when they say they feel drained by someone? This is a first route in: where the image came from, what the sources say, what modern practice borrows, and where the genuine risks sit. It is not a how-to, and it does not assume any interest in becoming anything.

When the vampire was invented, roughly

There is no single ancient vampire and no source text that contains one. The creature that emerged in eighteenth-century Europe is a composite, assembled by writers working with older material: the revenant of Slavic folklore, the strigoi with its grip on the living, the undead burial traditions of central Europe, the vampire bat of the Americas arriving later, and a set of literary conventions about aristocratic sexuality that belong more to the novel than to any tradition.

What changed in that period was the framing rather than the fear. Pre-modern vampire accounts were largely about disease, death and social disorder, often about a corpse that would not decompose and a village that needed to bury it properly. The eighteenth century made the vampire a person, a seducer, an aristocrat, and that version has been enormously more influential than anything that came before it. Almost every modern occult treatment is dealing with the literary vampire rather than the folk one.

The pieces it was assembled from

The folk material was never one story, and the differences matter. Some traditions describe a corpse returning because the burial was wrong, which makes the remedy practical and communal. Others describe a living person who has become something else, which makes it a question of community and consent. Others describe an outside agent drawing on a victim, which is closer to the energy-work framing that modern practice uses. A practitioner who says the vampire is a metaphor for exhaustion is drawing on a real strand, just not the one the films use.

  • The Slavic revenant, a corpse that returns and drains the living rather than haunts a place.
  • The strigoi and the notions of an external agent acting on a victim's own vitality.
  • Central European burial anxieties, including the corpse that would not stay put.
  • The vampire bat, known from the sixteenth century and folded into the image by name.
  • The aristocratic seducer of Gothic fiction, which contributed temperament rather than folklore.

What the sources say about the living

The recurring harms in folk accounts are not usually the bite. They are the drain: a slow weakening attributed to something taking from you, the household energy depleted, the livestock failing, the mood of a place souring. This is a picture of a community-level problem with a body attached to it, and it is easily read as a description of illness, of social dysfunction, or of a person whose presence reliably makes things worse.

That last reading is the one to sit with. Almost everyone has been the person in the room who seemed to drain it, usually without meaning to. The vampire legend externalises exactly that experience, and the literary version then gives it an elegant shape, a beautiful face and a sexual charge. Popular fiction about vampires is largely about the allure of being the thing that takes rather than the thing that gives, and recognising that is more useful than arguing about whether it is real.

The Liber Lvpinux problem

The Book of Werewolf is a short grimoire attributed to Remigius, and online occult content frequently claims it contains a werewolf transformation ritual. It does not. The text is about lycanthropy, about the condition of a person believed changed into an animal, and it is interested in remedies, cures and the recognition of the condition. Nothing in it transforms anybody into a wolf.

This is worth correcting explicitly because the error is so widespread and because it travels with an implicit claim about the age of the material. A great deal of what circulates as ancient working technique is a modern assembly that borrows the authority of a genuine source and then misreports what it says. The Liber Lvpinux is a real and interesting document. It is not what a lot of the material using its name claims it is.

Where modern practice gets its imagery

Vampiric energy work borrows three things and they are worth separating. The first is the life energy motif, a general idea that some people draw on others, which belongs to a much wider tradition of energy practice rather than to vampires specifically. The second is the transfer gesture, which in most systems means a boundary is crossed and something is taken, which is why consent questions attach to it so readily.

The third is the identity transformation, which is a separate practice and the one that carries documented psychological risk. The framing of becoming something other than what you were, a second self with a nature of its own, is not metaphorically neutral. For most people it is a useful image. For some people, particularly those with a history of dissociation or of psychotic vulnerability, it is a well-documented pathway to difficulty, and the literature on that is not speculative.

What becoming one would actually mean

Honestly, the tradition does not offer a clear answer, which is itself informative. The folk vampire became a vampire involuntarily, through a bite or a burial, and the modern occult version offers initiation as a deliberate choice. Those are incompatible. The chosen version is a nineteenth-century development built on the older involuntary one, and it inherits the older one's content while reversing its ethics, which is a difficult thing to do coherently.

So the honest answer to whether you can become one is that the tradition does not specify a process anyone can follow, and the parts of it that do specify a process are about avoiding the condition rather than inducing it. What you can do is work with the imagery as imagery, and the distinction is not a legalistic dodge. It changes what you are doing from a claim about your nature to a practice about your attention, and only one of those is testable.

The identity risk, named carefully

The relevant literature concerns identity-absorbing practices: techniques in which a person takes on a new identity, new name or new body, and the boundary between the constructed identity and the person becomes hard to locate. Dissociative symptoms, loss of access to ordinary memories, and the increasing difficulty of evaluating your own interpretation are the signals that matter. This is not exotic risk and it is not limited to occult practice; the same pattern appears in some disordered eating and some severe identity-affecting conditions.

The reason occult material is a poor place to meet this is that the practice is built to make the signal feel meaningful. A morning where you cannot remember last night is data. In a framework where dreams and omens are real, it is a message, and the interpretation is always available. If you are going to work anywhere near this territory, decide in advance what you will count as evidence that you should stop, and write it down while you are still able to think clearly.

Why blood work is more dangerous than it looks

The main practical hazard in this material is not supernatural. Blood has been treated as sacrificial in a great many traditions and it carries a specific symbolic charge that makes it an unusually powerful focus for attention. Someone performing a blood rite while already primed to feel drained is doing something considerably more effective on themselves than on anything outside them.

There is also an obvious physical dimension, though it is rarely the real concern. More interesting is the way symbolic sacrifice functions psychologically. It gives a person a way of signalling that something has been given up, which is a way of making a change feel real, and it can be used either to strengthen commitment or to postpone a decision indefinitely by performing the sacrifice instead of the act. If you are drawn to this material, that second use is the one to watch for in yourself.

The consent question

Any practice framed around taking something from another person needs a clear position about permission, and most material on this does not have one. This is not an abstract point. There is a real and documented population of people who have been harmed by practices performed on them without consent, usually by partners or family members who found the symbolism somewhere and treated it as licence.

The workable standard is straightforward and does not require renouncing the imagery. A person who has not agreed to a practice, in particular one involving their body, their blood or their attention, is not available as a resource. A person who has agreed and can withdraw that agreement is. Anything else is not a form of working, it is an infringement, and the fact that it can be framed in symbolic terms does not change what it is.

Separating the aesthetic from the claim

A great deal of popular vampire material is aesthetic rather than doctrinal. Dark romantic imagery, a particular kind of landscape, an aristocratic persona, a costume. None of that requires a claim about the supernatural and none of it is the same as a practice. People who are drawn to the aesthetic and assume they are thereby committed to the claim often find themselves defending positions they did not choose.

The reverse error is just as common, in the other direction. The material is treated as entirely metaphorical by people who have never looked at what the sources say, and then the sources are dismissed as nonsense. The folk accounts are informative about a particular social experience even if they are wrong about anything supernatural, and reading them that way costs nothing and produces more than either extreme offers.

A reasonable way to work with the material

The first item is the important one and it takes thirty seconds. Most difficulty with this material comes from a person holding an aesthetic preference and a doctrinal claim at once and not noticing the seam. Deciding which one you are working with removes the seam, and the rest of the sequence is ordinary good practice that applies to any aim worth testing.

The review step is what keeps the material from accumulating. Anything that has produced nothing across a fair number of attempts is telling you something, and the most common thing it is telling you is that the imagery was more interesting than the practice. That is a fine thing to have discovered, and much cheaper to find out than most people find out.

  1. Decide whether you are treating this as imagery or as claim, and write that decision down.
  2. If imagery, keep the practice about your own attention and stop there.
  3. Choose one focus and give it a fixed duration you can count.
  4. Record the outcome the same day, in a form another person could check.
  5. Review after a set number of workings, and drop anything that produced nothing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you become a vampire?

The tradition does not specify a process anyone can follow, which is worth noticing as a fact about the tradition rather than as a refusal. The folk vampire became one involuntarily, and the modern occult version reverses that while keeping the content, which does not produce a coherent method. What you can do is work with the imagery as imagery, and that is a real practice rather than a consolation prize, because the imagery is about attention and attention is workable.

Is vampirism practice harmful?

The serious documented risks come from identity-absorbing work rather than from the vampire imagery itself, and they matter particularly for anyone with a history of dissociation or psychotic vulnerability. The second real hazard is consent. Practices framed around drawing on another person have done real damage, usually when performed on someone who had not agreed. Frame the work as being about your own attention and both problems largely disappear.

Does the Book of Werewolf contain a transformation ritual?

No, and the claim appears repeatedly in online occult content. The Liber Lvpinux concerns lycanthropy as a condition and is interested in recognition and remedies, not in inducing it. Nothing in the text transforms anyone into a wolf. It is a real and interesting document and the persistent misdescription of it is a good example of how much modern working material borrows the authority of a genuine source while reporting something else entirely.

What does a one-time purchase get me for working with this material?

Very little, and the reason is worth stating plainly. The sources are public and the imagery is free, so a paid tool can only be offering organisation or atmosphere. Organisation is worth having if you keep a log of what you worked on and what happened, which is hard to do with a grimoire and easy to do with a database. Atmosphere is the thing to be careful about, since a tool that supplies dramatic imagery for a practice you have not defined is helping you avoid defining it.

Is any of this worth doing seriously?

Yes, and the seriousness is in the method rather than the theme. The folk accounts are real documents about a real social experience, the Gothic version is a real document about what a particular century found thrilling, and your own response to the material is real data about your attention. None of that requires the creature to exist, and the people who work with it most carefully are generally the ones who have stopped insisting that it does.
