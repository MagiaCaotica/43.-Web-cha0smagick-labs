Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

I have run this introduction more times than I would like to admit, mostly to friends, occasionally to strangers who wanted to know whether it was real. The answers changed less than I expected, and the questions stayed remarkably fixed. People arrive wanting to know whether they must believe, whether it is dangerous, and what they need to buy. Those three questions are good questions. The answers are short, and the second one is the one nobody likes. What follows is a distillation of the version I give now: where the thing came from, what the fundamentals actually consist of, the three ways beginners get stuck, and the minimum kit. It is field notes rather than a syllabus, which means it skips the parts that are fun to write and keeps the parts that changed my own practice.

The Basics Are Not a Subject

The thing newcomers expect is a body of doctrine with a lineage behind it. There is not much of one. Chaos magic as a named current shows up in the early 1970s, and the founding of the OTO in 1976 is usually taken as a starting point because it is the earliest dated institutional marker anybody can point to.

What came before that was mostly individual: a group of people in Britain who had read Crowley, who had read Spare, who had read Austin Spare's sigil papers, and who were independently arriving at the same unglamorous conclusion that the interesting part of magic is the part you can run yourself and check. That is the actual inheritance. Not a lineage of masters. A habit of mind.

So the fundamentals are a habit more than a curriculum. Once you know what the habit is, most of the reading is optional.

Two Books and a Name

Two texts do most of the work of making the thing legible. Liber Null and psi, by Peter Carroll, came out in 1977 and is short, cheap, and direct about experimental method. Liber Kaos, by Crowley, is older in framing and much less usable as an instruction manual, but it establishes that the experiment rather than the doctrine is the point.

The name is worth a moment because it was chosen. Chaos magic plays on the obvious phrase, and it also reaches for the Chaos enthroned in The Book of the Law, which the early writers took as a deliberate affiliation rather than a flattering comparison. Crowley is invoked here mostly to be disagreed with, which is a form of respect that gets misunderstood constantly by people looking for a founding father.

None of that matters much for practice. The books matter because they are readable, and the name matters because it is unfashionable enough that nobody claims it.

The Testing Habit Is the Product

Strip away the sigils, the statements of intent, the candle, the timing. What this current left behind that lasted is a change in what counts as respectable: testing a claim instead of inheriting it. That is a procedural shift, not a metaphysical one, and it is the only part I would defend as an actual advance.

Practically it looks like this: you write down what you expect, you do something, you record what happened, and you come back later with the record. Almost nobody does the fourth step, which is why the current's reputation for self-deception is partly deserved. The method is not protective on its own. It only works if you keep the log and actually read it.

Any system that forbids you from writing down your results has already taken something from you. That is a reasonable screening criterion for anything you are invited to follow.

Three Failure Modes, In Order

Fixation comes first. A sigil is a compressed intention and it works by being a thing you can stop thinking about. People attach their entire sense of progress to one glyph, then check it daily, and the checking destroys the mechanism. The interesting part of sigil work is the period when you have genuinely forgotten it.

Isolation comes second, and it comes packaged as exclusivity. The people who are getting somewhere are the ones still talking to people who are not doing this. The people who are not are the ones who read increasingly specialised material with decreasing contact with anyone. Nothing about the practice requires that and everything about the personality makes it easy.

Sleep disruption comes third and is the most physical. Late nights, obsessive session planning, and an overstimulated imagination before bed will cost you, and the bill arrives as poor sleep rather than as a lesson. Anyone selling a practice that reliably shortens your sleep for months is selling you something.

The Minimum Kit

One intention, stated as an action rather than a state. Not to be happy. To send the invoice. Not to be confident. To ask for the extension by Thursday. The distinction sounds pedantic and it is the single most useful thing in the introduction.

Its sigil, reduced however you like. A pen and paper are enough. Drawing by hand and generating one are the same operation with different amounts of care attached, and neither is more legitimate.

A private hour. Not a temple, not an altar, not a dedicated room. An hour where nobody will ask you what you are doing. The frequency of a session matters far less than whether it is repeatable, and repeatability is nearly impossible in a room you use for other things.

That is the whole shopping list. Everything else is optional or decorative, including most of what gets sold.

The Thing About Belief

Belief is a tool here rather than a precondition. You are not asked to believe that a glyph moves money. You are asked to be able to hold an intention clearly enough to encode it and then let it out of your hands, which is a different capacity and one most people find much harder than it sounds.

This is where most explanations of the practice go wrong, because they are written for someone who already wants to believe. For that reader the question is never whether. For everyone else the question is whether anything happens, and the honest position is that attention, expectation, and follow-through do measurable work on real outcomes while nothing in the record supports a mechanism that reaches outside the practitioner.

You can practise seriously with that position held. Plenty of people do.

What I Stopped Teaching

I stopped teaching correspondences as a first step. They are genuinely useful later and they are a terrible doorway, because they give a beginner the impression that the practice consists of getting tables right. Nothing in the early sessions depends on knowing that a particular number belongs to a particular planet.

I stopped teaching long initiatory sequences. An hour of doing one small thing and writing it down teaches more than a weekend of ritual performed without a single note.

And I stopped treating the debate about whether the current owes anything to Thelema as a real disagreement. It is a question about credit, and credit does not change what a beginner does on a Tuesday evening. The practice is short enough to start before you have settled it, and starting before you have settled it is the whole argument.

A Diagnostic

If your sessions produce a feeling of having done something, and no record afterwards, you have a spiritual practice and not a working one. Both are fine. They are just not the same thing, and confusing them is how people end up eighteen months in with a notebook full of feelings and no idea whether any of it works.

If you have records and no results, you may be running the wrong experiment, or you may be running a real one too small to see. Most early results are too small to see. That is not a reason to stop; it is a reason to stop checking daily and to decide in advance how long you will run before you decide anything.

If you have records, results you did not expect, and a clear account of what you did differently, you are doing the thing. Everything else is preparation for that, and the preparation is short.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is belief a precondition for practising magic?

No, and the requirement would be self-defeating anyway, because belief on demand is not belief. What the method does need is the ability to state an intention precisely and then release it, which is a skill you can practise whether or not you think anything is listening. Plenty of people run a serious practice while being entirely unsure what, if anything, is doing the work.

Is chaos magic dangerous?

The practice itself is not, and the risks are ordinary ones with occult packaging. Fixation on a single glyph, gradual withdrawal from people who are not involved, and sleep disruption show up reliably and all three are signs that the framing has taken over from the activity. A practice that makes you less connected to your life, less rested, or more convinced that your own reactions are supernatural is the wrong practice.

What do I need to start?

An intention phrased as something you will do, its sigil, and an hour you can protect. A pen, paper, and a private room are genuinely sufficient, and the expensive equipment sold under this name is optional. If you want help building the statement, the sigil, and the timing of a first session, a structured checklist covers it in about twenty minutes.

How long before I see anything?

Longer than most people wait, and there is no good answer because the early experiments are usually too small to see. What is measurable inside a few months is whether you have built the habit: sessions that repeat, notes that accumulate, and a growing sense of which of your own actions actually move the situation. Outcomes arrive on a different schedule than habit does, and the two get confused constantly.

Do I need to belong to an order or tradition?

No, and the lack of a gate is a design feature rather than a gap. The original writers were reacting to exactly the gatekeeping that makes initiatory systems unworkable for most people. You can practise this alone with books and a notebook, and you can practise it inside a lodge or an order if you prefer, and the only thing that genuinely matters is whether the people around you are checking results or accumulating status.
