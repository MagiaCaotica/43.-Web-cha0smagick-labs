How to Read Tarot Cards: A Rider-Waite Method

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

Most people who are stuck with tarot are stuck for the same reason: they learned the card meanings as a list of definitions instead of as a set of pictures. The Rider-Waite deck was published in 1909 with the imagery designed to carry the meanings, and it is the first widely used teaching deck rather than a codex with a lookup table. That design decision is why it dominates study traditions, and it means the deck can be learned rather than memorised. What follows is a working order of study, a reading method that survives contact with a difficult question, and an account of the parts that trip up beginners: the courts, the reversals, and the habit of asking questions too vague to answer. It is written for someone who has just picked up a deck and found the book confusing.

A Deck Built to Be Taught

The Rider-Waite-Smith deck appeared in 1909, designed by A. E. Waite for the Grand Orient Society and illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith. Its importance is structural rather than mystical. The imagery was chosen so that a reader could decode meaning from the picture, and every figure carries a tool, a posture, a number or an animal that is doing something.

Earlier decks were largely numbered suits with a small number of face cards, and their meanings lived in a book. Waite expanded the court from five figures to four and gave the Major Arcana a coherent iconographic sequence, which is what made independent study possible. Before that deck, tarot reading required a teacher. After it, you could learn alone.

So the primary skill is not memorising. It is describing what you see, out loud, without interpretation attached. The Fool is stepping off a cliff with a small dog, and the sun is behind him. He is carrying a stick over his shoulder with a bundle tied to it. The sky is about to clear. None of that is yet a meaning. Doing the description first is what keeps interpretation from arriving pre-loaded.

Learning the Major Arcana in Order

Start with the twenty-two Major Arcana and learn them in sequence rather than alphabetically. The order is a journey structure, and skipping ahead loses the internal logic that makes the deck learnable in a couple of months.

Begin with the first three because they establish the whole arc: the Fool at zero, the Magician at one, the High Priestess at two. Then move through the elemental suits in order, since the suits map onto the four elements, and then finish with the world card at the end. Studying the majors as a sequence, in pairs, works better than one at a time, because the tension between adjacent cards is where most of the insight lives.

A workable pace is three or four cards a week with a written note on each: what the figure is doing, one object that carries meaning, and one thing you are unsure about. After twenty-two weeks you have a deck you can read. The people who fail at this are the ones who try to memorise every dictionary entry in week one and abandon the practice in week three.

Suits as Patterns Rather Than Meanings

The suits are the working part of the deck, and they are best learned as patterns rather than as lists. Cups carry water, emotion, relationship and the inner life. Wands carry fire, will, conflict and creative drive. Swords carry air, thought, language and injury. Pentacles carry earth, body, money and work.

The single most useful step is to learn the elemental emphasis of each suit as a default reading position, then let the specific card modify it. A page of Pentacles is a small, concrete, material beginning. A nine of Swords is a mind in trouble at three in the morning, regardless of what the question was about.

Because suits are families, a spread containing three cards from one suit is telling you something coherent. Three Cups in a three-card spread is a social situation, not three separate points. Reading position by position before you read card by card is what makes that visible, and it is the order beginners should use for their first several hundred readings.

  • Cups: water, emotion, relationship, the inner life
  • Wands: fire, will, conflict, creative drive
  • Swords: air, thought, language, injury
  • Pentacles: earth, body, money, work

Why the Courts Show Attendants Facing You

In the Rider-Waite courts, the attendants are arranged facing the viewer's position. That compositional choice is the entire basis for the convention that court cards describe people in a situation rather than events happening to them, and it is why the courts work so well for practical questions.

A Page of Pentacles is a person who has just started something. A Queen of Swords is someone direct and boundary-setting. A King of Cups is emotionally regulated, sometimes to the point of being unreachable. Read as a description of the energy in a position, a court card answers a much more useful question than a Major Arcana does.

The practical move is to use courts for the people in the situation and Major Arcana for the forces shaping it. If the question is about a job and a King of Pentacles shows up in the outcome position, that is a description of a person or a mode of operating, and it points to a concrete approach rather than to an abstraction.

Position First, Card Second

Almost every beginner reads card by card: card one means this, card two means that, card three means the other thing. It produces a list of three unrelated sentences and no conclusion. Reading position first means naming what each position answers before you look at the card, and then letting the card answer that question and not the one you expected.

In a three-card spread the positions are situation, obstacle and direction. Those names constrain interpretation hard, and the constraint is the point. A Tower as obstacle and a Tower as direction are opposite readings, and the position is the only thing that tells them apart.

Two habits support this. Say the position name out loud before you describe the card, so your description is aimed rather than free-floating. And write the reading down immediately, because the version you remember two days later has been silently rewritten toward a conclusion you already wanted.

Three Cards Is Enough for Almost Everything

The three-card spread is easy to interpret and hard to over-interpret, and those are the two properties you want. Situation, obstacle, direction answers most practical questions without demanding a memorised list of a hundred positions.

The Celtic Cross is a genuinely excellent ten-card spread, and it is also the most common source of beginner paralysis, because ten cards generate roughly ten possible narratives and picking one requires a confidence you have not built yet. Use it once you are comfortable with three cards and you already know the meanings well enough to stop seeing them.

More cards is not more information. Each extra card multiplies the interpretive branching while adding little that a better question would not have supplied. If a reading feels overloaded, the fix is almost always a sharper question, not a smaller spread.

Reversals Without the Dread Framing

A reversed card is a card turned upside down. It can mean blocked, internalised, inverted, delayed or excess, and which reading fits depends on the card, the position and the question. It does not inherently mean danger, and the popular framing of reversals as ominous is a modern addition rather than a feature of the tradition.

Start by treating reversals as a change of direction on the same axis. The upright Empress is abundance in expression; reversed, it is abundance held back or applied inwardly. Upright Swords is clarity through thought; reversed, it is the same clarity turned against you or delayed. One step of rotation produces most of the useful readings.

A defensible practice is to read the whole spread upright first, note what is left unexplained, and only then look at the reversals. That ordering keeps the reversal from acting as a dramatic intensifier on an otherwise flat reading, which is what happens when beginners treat every reversed card as a warning.

Questions You Can Actually Answer With a Card

The single most common reason a tarot reading fails is the question. Be happy cannot be worked with. Find out if he loves me cannot be worked with. Three client conversations this week can. A question has to be specific enough that an answer changes what you do next.

A workable question names a situation, a timeframe and an action. What is blocking the launch of my service in the next thirty days. Whether to take the second offer before the deadline on Friday. Whether the delay on the invoice is a bookkeeping problem or a relationship problem. Those have answers you can act on, and the cards are far more useful when they are.

Write the question down before you shuffle, keep a journal of readings with the question, the spread and your interpretation, and go back and check your own track record after a few months. A tarot journal is a record of your interpretation habits, and most readers discover that the same pattern of cards keeps getting misread in the same way, which is far more useful information than any single reading produced.

  1. Name the situation, the timeframe and the action in the question
  2. Say the position name aloud before describing the card
  3. Read the spread upright first, then check the reversals
  4. Write the question and your reading down on the same page
  5. Re-read your own entries after a few months to find your habits

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Rider-Waite deck a real fortune-telling deck?

It is primarily a teaching deck, and that is exactly why it dominates study traditions. The imagery was designed to encode the meanings so a reader could learn without a teacher, which is what happened. Commercial framing as a tool of fortune is part of the tradition and was deliberate, so owning a deck for that purpose is ordinary rather than naive. The deck works fine as a reflective tool and as a structured way to think through a decision.

How many cards should I learn first?

The twenty-two Major Arcana, then the four suits as patterns, then the courts. Start with the majors in sequence rather than in random order, because the sequence is itself a structure and it is what makes the deck learnable in a couple of months. Three or four cards a week with one written note each is a pace most people sustain. Learning all seventy-eight in a fortnight is the most common route to abandoning the practice.

What is the best beginner spread?

Three cards: situation, obstacle and direction. It is easy to interpret and hard to over-interpret, and those are the properties that matter when you are still learning. The Celtic Cross is a genuinely excellent ten-card spread, but ten cards multiply the interpretive branching and most beginners end up choosing a narrative rather than reading one. Save it until the three-card reading feels automatic.

What does a reversed card actually mean?

Treat it as a change of direction on the same axis rather than as a warning. Upright, a card expresses its energy outward; reversed, that same energy turns inward, blocks, delays or intensifies depending on the card. Reading the whole spread upright first and only then checking the reversals keeps them from acting as a dramatic intensifier on an otherwise flat reading, which is what happens when every reversal gets treated as a caution.

What does buying an application add to learning a deck?

The meanings are in any library book and the whole method is free. What a paid application actually buys is the lookup layer: every card meaning and reversal on tap while your hands are busy, shuffled spreads with no memorisation required, and saved readings with your question attached so you can look back at your own track record. That last one is the reason to pay, because a tarot journal is where you discover your own interpretation habits. One payment, kept forever, suits a deck you will use for years better than rent on the same lookup.
