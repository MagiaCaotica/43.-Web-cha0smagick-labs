FÍSICA CUÁNTICA

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

The quantum vocabulary that appears in occult writing did not travel with its definitions attached. Words like superposition, observation, and entanglement were coined in a specific technical context, and they are being used now to describe things they were never defined to describe. That is not automatically a mistake. It becomes a mistake when the transfer is presented as evidence, and it very frequently is. The useful move is to separate the two things that are going on: there is a real and measurable effect that ritual produces, and there is a borrowed vocabulary that does not describe that effect. The first is well supported. The second does nothing for it.

A word imported from another field

Superposition is the clean example. In the context where it was coined, it describes a quantum system that is in a state describable as a combination of alternatives until it is measured. The word is precise, technical, and tied to a mathematical formalism that makes specific predictions. None of that travels with the ordinary use.

In occult writing, the word is usually doing something much looser. It gets attached to the idea of holding two possibilities at once, or of a symbol meaning more than one thing, or of an intention that has not yet chosen its object. Those are ordinary human mental activities and they may well be real, but calling them superposition tells you nothing you did not already know and implies a formal machinery that has not been shown to apply. The word has been borrowed without the theory, and the theory is where the content was.

What superposition means where it was coined

The technical meaning involves a system whose state is represented as a combination of basis states, with the coefficients carrying observable magnitudes. A measurement yields one outcome with probabilities given by the squared magnitudes. The formalism makes predictions that were confirmed with extraordinary accuracy, and the puzzle is not that the predictions failed but that they succeed.

None of this is a metaphor for indecision. A person considering two options is not in a superposition, and the resemblance between the situations is superficial. The quantum case is unusual because the alternative states are not classical alternatives that will turn out to have been one of them all along. That is precisely the part that would be needed to make the analogy load, and it is exactly the part the metaphor leaves out.

What the same word is doing in a ritual claim

In a ritual context the word is generally used to make a claim about the practitioner. Holding a sigil while performing an act of will is described as putting intention into superposition, which usually means holding two things in mind at once, or holding a symbolic form alongside a verbal one. Whether or not that is a real psychological operation, it is a claim about the practitioner's attention.

And that claim is not strengthened by the name. You could describe the same operation without the word, in plain language, and it would be the same operation. The word is doing rhetorical work rather than explanatory work, and the test is simple. Delete the technical terms and see whether the sentence becomes less informative. Usually it does not, and a claim that loses nothing when the jargon is removed never had technical content to begin with.

Where the import breaks

The break is at the level of scale and of the relationship between observer and system. The formalism applies to systems whose behaviour is governed by that framework, and it makes no claims about ordinary human intention. Stretching it to cover symbolic action requires an argument that the observer is a quantum measurement, and nobody has produced that argument in a form that survives contact with the physics.

There is a second break, and it is the more interesting one. Quantum effects are not amplified by wanting them more. The formalism is indifferent to the state of the person running the experiment, and that indifference is a load-bearing feature rather than a limitation. Anything that scales with the strength of a desire is not behaving like the thing being borrowed, and the folk literature is full of claims that scale precisely with desire.

The part of the mechanism that is real

Strip out the borrowed vocabulary and there is a genuine finding underneath. Ritual reliably produces measurable autonomic change. Heart rate variability drops, breathing pattern changes, muscle tension releases, and the whole set of physiological shifts that accompany attention becoming narrow and sustained shows up in people performing repetitive structured actions with a defined focus.

This is not a small effect and it is not mysterious. It is the ordinary physiology of focused attention, and it is one of the better-evidenced things about ritual practice. Whether you call it entrainment, arousal regulation, or a state shift does not change the measurement. It is real, it is measurable, and it explains a great deal about how practitioners feel during and after a working.

The part that needs no quantum anything

The autonomic finding is the whole of the well-evidenced part, and it requires none of the borrowed language. A person who sits still, holds a posture, performs a set sequence of gestures at a set time, and concentrates on a single defined intention is doing something that has been measured by people who work in psychophysiology, using equipment that has nothing mystical about it.

This is worth stating plainly because it sounds like a demotion and is not one. If the practice changes your heart rate variability and your breathing, then something has happened, and the description of that something in ordinary physiological terms is complete. You do not need a more impressive description. A practitioner whose ritual reliably produces a measurable state change has achieved something real, and the vocabulary they reach for afterwards is a separate question with no bearing on it.

Observation, and the sense the word carries

Observation is the second import and it is the one most people meet first. In the technical context, observation refers to a measurement interaction, and the famous difficulty is that the act of measuring is not cleanly separable from the system being measured. The problem is precise, technical, and about the relation between an apparatus and a system.

In occult usage, observation usually becomes attention, awareness, or the act of witnessing, and the load-bearing connotation is that awareness affects what is observed. As a description of attention this is unobjectionable. As an import it is misleading twice over, because it smuggles in the idea of a measurement collapse and then usually repurposes that collapse as a mechanism by which intention reaches the world. If you drop the technical sense, what remains is a description of the practitioner paying attention, which is where the actual effect lives.

Entanglement, and the property it names

Entanglement names a specific measurable property of a composite system: the state of the parts cannot be described independently, and the correlations between measurements on separated parts exceed what classical correlations allow. It is a real, tested, extraordinary property and it is a bad metaphor.

The reason it is a bad metaphor is that the property has no observational cousin in ordinary life at all. People are not entangled. Objects are not entangled in a way that produces the named correlation. The word gets used in occult writing to convey a sense of hidden connection between disparate things, which is a fine poetic effect and has nothing to do with the property. The honest move is to keep the word on its own side of the line, where it describes a physical fact about composite systems, and not bring it across to describe feelings.

The deck, and the arithmetic it teaches

Rhine's card-guessing work is the most useful object in this area, and not because its results support anything. It is useful because the design is a clean demonstration of what the arithmetic does to a claim. Five symbols, a deck, a guess, and a score out of twenty-five. Pure chance gives twenty percent.

Everything that has happened in the subsequent decades of research on this is a lesson about that number. Hit rates above twenty percent appear in some conditions and vanish in others, the interesting subset shrinks as controls tighten, and the methodological literature is largely a story of people calculating what a genuine twenty percent would look like in a small sample and not finding it. Nobody needs any physics at all to understand why. The denominator is where the whole argument lives, and it has never needed a quantum vocabulary to be understood.

What a ritual can plausibly be doing

Put the pieces together without the imports and you get a coherent and fairly modest account. A defined sequence of actions, performed at a set time, with a single named intention, produces a measurable change in the practitioner's autonomic state and a measurable change in the salience of the intention. That is enough to explain the intensity practitioners report, the sense of occasion, and the subjective feeling that something has been done.

It is also enough to explain the part of the practice that actually produces results, which is the follow-through. A state that has been narrowed by twenty minutes of structured attention, followed by a fortnight of ordinary behaviour with the intention held in view, is a procedure that can be expected to change what a person notices and does. Nothing in that account needs to reach outside the practitioner, and nothing in it is diminished by dropping the technical vocabulary.

The vocabulary worth giving up

The words worth giving up are the ones that make a claim sound more physical than it is. Superposition, observer effect, and entanglement in their ritual applications all fail the deletion test, and they fail it in a specific way: they promise a level of rigour that the claim does not have. That promise is a cost, because it raises the standard of proof while lowering the credibility.

The words worth keeping are the plain ones. Focus, sequence, timing, intention, attention, record, result. They are less exciting and they are the vocabulary in which the measurable findings are actually stated. A person who can describe their practice in these terms can be tested, and testing is what separates a technique from a story about a technique.

The sentence to keep

If you keep one sentence from this, keep this. The practice produces a real and measurable change in the practitioner's own state, and the vocabulary used to describe that change should be the vocabulary in which it was measured.

This is not a rejection of the quantum physics, which is well evidenced and largely irrelevant here. It is a rejection of a borrowing that adds nothing and costs credibility, and it is available to anyone who makes the deletion test. Write your practice down in plain terms, strip the technical words, and see whether the description gets worse. It does not get worse. It gets more accurate, which is a better outcome than sounding impressive.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does quantum physics support the idea that intention affects reality?

Not in the way the borrowing assumes. The formalism makes no claim about human intention, and the systems it describes are indifferent to how much anyone wants an outcome. The interesting observations about measurement are about the relation between an apparatus and a system, not about desire reaching outward, and the step from one to the other has not been made in a form that holds up.

What is the most solidly evidenced effect of ritual practice?

Measurable autonomic change, which is consistent and reproducible. Heart rate variability, breathing pattern, and muscle tension all shift during structured ritual performance, and the shifts are in the direction associated with narrowed attention and sustained arousal. This is a genuine finding about the practitioner's state, it has been measured with ordinary equipment, and it explains a great deal of what practitioners report.

Is superposition just a fancy word for indecision?

In its technical context, no, and the difference matters. The quantum case involves combinations of alternatives that are not classical alternatives waiting to be resolved, and the formalism makes precise predictions about the probabilities of outcomes. Indecision is not that. When the word is used for indecision it has been borrowed without its content, and the borrowing makes the claim sound more technical than it is.

Why do I keep hearing about observer effects in magic writing?

Probably because the word observation travels well and the technical sense does not. In its original context it means a measurement interaction, and the famous difficulty there is about whether measurement is cleanly separable from the system. What survives the trip into occult writing is usually just attention or awareness, which is an ordinary and unobjectionable thing, and the remaining suggestion that awareness reaches the world is added on the far side without support.

Does all this mean occult practice is not real?

No, and the question is malformed. It asks whether an account should include a particular vocabulary, when the useful question is what the practice does and whether that is measurable. A structured sequence with a named intention produces measurable changes in the practitioner's state and changes what they notice and do afterwards. That is real, it is documented, and it does not require the technical terms that are currently attached to it.

Put this into practice
