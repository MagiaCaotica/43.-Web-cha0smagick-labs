Wealth and Abundance

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 11 min read

The abundance material in this tradition is usually written as a series of steps, and the steps are useful, but they obscure the thing that makes the method interesting. A sigil is not a token you make and then use. It is a fixed point of attention that you built, and the value is entirely in the relationship between the object and the sentence it stands for. This piece takes a single sigil and follows it through, from choosing the sentence to deciding what happens when the window closes with nothing in it. No batches, no cycles, no variants. One target, one glyph, one charge, one log, one review. If the approach is right, doing it once properly teaches more than doing it twenty times in a scattered fashion, and if the approach is wrong the single version makes that obvious far faster.

Choosing one sigil, and why one

Almost every difficulty people have with this practice comes from attempting several at once. Two workings open simultaneously means the attention behind each is divided, the log becomes ambiguous, and the review cannot attribute an outcome to either. The tradition's advice to run one at a time is not ritual purity. It is the minimum requirement for the log to mean anything.

Choose the one with the clearest observable condition and the shortest distance from your present circumstances. Not the largest target, which is the usual instinct. The largest target in a first cycle produces a working you will abandon in week three, and an abandoned working teaches you nothing at all. A modest target you will actually complete produces a record you can extend, and the record is the only thing in this practice that compounds.

The sentence, and the four questions it must answer

Before reducing anything, the sentence has to survive four checks. Is it in the imperative, addressed to someone rather than wished at a situation. Does it name one outcome rather than a category. Could a person who did not believe in any of this evaluate it on a stated date. And is it written in terms you would be willing to put in front of somebody else.

The last check sounds odd and it is the one that catches the most trouble. A sentence you would not put in a log because it reads as grasping is usually a sentence aimed at the wrong thing, because the urgency in it is a sign that you want reassurance rather than a procedure. Rewrite until the log entry sounds like a business objective. That is a surprisingly good test and it costs a rewrite rather than a month.

The reduction, done three ways for comparison

Reduce the sentence three times, by three different methods, and put all three in front of you. Not to choose a favourite, though you may end up with one. To find out which reduction you actually use, because a method you have not tested produces a result you cannot repeat, and a sigil you cannot reproduce is a one-off drawing rather than a device.

The letter-reduction method gives you the most information, because you can see which letters survived and why, and you can regenerate the same glyph from the same sentence in a year. The geometric method gives you the least, and it is the one most software implements. A written abbreviation method gives something in between and is the fastest for a long sentence. Do all three, notice which one you reach for by reflex a month later, and use that one consistently from then on. The consistency is worth more than the choice.

Drawing the glyph, and what the hand contributes

Draw it slowly, in one continuous movement if your method allows it, and do not redraw it. The redrawing instinct is strong and it is worth resisting for a specific reason. Each version carries a slightly different concentration, and choosing between them means choosing on the basis of which one looks best, which tells you about your aesthetic preferences and nothing about the sentence. A crude first attempt that you commit to is a better working object than a polished fourth attempt you spent an hour choosing.

The hand does contribute something, and it is not a supernatural contribution. Producing a mark manually occupies attention in a way that clicking a button does not, and the physical act of committing a line to paper is a commitment device of the same family as writing the sentence. If you use a generator, then draw the resulting glyph yourself once, by hand, and charge from the drawing. That preserves the part of the method that does work.

Where the sigil is kept, and why that matters

Keep it somewhere specific, and choose the location before you charge it. A small box, a drawer that does not get tidied, the back of a book. What you are avoiding is the ordinary environment, because a sigil that sits on a desk among receipts is one you will handle without a deliberate moment, and handling without intention is exactly what the dismissal step is meant to prevent.

The tradition also uses the stored object as a physical reminder, checking it briefly at the start of each cycle. That is a legitimate and rather effective practice, and it is also the first place where the habit can turn into something you would rather not have. If you notice yourself opening the drawer more often than once a week, the object has stopped being a container and become an obsessive focus. Put it somewhere less convenient.

The charge, specified precisely enough to repeat

Lay the specification out in writing, because the tradition's charge instructions describe an internal state and states vary between people and between days. Four components, in order. You are aware of the sentence, not the picture. You are aware of the target as a thing that could be observed. You sustain that for about a minute. You stop. Anything longer is describing the same state in more words, and a description you cannot execute is not an instruction.

Write those four lines in the log immediately after, including how long it actually took, because the gap between the prescribed duration and the actual one is a variable that otherwise goes unrecorded. Across a dozen cycles, that number tells you more about the sustainability of the method than any other figure in the log, and it costs nothing to capture.

The dismissal, and what makes it final

The closing should be a single decisive action rather than a sequence, and this is a case where restraint is the technique. Put the glyph away. Take down whatever you set up. Leave. A dismissal with several parts creates opportunities to perform only some of it, and a partly performed dismissal leaves the working technically open, which is the state that produces the background hum of an unfinished sequence.

What the dismissal is for is not the object. It is the reallocation of attention. A working you have not closed continues to occupy a small amount of attention indefinitely, and that drain is easy to mistake for a general lack of concentration in the rest of your life. Fifteen seconds of decisive closing is a good trade and it is the most-skipped step in the entire method.

The derivatives, and whether they earn their trouble

A derivative is a later sigil carrying the same intent, sometimes with additions. They exist because a serious practice spanning years generates more sentences than one at a time, and the temptation is to have a small series running. This is a well-known route to a practice that feels busy and produces nothing evaluable, and the cost is higher than most people expect because every derivative divides the attention that the original was relying on.

The defensible version is narrow. A derivative is legitimate when a specified condition of the original has actually occurred, such as a date arriving without the observation, and the derivative addresses the new situation rather than restating the old aim. Everything else is avoidance of a review. If you have three or more active sigils, pick the oldest, finish its window, and let the other two lapse rather than carrying them forward.

Observing the window, with a decision rule

On the review date, before opening anything else, read the entry. Then look at the observation you wrote down. Then decide, in one of three categories: the condition was met, the condition was not met, or the condition cannot be determined from what was available. The third category is legitimate and most logs lack it entirely, and it exists because some observations require information you will not have.

Whichever category you choose, write it in the log before you start reasoning about what it means. The reasoning comes afterwards and is a separate entry. This two-step structure is what allows the log to remain evidence rather than becoming a narrative, and the difference matters the moment you go back over a year of entries looking for a pattern. A narrative you wrote afterwards will always find one.

When a result arrives before the window closes

An early result is a real result and should be logged as one, dated to the day you observed it. The temptation is to decide immediately that the method works and to move on to a bigger target immediately, and that sequence is where this practice usually starts producing the results people quote. The observation was met and the working is complete, but the record is only one row.

Write the entry, close the working properly, and keep the review date anyway. Sit with it for the remaining window without starting anything new. If the condition stays met, you have a stronger result than a timely one, and if it somehow reverses, you have learned something genuinely useful about the reliability of the observation, which is exactly the information a single early success would have hidden from you.

When the window closes with nothing in it

Write the null, state the date, and close the file. Then do not escalate. The whole apparatus of this genre is engineered to convert a null into an opportunity, and the mechanisms are recognisable: a new offering is now required, the intent needs to be written more strongly, a different entity should be asked, or the working needs to be repeated over a lunar cycle. Each of these is plausible-sounding and each of them moves you further from the only thing that would actually help, which is a log you can trust.

A null is cheap information. You have learned that the observation did not occur within the window, which is a fact about the world and about your capacity to act, and both are useful. If nulls begin to accumulate across several different targets and a plausible mechanism for each, that is a strong signal that the behaviour rather than the glyph is the variable, and at that point the practice is telling you something worth hearing.

Running it a second time, differently

Change exactly one thing. The most informative single change is the target, because a target that was achievable and one that was not will produce different outcomes and tell you something about the range of the method. The second most informative is the reduction method, which tests whether your own sense that one approach works better is real or is just familiarity.

Keep everything else identical, including the charge length, the storage location, the review interval and the logging fields. The instinct at the second cycle is to improve several things at once, and that instinct destroys the comparison, because afterwards there is no way to attribute the difference to any of them. Two comparable cycles are worth more than ten improved ones, and the reason is that the ten will teach you nothing you can carry forward.

What the sigil cannot do, stated plainly

It cannot move money. It cannot influence another person's decision, and any method that claims to is claiming something you cannot test and cannot ethically use, since the only way to evaluate it would be to coerce somebody. It will not resolve a financial problem that requires you to stop spending, and a sigil aimed at an income increase while the spending is unchanged will produce a null that is telling you something accurate.

It will not work while you are in a state you would describe as desperate, because the behaviour that produces results is unavailable in that state and the glyph is not going to supply it. And it will not do anything for somebody else, which is the limitation that applies to every working in this tradition and is the one most often quietly ignored. What it can do is make a vague intention into a specific commitment, put that commitment in front of you at a moment when a decision is available, and keep the record that tells you whether it was worth doing again.

The record that makes the second run possible

One entry, dated, containing five things: the sentence as written, the reduction method, the storage location, the charge duration as it actually was, and the observation with its date. That is the whole apparatus. Everything else in the tradition's documentation for this working is either refinement, tradition, or scaffolding for a structure you will eventually want to build once the first structure is stable.

Keep the entry in the same place you keep the glyph, and treat the pair as a single object. The physical association between the sentence and the page is doing real work even though it is entirely ordinary psychology, and the reason so much traditional practice involves keeping written records next to physical objects is probably this and nothing more. Nothing in the arrangement needs to be explained in order to work, which is either a relief or a problem depending on what you wanted.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a paid sigil generator change the sigil itself?

No, and this is worth stating plainly because the marketing often implies otherwise. A generator implements a reduction algorithm, and you can implement the same algorithm by hand in ten minutes. What changes is the tedium and the reproducibility, both of which are minor. If you buy one, buy it for the convenience of doing many, not because it produces a better glyph, and draw the result by hand before charging so that the manual part of the method stays in place.

Should I make more than one sigil for the same target?

Not while the first is still inside its window. Two sigils aimed at one target split the attention and make the log ambiguous, and the temptation to make a second is nearly always a response to impatience rather than to a reason. If the first produces nothing, a second with a different target teaches you more than a second aimed at the same one, because it separates the question from the method.

How long should I keep the glyph afterwards?

Long enough to be useful and then no longer. Keeping it does not preserve or accumulate anything, but a stored object that you look at frequently becomes a focus for the worry the practice is supposed to reduce. Once the review is written, put it somewhere you will not handle it, and if you find yourself opening that drawer more than once a week, move it to somewhere less convenient.

Does the charge need to happen at a particular hour?

No, and the constraint will cost you more than it returns. The stated hours matter to some lineages for reasons internal to those lineages, and a working that waits for the right hour is a working that sometimes does not happen at all. Pick a time you will reliably be free, keep the charge short, and record the actual duration. Consistency of recording is worth more here than consistency of timing.

What if I never look at the result?

Then you have a working with no evidence attached, which is worse than useless because it feels like it counted. Set the review date in the same sitting as the charge, put a calendar entry on the day, and treat the entry as a commitment you made to yourself in writing. Most people who skip the review did not skip it out of indifference. They skipped it because the anticipation was doing more for them than the answer would have been.
