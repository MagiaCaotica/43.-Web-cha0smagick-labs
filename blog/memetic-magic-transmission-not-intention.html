Memetic Magic: The One Chaos Practice With a Measurable Result

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Every other branch of this material asks you to believe that a small mark can move the world, and every one of those asks is unfalsifiable from the outside. Memetic magic is the exception, and that is why it deserves more attention than it gets. A meme has a reproduction rate, a half-life, and a carrier, and all three are countable. What makes a viral occult image spread is not the occult content, and collapsing those two facts into one is the commonest error in the genre. What the anonymous boards actually demonstrate is not that memes are magic. It is that the format is a vector and the payload is optional, which means the occult tradition can be carried by something nobody has to believe in order to reproduce. The useful question is not whether a meme changed reality. It is what a memetic engineer can design.

What memetic magic claims, stated precisely

A meme is an idea that reproduces by being copied rather than by being argued for. That is the whole definition, and it is the whole claim. In occult usage a memetic working adds one stipulation: you design the carrier rather than waiting for one, and you treat the design as the operation rather than the content.

The design space has four variables, and every memetic decision is a choice among them. The payload is what the idea is about. The carrier is the format it travels in, and this is the variable that matters most. The trigger is what makes a person reproduce it, usually a feeling that copying is the correct response. And the half-life is how long it stays in circulation before it needs a new trigger, which is the number almost nobody in this material talks about.

Why the carrier matters more than the payload

Two images carrying identical occult content can behave completely differently. One is copied, the other is ignored, and the difference is nearly always format: legibility at thumbnail size, whether the words are readable without zooming, whether the thing can be described in one sentence by someone who has never seen it.

This is why a sigil is such a good carrier and a poor payload. A sigil is a compact, visually distinctive, easily reproduced mark that carries an untranslatable private meaning. It is legible as a shape before it is legible as a symbol, which means it survives translation between people who share the meaning and people who do not. A sigil posted to a board full of people who do not believe in sigils still gets copied, and it keeps working, because the reproduction does not require comprehension.

A sigil posted with a paragraph of explanation does dramatically worse. The explanation is what makes it legible, and legibility to a specific audience is exactly what destroys the property. Every practitioner in this field has watched a beautiful sigil die because it was explained in the same post.

The measure you can actually take

Forget whether it worked. Count what happened. Over thirty days, a memetic working has one observable: how many times somebody else reproduced it without being asked, and how many of those reproductions came from people who had not seen the original.

That number is countable because the reproduction is a discrete event with a timestamp, and because the medium is public. Almost nothing else in occult practice gives you a free count like this. A sigil for money cannot be audited by a third party. A meme can, and the reason memes are the best-equipped branch of this material for beginners is that the practitioner is not the only one who can tell whether it worked.

Write the count down on day one, before the thing is posted, and write down what would count as success before you know what success looks like. Thirty days, three reproductions by people who did not see the original, is a reasonable target for a first attempt. Zero is common and is not a verdict on the practice, because distribution depends on factors the maker does not control.

The failure that looks like success: transmission with no behaviour change

A viral occult sigil is the most common outcome in this branch and the least useful one. It gets copied four hundred times and no single person who copied it did anything differently. The transmission was real and the practice was empty.

The reason is that the two are causally separate. A carrier spreads because it is easy to spread, and spreading is a property of the format. Behaviour change happens when the content reaches a person who is already at the decision point, and that almost never happens to someone who copied an image for the aesthetic.

The diagnostic is simple. Ask what the reproduction is for. If the honest answer is that it looked good, or that it was funny, or that it made the poster look interesting, then what you built is a sticker. Stickers are not worthless, and a practice built around producing stickers is a legitimate art practice, but it is not memetic magic and it will never tell you anything about whether intention affects outcomes.

Designing for a second generation

Most occult images are designed for the person who made them. The ones that travel are designed for the person who receives them, who will have no context, no explanation, and no reason to ask what it means.

Three properties separate a carrier that travels from one that does not, and all three are craft, not magic. It must be describable in a sentence by someone who does not understand it. It must reproduce correctly when someone screenshots it, which rules out anything that depends on fine detail at small size. And it must be repeatable, so that a second person can make their own version without the first person.

The third property is the one occult material almost always fails, and it fails for a specific reason. Traditional sigilisation requires that nobody else can make the same mark, because the mark's uniqueness is part of its identity. A good memetic carrier requires the opposite, that everybody can make the same mark, and that the mark still means what it meant to the person who designed it.

A four-week memetic working you can review

Week one, write the payload as a single sentence a stranger could act on. Not a feeling, not an atmosphere, an instruction. If the sentence cannot be acted on by someone who has never met you, the payload is decorative and you should change it before anything else.

Week two, make the carrier. One mark, no text in the image, legible at thumbnail size. If you find yourself wanting to add a caption, that is the signal that the carrier is not finished. The test is whether it reads correctly with the text cropped off.

Week three, post it once, in one place, and write the day down. Do not post it in five places, because a distribution that reaches five audiences tells you nothing about whether the thing travels. One post in one venue is the only comparison you can make against your own baseline.

Week four, count. How many reproductions, by whom, and whether anybody reported acting on the sentence. Write the three numbers and one sentence on what you think the carrier did. Write the sentence before you look for a reason, because a reason written afterwards will fit whatever you found.

  1. Write the payload as one actionable sentence a stranger could act on.
  2. Build the carrier with no text in the image, legible at thumbnail size.
  3. Post once, in one venue, and record the day.
  4. Count reproductions by people who never saw the original.
  5. Write your explanation before you go looking for one.

Where the anonymous material is right and where it is not

It is right about the mechanics. The boards that document these transmissions are doing careful empirical work on a phenomenon that is real, measurable, and largely unstudied. That is worth taking seriously and it is worth doing yourself.

It is wrong about the attribution, and the error is consistent. When an occult image changes behaviour, the attribution usually goes to the image, when the thing that changed behaviour was the sentence a person acted on. In practice the format delivered the message to a person and the message did the work, and the format took the credit because the format is what people can see and discuss.

It is also wrong about the secrecy, and this is the part worth pushing back on hardest. The tradition keeps hidden the things that are most likely to work, on the theory that common knowledge loses potency. Transmission says the opposite: potency in this branch is a function of how many people can carry it, and every hour spent on concealment is an hour the carrier is not in circulation.

What to buy, and what to refuse

Nothing. This branch needs no equipment, which makes it the cheapest entry point in the whole catalogue, and the tools that get sold for it are almost all sold for the adjacent practices.

Refuse anything marketed as a memetic generator that produces a unique mark per user. That design is directly hostile to the transmission property, and the marketing copy for it never mentions why. If you want a sigil engine, the useful version lets you set a fixed shape, and the ones that personalise per person are solving a different problem.

One subscription-free habit is worth more than any of them: a running count, kept in a plain note, of every time something of yours was reproduced by somebody else. That log is the only instrument in this branch that produces knowledge, and it costs nothing to run.

The question the branch keeps dodging

Every tradition that transmits has to answer one question: what happens when the meaning is lost and the carrier keeps circulating. A rosary without a rosary book is beads. A sigil without its statement is a shape. The anonymous material is full of shapes whose statements were never written down, moving through a population that does not know they are carrying anything.

The defensible position is that this is fine and even useful, because a carrier that outlives its payload becomes available for a new one. The indefensible position is claiming continuity, that an idea is still travelling when all that survives is a visual resemblance.

So the question is worth asking of your own material. If somebody copies your work in ten years and nobody knows what it meant, is that a success or a hollow one? Both answers are legitimate. The important part is deciding, and the deciding takes about a minute.

Five ways a memetic working goes wrong

Transmitting without a payload, which produces a sticker and then four hundred copies of a sticker. Designing a unique carrier, which is the traditional sigil instinct applied to a problem that needs the opposite. Never writing the sentence, so nothing can change anybody's behaviour and the only measurable output is applause. Distributing to five venues at once, which converts a test into an advertisement and leaves no baseline. And measuring belief instead of behaviour, which is the move that makes this branch look spiritual rather than observable, and it is the reason most accounts of memetic magic are unfalsifiable when they did not have to be.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is memetic magic real?

The transmission is real and measurable. Whether the design of a carrier influences anything beyond its own reproduction rate is an open question, and the honest position is that nobody has run a controlled version, because the field has no interest in controls. What you can do is run your own count, which is worth more than the opinions in the comments.

Why should a sigil be easy for other people to copy?

Because the mark has to travel before it can carry anything, and a mark nobody else can reproduce travels exactly one instance. This is the direct opposite of traditional sigilisation, where uniqueness carries part of the identity, and the tension between the two is the most interesting design problem in this branch.

Do memes prove that belief changes reality?

They prove that belief changes behaviour, which is not the same claim. A meme that reaches a person at the moment they are already deciding can tip that decision, and that is a large effect in the right context. It is not evidence about intentions and outcomes in general, and the most common overreach in this material is treating one as the other.

Is it worth paying for a memetic or sigil app?

Usually not. A sigil generator that produces a unique mark per user works against the transmission property, and a free online generator covers the mechanics completely. The one thing worth paying for is a sigil tool that lets you fix a shape and reuse it, and even that is a convenience rather than a requirement.

What is the half-life of a good occult image?

Days to a couple of weeks in a fast medium, and months in a slow one, which is why the trigger matters more than the payload. Most of these images are designed once and then die because nobody gave them a second reason to be copied. The design question nobody asks is what the third version should be.
