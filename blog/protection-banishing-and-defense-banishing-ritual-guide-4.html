Protection, Banishing and Defense

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 11 min read

I built a protective working around a length of wood, and I want to report honestly on the finding, which is that the object did less than the sequence did, and that the month I left it in a drawer produced nothing worth reporting. That is a smaller claim than anybody wants from this material and it is the one the notes support. The object was not decorative, in the sense that the ritual engaged it directly, and it was not inert either. It was a fixed point, and a fixed point turns out to be worth something for reasons that have more to do with continuity and location than with the material. What I got wrong, and what took the second half of the year to diagnose, is that I had been treating the sequence as the decorative part, on the reasonable assumption that a sequence is a formality around a real act. The reverse turned out to be true. When I forgot the object the working was still there. When I skipped the sequence the working was gone, even with the object in my hand. There is a lesson in that about where a practice actually lives, and I would rather report the lesson than the object.

The working, and the object at the centre of it

A length of wood, roughly the length of my forearm, unmodified, made in a single sitting, and then used every evening for two months. It went in the drawer after that. The intention was protective, in the ordinary sense: a boundary around a particular space and a particular set of hours, held by a thing I could pick up.

What I had was not a rare object. I made it because the material this tradition works with is mostly mass-produced and a thing made in one sitting carries a specific property, which is that nobody else made it. Whether that property does anything is exactly the question, and the honest version of the answer is that I could not separate it from the fact that making the thing took twenty minutes of my attention. The making is the more plausible mechanism and I am aware that is a deflationary conclusion.

What the object was doing

Two things I can defend and one I cannot. It marked a location, because the sequence happened in one room and nowhere else, and the object was where the room began. It marked a time, because the evening sequence happened at a fixed hour and the object was what the hour referred to. Those two are the same function in different registers and both are plausible mechanisms for continuity.

The one I cannot defend is any claim about the material. Wood is wood. I tested the association with several traditional correspondences and found that the correspondences in the tables do not agree with each other on what a length of wood is for, which is a fact about the tables rather than about wood. So whatever the object was doing, the framework of correspondences did not add anything to it and I should have noticed that earlier.

The evening it stopped being an instrument

Somewhere in month two I picked it up and put it back down and did the sequence, and the object was in my hand as a shape rather than as a step, and I noticed. That is the whole event. There is nothing dramatic in it and it is the only moment in two months where something changed in the practice rather than in the result.

What had changed is that I had begun to hold the object as the practice and the sequence as the ritual surrounding it. That is a natural drift and it happens to everything that is used often. The instrument became the thing, the tool became the talisman, and the only symptom was that the tool had stopped being used and I had not noticed, which is a symptom with a very long fuse in any practice built around a physical object.

The sequence, which is the part that carried it

The sequence is a short set of moves with a declared boundary in the middle and a walk to the door at the end, and it is the part of this working that I would now describe as the whole thing. The declared boundary does the work, the walk makes the exit unambiguous, and the whole thing takes under two minutes.

This is not a discovery about the object. It is a discovery about ordering, and the traditional versions put the sequence first and the apparatus after, which is the reverse of what almost everybody I have spoken to does. The traditional order is: declare, move, put the thing down, stop. The order I had drifted into was: pick up the thing, declare, move, put down, stop. Those are not the same working and only one of them survives a bad week.

Two months with the object

The two months produced nothing I would call a result. The discomfort I was hoping to address did not visibly reduce, in the way that such things are measured, which is that I noticed it less often. That is a real change and an entirely subjective one, and I put it in the same category as the quiet fortnight in the first month: something I cannot attribute.

What I can say is that the practice survived two months, which is longer than several things I have tried. It was short enough to do on a bad evening, and being short enough to do on a bad evening is the whole difference between a practice and a plan. When I later made one of these with a twenty-minute sequence, it lasted nine days, and the difference was not the intention or the object. It was the length of the commitment at the moment I was least motivated to make it.

The month I forgot it and nothing broke

It stayed in the drawer for most of a month. I did the sequence, mostly, without it. Nothing happened. That sentence contains the entire finding and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not show, because the temptation in a report like this is to over-read it.

It does not show that the object was inert. It shows that the working did not require the object in the sense of a necessary component, and that the sequence was the part I was actually doing. It also does not show that the object was useless, since the object may have been doing something I was not in a position to notice. What it rules out is the strong version of the claim, which is that the material was load-bearing. That version is gone, and I am content to have lost it, because it was the version that required me to believe something I could not defend to anybody.

A fear I could not place

In the middle of the month with the object in the drawer, something happened that I could not classify. I felt a strong sense of a boundary, in a room where I was alone, with no object and a shortened sequence. I could not put it in the record as a result, because I could not say what caused it, and I could not put it in as nothing, because it was specific.

I mention it because it is the kind of thing that gets edited out of a record by someone who is being careful, and being careful is right, but editing it out entirely makes the record unusable for the person who reads it in three years. It went in with a note saying unclassified, and the note is more useful than a clean month would have been. The alternative is a record with a gap in it where something happened, and gaps are where people stop writing things down.

What the record shows

Six entries, in descending order of confidence. The sequence is what the practice was, and the object was a fixed point that the sequence referred to. A two-minute commitment survived two months; a twenty-minute one survived nine days. A strong claim about the material is not supported and I have dropped it. The declared boundary plus a walk to the door is the part with a defensible function, and it is the part that comes from the tradition rather than from me.

Two things the record does not show, both of which are the usual targets of this kind of reporting. It does not show that the working was effective against anything, because nothing external happened that I could measure. And it does not show that the practice was a placebo, because I have no way to establish that either. The record establishes what I did and what I noticed, in that order, and it is honest about the difference between the two.

The three operations, in the order they were needed

Contemporary writers in this field distinguish three things that get run together, and the distinction became useful to me only after I had got them the wrong way round for two months. Removing something. Purifying a space. Re-establishing your own footing. The first is the classical operation, the second is a domestic one, and the third is the one that people actually need most often and almost never do.

The order I needed was the third first, then the first, then the second, and I had been doing them in the order the tradition presents them. That ordering error cost me most of the useful part of those two months. Most of what presents itself as a need for a protective working is a need to be somewhere else for a while, and a sequence that takes two minutes can be done on the way out of a room, whereas a twenty-minute cleansing cannot. The three operations in the order they were needed, with two minutes each, would have done the whole job of the version I built.

Where a boundary becomes a talisman

The transition is gradual and there is no moment to notice it. What happens is that the object acquires a status independent of the operation, so that the presence of the object begins to feel like the thing that was doing the work, and the operation becomes what you do so that you can be near the object. This is the standard failure of any practice built around a material component and it is not a critique of having one.

The test I use now is whether I would still do the sequence if the object were gone, and the answer should be yes with no hesitation. If the answer is genuinely uncertain, the object has taken over. The traditional materials handle this by not giving the object a name, by never discussing it as a source of power, and by making the declaration rather than the material the operative part. Those choices look like austere grammar and they turn out to be a load-bearing design decision about where a practice lives.

What the object is for now

It is on the shelf, and it is a reminder. I use it when I am starting something new, because making a thing in one sitting is a reliable way of spending twenty minutes on a decision, and that is a better use of twenty minutes than most of the ways I have spent them. It is not consulted, it is not charged, and it is not in a drawer where I forget it, which was the outcome I was avoiding and which is itself the lesson.

If I built another one, which I do not intend to, I would keep the declared boundary and the walk and drop the object, and I would expect to have less of a feeling about it. That is the trade, and I took it deliberately rather than by drift, which is the actual difference between this account and the first two months of it. A practice you can maintain is worth more than a practice you are attached to, and the attachment is what stops the maintenance.

What I would put on the list

A two-minute sequence, done at a fixed hour, in a fixed room, with a declared boundary and a walk to the door. No object. A record with three columns and a place for anything unclassified. The three operations in the order they are needed rather than the order they are taught. And a rule, written down while I still remember what it is for, that the practice continues on the two minutes and not on the feeling.

That last one is the only item that is really new, and it is not an occult instruction. It is a piece of ordinary maintenance advice that applies to any practice, and it took a drawer, a month, and a drifted ordering to make it obvious. Everything else in this account was available in the tradition from the beginning. I took the object for the practice because the object was interesting, and I have not entirely stopped doing that, and the record is the only thing that tells me when I have.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a protective object need to be made by hand?

Not for any reason I could defend. I made mine in one sitting because making it took twenty minutes of attention, and that is a plausible mechanism, but I could not separate the hand-made property from the twenty minutes of attention that went into it. A mass-produced object used consistently would perform the same function in my experience. What matters is the fixed point, not the material, and the traditional reluctance to name the material as a source of power turns out to be a sensible design decision.

How long should a protective ritual be?

Short enough to do on the evening you least want to do it. Mine was under two minutes, and it survived two months. A twenty-minute version I built later lasted nine days, and nothing about the intention or the object had changed. Length is the variable people optimise for the wrong reason. The commitment you will actually keep at your lowest motivation is the one that determines whether you have a practice in month three.

What is the difference between banishing, cleansing, and grounding?

Removing, purifying, and re-establishing your own footing. Most people need the third first, since much of what presents itself as a need for a banishing is a need to be somewhere else for a while, and then the first, and rarely the second. Contemporary writers distinguish them because people routinely run all three together and then cannot work out which one was missing. Each is a different operation with a different timescale, and the order matters more than the technique.

My protective object stopped feeling powerful, what now?

That is the drift everybody's objects undergo, and it is not a failure, it is the same process as anything used often becoming furniture. Check whether you would still do the sequence without it. If the answer is uncertain, the object has taken over the practice, and the fix is to put it down for a month and keep the sequence. The traditional materials avoid naming the object as a source of power, which is not austere grammar but a design decision about where a practice lives.

Does the record have to be kept for a working to work?

I cannot show that it has to be, and I can show that it is the only thing that tells you what is happening. A practice without a record is a practice that confirms whatever you believed when you started, and it will feel effective the entire time. The record is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between a practice and a set of beliefs about a practice, and the unclassified column matters more than the tidy ones, because the tidy ones can be written retrospectively.
