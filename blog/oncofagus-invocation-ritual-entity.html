ONCOFAGUS

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

ONCOFAGUS is one of the better-documented names in the smaller grimoire catalogues, which makes it a reasonable place to learn the mechanics of entity work. What follows is the operation rather than the biography: how to build a frame, how to ask, how to close, and how to tell afterwards whether anything actually happened.

Choosing a Name to Learn On

Pick a name with a stated office you can check, not a name you have seen on a video thumbnail. The smaller catalogues are better for this than the famous ones, because the offices are short, concrete and unlikely to have been inflated in the retelling. A name whose office you can read in one sentence is a name you can work with honestly.

The other requirement is that you have no emotional stake in the result. Beginners gravitate to names associated with power or money and then read every event afterwards as confirmation. Learning the mechanics on a low-stakes name first means the framework gets tested before the desire does.

ONCOFAGUS fits that brief. It is a working entry with a clear and narrow remit, and nobody's plan for the year depends on the outcome of learning it.

Invocation and Evocation Are Not the Same Operation

In classical ceremonial usage, evocation means calling a spirit so that it appears, and invocation means inviting its influence to operate through the practitioner. Two different operations, with two different amounts of apparatus and two different failure modes. Almost all modern writing collapses them into a single word, which is why so much advice contradicts itself.

Evocation is the heavier one. It wants a defined space, a stated time, a visible sign of presence, and a formal dismissal. It also wants an exit that you have rehearsed, because the failure mode of evocation is an unclosed session, not a hostile spirit.

Invocation is lighter and suits most of what people actually want. You are not asking for a visitor; you are asking for a pattern to run through you for a while. It fits in ten minutes on a cleared table, it does not need a costume or a full hour of preparation, and it ends when you decide it ends.

How a Named Entity Functions in Practice

Peter Carroll's influential definition treated entities in chaos magic as aspects of the practitioner's own mind rather than external beings. That position is worth holding, not because it is the only defensible one, but because it takes most of the risk out of the question while leaving the working intact.

On that reading a name is a compressed description of a mode of attention. Working with one is closer to writing an instruction for a part of yourself you do not normally have direct access to, which is why the results feel like competence arriving from outside rather than effort succeeding.

The alternative reading keeps the entity external and the risk correspondingly higher. Chaos magic mostly runs on the first, and the entities used in it are the ones with rich enough symbolism to be worth the compression: the seventy-two of the Ars Goetia being the most structured catalogue in Western grimoire literature, with a rank, a form, an office and named powers for each entry.

Building the Frame From Nothing

The end is the component people leave out, and leaving it out is the most common reason a session runs long. Decide the closing before you begin and the working has a shape. Leave it open and you will find yourself still sitting there an hour later, adding ceremony because you do not want to stop, which is the opposite of discipline.

  1. The name, spoken once, out loud, in the present tense.
  2. The office, in your own words, in one sentence, drawn from a source you have actually read.
  3. The request, phrased as a request, with a timeframe attached out loud.
  4. The end, decided in advance, so you are not deciding it while you are inside it.

The Sequence, Step by Step

Step four is where the discipline lives. The office line and the request line are different sentences answering different questions, and the failure to separate them produces a working that is emotionally sincere and structurally confused.

  1. Clear a surface and put a dark cloth or a sheet of paper on it.
  2. Write the date and the time. Do this first, while your attention is still ordinary.
  3. Mark a boundary: chalk, a line of salt, or the edge of the cloth.
  4. Write the office and the request as two separate lines. The office is what it does, the request is what you want. Confusing them is the most frequent mistake in this material.
  5. Say the name once. Four seconds, no titles, no repetitions.
  6. Do something with your hands for a few minutes. Write, wash a cup, fold the cloth.
  7. Repeat the request in identical words, so the second statement matches the first.
  8. Close: put the pen down, snuff the light if you lit one, and say that the working is closed.
  9. Read the record back before you put it away.

Reading the Result Without Cheating

Before you start, write down one specific thing that would count as an answer. Not the outcome you want, which is too generous, but a checkable event: a message arriving, a decision finally being made, a detail you were missing suddenly showing up. Write it on the sheet and leave it there.

Afterwards, look at the sheet. Feeling moved is not a result. It is common, it is cheap, and it follows from any unhurried hour spent concentrating on a problem. The written check is the only thing that separates a working that did something from a working that was a good hour.

Run three attempts with a corrected office and a stated timeframe before drawing any conclusion. If three separate tries produce nothing you can point to, the framework is fine and the request is wrong. Change the question, not the method.

Where These Workings Fail

A mismatched office is the first and most common cause. You want a name that does something adjacent to what you asked, the working runs, nothing happens, and the conclusion drawn is that the whole material is inert. The material is fine; the lookup was not.

The second is a request with no timeframe. A request without an end cannot be wrong, and a request that cannot be wrong gives you no way to notice it doing nothing.

The third is a session with no closing, which leaves the next evening's attempt stacked on top of an unfinished one. The fourth is treating a name as a vending machine rather than a pattern, and asking the same office for a different outcome each time until the record stops meaning anything.

The fifth is apparatus. Building an elaborate setup for a ten-minute invocation adds moving parts and removes attention, which was the only thing doing the work.

Tools, Timing and Materials

A pen, paper, a light you can snuff, and an hour you are not interrupted in. That is the whole list, and the fourth item is the one nobody can buy.

Timing follows the office rather than the clock. If the source assigns the entity to a day, a planet or a particular position, working inside that window costs nothing and is a reasonable default. If it says nothing about timing, pick a fixed hour and keep using it, so that repetition builds a pattern you can actually compare against.

Material is close to irrelevant, which is a relief for anyone who assumed otherwise. Anything that marks the surface works. The value of a chalk line or a black cloth is that it is cheap enough to set up without thinking about it, and that is its entire virtue.

Repeating Without Going Stale

Three workings on one office is a sequence. A fourth starts to be noise, and past that the repetition stops producing information and starts producing a feeling that you have been busy.

Keep the frame identical and change one variable each time. Change the hour, or the request, or the timeframe. Hold everything else still so that the log gives you something to compare. A log where nothing varied tells you nothing except that you sat down several times.

The archive of records is what turns this from a habit into a practice. Two hundred lines of date, office, request, timeframe and outcome is a body of evidence about your own workings that no amount of theorising replaces.

Signs It Landed, and Signs It Did Not

A result that arrived on time, in the shape you wrote down beforehand, is a result. Anything that happened at some point in a three-week window is a coincidence with a story attached, and reading it as confirmation is the most reliable way to convince yourself of something false.

Useful partial signs exist and they are modest: an unusual fluency in the subject, a connection you did not go looking for, a difficulty that resolves without you addressing it. Treat those as encouraging and not as proof, because they have ordinary explanations and ordinary explanations are usually the right ones.

The clearest negative sign is not a failed working but a failed record. Two months of sessions with nothing written down is the outcome to correct, because it means you will never know whether any of it worked.

The Ethics, Briefly

There is no reliable evidence of external danger in this material, and the real hazard is mundane: working with a name you have never researched. Fifteen minutes with a source text settles whether the office matches your need, and skipping it is the only genuinely avoidable mistake here.

A second ethical point is about other people. Do not build a practice whose results other people have to pay for, and do not use an entity frame to extract from anyone who has not agreed to it. A working aimed at another person's decisions is a different act from a working aimed at your own situation, and it belongs in a different category entirely.

A third is self-deception. Keep the check honest, record the failures, and let a poor result end the working rather than escalating it. An approach that requires you to believe in it to work is not a technique, and the log is what tells you which one you have.

Fitting This Into a Wider Practice

Entity work is one tool among several, and it is a poor default. Most of what people want from this material can be had faster with a written intention, a sigil, and a week of attention, and the entity frame earns its place only when the specific pattern you are reaching for is one you can name.

Where it earns its place is in precision. A catalogue of named offices gives you a vocabulary for situations that are otherwise shapeless, and naming the situation accurately is a large part of doing anything about it.

The wider practice is the log. One sheet per year, listing every working across every method, is worth more than any single system you could commit to, because it is the only artefact that compares them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I work with demons at all?

There is no reliable evidence of external danger, and working with a name you have never researched is the actual hazard. Read the entity's stated office first and confirm it matches the domain you need. If the office lines up and you keep an honest written check, this is bookkeeping with an unusual vocabulary, which is a much less dramatic thing than the reputation suggests.

What is the difference between evocation and invocation?

Evocation calls the entity to manifest, and classical practice treats it as the heavier operation with more apparatus, a visible sign of presence and a rehearsed dismissal. Invocation asks the entity to operate through you, which suits ten minutes on a cleared table. Most modern writing uses the two words interchangeably, which is why so much advice about this material contradicts itself.

Do I need a full ritual circle?

No. Chaos magic reduced the apparatus on purpose, and the reduction is the point. A defined intention, a sigil, a stated frame and an end are historically sufficient and practically sufficient. Everything beyond that adds moving parts and takes attention away from the one thing that was doing the work.

Is ONCOFAGUS safe to work with as a first name?

It is a good choice for learning precisely because the stakes are low and the office is narrow, which means a flat result costs you nothing and teaches you the mechanics faster than a famous name would. Read the office line yourself rather than trusting a summary, write down one checkable outcome before you start, and treat the first few sessions as practice in the frame rather than as attempts to get something.

How long should one of these workings take?

About ten minutes for the invocation form described here, plus the two minutes before and after for setting up and reading the record. A longer session is not a stronger one. If you find yourself extending it, that is usually reluctance to close rather than something the working needs, and the end is the part that makes the next attempt possible.
