Love, Attraction and Relationships

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

This is a record of twelve weeks spent doing love magic badly, on purpose, with a written rulebook, so that the rulebook could be checked afterwards. Most of the material written about this subject is instructional, and instruction has a structural problem: it describes the intended result and leaves the record of what actually happened out. What follows is the other half. I ran three separate workings, wrote down the terms before each one, and kept the failures in the file instead of filing them separately. One of the three was aimed at another person, which is where the ethics get sharp, and I stopped that one earlier than the rulebook said I should. Read this as a set of results with the favourable parts removed, not as an argument against the practice. The consent objection raised against love magic is the strongest argument in this whole field, and the twelve weeks did nothing to weaken it.

What this series of workings was supposed to show

The question I set out to answer was narrow. If a sigil aimed at a relationship is charged, and the relationship does not change, what does the practitioner have that they did not have before? Three possible answers exist. The sigil did nothing. The sigil did something that cannot be observed. The sigil changed the practitioner and the outcome is downstream of that. Only the third answer is testable from inside a single life over twelve weeks, so that is the one I was looking for.

Before starting I wrote down what would count as a miss, because I did not trust myself to decide afterwards. A miss was any window in which the specific thing I had asked for did not happen and I could not name a step I had taken that differed from the previous window. That definition turned out to be harsher than most published versions of this practice can survive.

The rules I fixed before the first charge

Three rules, written on one page and photographed so I could not revise them quietly. One working per category, so a result in one area could not contaminate the reading of another. No target that had not spoken to me in the previous six months. And a hard rule that no second charge against the same person would be made inside the twelve weeks, whatever the first one seemed to produce.

That last rule was not in the tradition I was working inside. It came from the ethics literature, and it was the single decision that shaped the whole run. It meant that one of my three workings could not be repeated under any circumstances, which limited the evidence considerably. I would make the same rule again.

  • One working per category, so the readings do not contaminate each other.
  • No target who had not spoken to me within the previous six months.
  • No repeat charge against the same person, whatever the first one produced.

The first working, aimed at my own availability

I reduced the intent to a single imperative sentence: become available to the person I already liked. The target was myself, which removes the consent problem entirely and also removes most of the drama that makes this practice interesting. Charged over three evenings, then deliberately ignored for four weeks.

Something did change, and I want to be careful about the claim. I stopped declining invitations I would previously have skipped. That is a behavioural change I can date to the window, and it persisted after I stopped looking for it. What did not change was the actual meeting rate with that person, which is the thing I had pretended to be the target. The sigil appears to have moved something upstream of the outcome.

The second working, aimed at another person, and why it stopped

This is the one the ethics literature is about, and it behaved exactly as the objection predicts. The sigil was reduced, charged twice, and produced a measurable change in my own behaviour: I made contact three times in a fortnight, which is more than my baseline with anybody. Nothing about the other person changed. Their replies stayed short, the last two were slower than the first, and I read the change in my own behaviour as a result while reading their silence as a lack of interest.

I stopped on day nineteen, six days short of the review date. The reason was not a realisation about magic. It was noticing that I had begun adjusting my sending toward messages designed to provoke a reply, and that I had started checking whether the timing matched an astrological window. That is escalation, and it arrived through small practical decisions rather than a single dramatic one.

The third working, aimed at restoring what had already gone

Reconciliation work gets described as lower risk than attraction work, and after the second working I was inclined to agree. The bond predated the practice, so there was no question of manufacturing a preference. The target was a friendship that had ended badly two years earlier. Charged once, against the no-repeat rule, which this target was exempt from under my own terms.

Nothing happened. Four weeks, no contact from either side, and one of my own messages answered with a delay that I read as politeness rather than a door reopening. This is the working I found most frustrating and most informative, because the practice offers no way to distinguish between a sigil that failed and a person who had finished wanting the thing being restored.

Where the failures actually clustered

Not in the workings. In the week after each one. The pattern was consistent enough that I could write it down as a rule: the risk is in the interpretation, not the charge. A charge takes ninety seconds. The following four days are when I looked for signs, assigned meaning to ordinary messages, and decided whether to send one more.

The second cluster was in my record keeping. I logged the first working carefully, the second carefully, and the third in two lines because I did not want to admit it had failed. That gap in the file is the most informative thing in it, and it is also the reason the twelve weeks produced less usable evidence than they could have.

  1. The charge itself is ninety seconds. The interpretation lasts four days.
  2. Ordinary messages get assigned meaning the moment you are looking for a result.
  3. Misses are logged in one line and wins in a paragraph, unless you fix that in advance.
  4. The decision to send one more message is the actual ritual, and nobody calls it one.

What the consent objection gets right

The argument is not that love magic is forbidden or that spirits object. It is that a sigil aimed at a named person overrides that person's stated preferences, and the practitioner is the only one who is asked whether anything is wrong. Chaos magic's own position makes this worse rather than better. If the practitioner's will is the operative ingredient, then the ethics are entirely a matter of what the will is pointed at, and the tradition supplies no rule for pointing it at somebody.

What my second working added is a mechanism the objection does not need. The problem is not only that I overrode a preference. It is that the practice rewards me for overriding it, in small increments, because each increment feels like effort rather than like harm. An argument that only says this is manipulation is too weak. The stronger version is that it is a reward structure, and reward structures are harder to notice than intentions.

The escalation pattern, described without the metaphysics

Occult sources talk about repeated love magic cascading. I would not defend the metaphysics and I would defend the description. What cascaded in my record was behaviour: one charge, then a second charge, then timing decisions, then message design, then a sixth week in which the object of the working had become a subject of the working. Each step was small. The direction was not in doubt at any point.

The useful part of the traditional description is that it names the process rather than the outcome. Nothing supernatural is required to explain why a person who has decided a relationship is desirable will gradually reorganise their behaviour around that conclusion. The pattern is available to anyone willing to look at their own record with the same attention they would give a stranger.

What the applications were useful for

Almost nothing, over twelve weeks. The reduction step is the one piece of software I would pay for, and I paid for it once rather than subscribing. Drawing the glyph by hand took longer and I got the same result. The tracking features were where the app earned nothing at all, because the tracking that mattered was the four-day interpretation window, and no application prompts you to log that.

The one thing a digital tool did well was holding the original sentence. I reduced each intent to one imperative line, and having that exact line sitting in a record I could not revise was more useful than the drawing ever was. A free notebook does the same job. The paid version does it with an interface.

The moment it turned into something I did not want

It was not dramatic. It was the moment I started keeping a separate note of what I had sent and when, for the purpose of avoiding sending too much. I had begun managing the other person's inbox from a distance. That is a change in what the practice is for, and it happened in the fourth week while I was still telling myself I was only keeping my own attention steady.

I put the sigil in a drawer and did not charge anything for six weeks. The pull to resume was strongest in the first three days and largely gone by the second week, which is worth recording because the folk account predicts the opposite, a steadily building compulsion. What I got instead was a habit that exhausted itself without any intervention from me.

What survived the twelve weeks, and what went in the drawer

I would repeat the first working and the rulebook. Writing the terms before charging, including the definition of a miss, is what made the twelve weeks legible. I would not repeat the second, and the reason is not that it failed. It is that the design required a rule to stop it, and the rule came from outside the practice.

I dropped the astrological timing entirely after week five. It added a calculation that cost me about ten minutes and, more importantly, gave me a reason to delay that felt principled. I started charging later rather than at the right moment, and the record got worse. Everything I kept was small, boring and written down.

The record, and what it supports

The file supports a claim much narrower than the one I started with. A sigil aimed at a relationship did not change anybody else's behaviour, including the behaviour of the person it was aimed at. It reliably changed mine. The only working that produced a change I could see in the world was the one aimed at myself, and the change was upstream of the outcome rather than the outcome itself.

It also supports a claim about the method rather than the magic. The four days after a charge are where this practice actually runs, and that window is invisible to the tradition because it is where the practitioner is alone with an interpretation. If you want to know whether this works, write down what you did in week two, not in week one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What did twelve weeks of love magic actually produce?

One usable change in my own behaviour and nothing measurable in anybody else's. The working aimed at my own availability moved my behaviour and did not move the meeting rate I had pretended to be the target. The working aimed at another person changed my sending and nothing about their replies. The reconciliation working produced no contact at all. The honest total is one narrow effect, two nulls, and a clear account of where the escalation risk lives.

Does that mean love magic does not work?

It means the claim is testable and the test came back narrow. My own behaviour changed in a way I could date. No third party's behaviour changed in any way I could detect. There is no version of that result set that supports the strong claim, and there is a version of it that supports a much smaller and more practical claim about your own conduct, which is the only part of the practice I would hand to somebody else.

What is a defensible way to practise this?

Aim it at your own behaviour: your availability, your honesty, your willingness to be the person who says the difficult thing. That version has no consent problem because you are the only party involved, and it is also the version with a trackable outcome. Aiming it at a named person overrides a stated preference, and the tradition supplies no rule for judging when that override is acceptable.

Is it worth paying for a love magic application?

The reduction step is the one piece of software worth buying, and I would buy it once rather than subscribe. A free notebook holds the original sentence just as well, and the sentence is the part that carries the work. What I would not pay for is a tracking suite, because the tracking that would actually have helped me was the four-day window after a charge, and no application prompts you to write that down.

What made me stop was not a spiritual experience. What was it?

Noticing that I had started choosing message wording designed to force a reply, and that I was timing sends to an astrological window. That is a practical change in behaviour and it arrived through small increments rather than one decision. By then I was managing somebody else's inbox from a distance, which is a different activity from what I set out to do, and it had happened while I was still telling myself I was only steadying my own attention.
