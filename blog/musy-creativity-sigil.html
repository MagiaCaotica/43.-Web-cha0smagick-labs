MUSY

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Almost every creative block has the same three-part anatomy, and almost every method for treating it addresses one of those parts while leaving the other two alone. The first part is the belief that good work arrives from outside, delivered by a mood, a muse, a favourable hour, or a burst of confidence that has to catch you before it disappears. The second is the absence of a structure: no fixed form, no countable output, no date, nothing that would make tomorrow's work a continuation of today's rather than a fresh negotiation. The third is the internal verdict, the quiet voice that assesses the quality of what you are making and uses that assessment as a reason to stop. A creative sigil is a small technology aimed at all three at once, and it works best when you understand which part it is actually carrying, because the parts fail differently and call for different repairs.

A name for a creative practice

Give the practice a name before you give it anything else. A name does something a goal statement cannot: it turns a mood you hope to have into a thing you have agreed to do. Names in this tradition are usually short and slightly arbitrary, chosen for sound rather than meaning, and the arbitrariness is useful. A descriptive name like my writing hour recruits the part of you that wants the work to succeed, which is the part that has been vetoing it for months.

The second job of the name is separation. Once the practice has a name, it stops being an assessment of you. When you sit down and nothing comes, you have not failed as a writer; you have failed to do a thing called MUSY, which is a smaller and more repairable event. Most people who try to work this way abandon it in the first fortnight, and the reason is almost always that they let the name leak back into a judgement.

The substitution that does the work

The load-bearing move in a creative practice is not visualisation, intention, or ritual. It is substitution. You replace the external source of inspiration with an internal mechanism of commitment, and you do it explicitly enough that the old belief loses its grip. Inspiration was always a story about permission, and a story about permission can be withdrawn at any moment by whoever issued it.

A committed mechanism cannot be withdrawn. A page count, a fixed duration, a scheduled hour: these are dull, verifiable, and indifferent to your mood, which is precisely why they work. When the practice is running properly, you stop asking whether you feel like writing and start asking whether the number has been met. That sounds like a small semantic shift and it is not; it moves the activity from a mood-dependent performance to a scheduled process, and processes produce more than performances do.

Constraint as a scheduled appointment

Constraint is the part of the method that gets talked about least and matters most. Fix the form, fix the count, fix the hour, and the deadline does the rest, because a constraint converts an open-ended commitment into a bounded one. Open-ended commitments are the easiest thing in the world to abandon; a commitment to three hundred words before lunch is not.

The reliable finding here is not mystical. Research on creativity keeps turning up the same result: imposed structure raises output, and the effect is large enough that professionals use it deliberately. A deadline, a fixed format, a limited palette, a word count: each of these removes a class of decisions, and each class of decisions is a place where production stops. Constraints also change what counts as finished, which matters more than it sounds, because a blocked writer is usually waiting for a standard of completion that no draft would ever meet.

Spare's method, and the part nobody copies

Austin Osman Spare formalised sigilisation in the early twentieth century, and the method he described was narrower than the one most people use now. He wrote specularly, which is to say in a mirror-image script nobody else can read, and he produced a fixed amount every day regardless of quality. The sigil in his usage was a compression device: a way of holding a sentence in a form that would survive being forgotten.

The part nobody copies is the boring half. The mirrored writing is the memorable half, and it is the half that does no work. What changes a practice is the daily fixed output, and almost nobody sustains it for long enough to find out. Practitioners who keep the spectacle and drop the discipline get a pleasant afternoon and no cumulative result, because the accumulation lives entirely in the repetition.

Diagnosing which of the three parts is broken

When a creative practice stalls, the useful question is which of the three components has failed, because the repair is different for each. If you are waiting to feel inspired, the substitution has failed and you need a smaller, dumber commitment. If you are producing but scattering, the constraint has failed and you need a form you cannot exceed. If you are producing steadily and then abandoning the output before it is finished, the verdict has failed, and that is the hardest of the three.

You can tell which one is happening by looking at where the practice stops. Stopping before you begin is a permission problem. Stopping halfway through a session is a duration problem. Stopping just before you would have to show the work is a judgement problem, and it is the one most methods miss. A block that happens at the moment of exposure is not laziness; it is a standard being applied retroactively to work that was never going to satisfy it.

Writing the sentence before the form

Build the aim as a sentence in the imperative mood, and write it before you design anything. Not a topic, not a mood, not a description of what you hope to make: a command, addressed to yourself, in the present tense. I will write four hundred words before I stop. I will finish the piece before Friday. I will show it to one person.

The imperative matters because a wish has no tense and no agent. A wish dissolves under pressure because there is nothing in it to hold. A command has a subject and a verb, which means it can be complied with or failed, and compliance can be counted. The form follows the sentence and not the other way round, and if you find yourself designing a shape before you have written what the shape is for, you have started a different activity with the same tools.

A first week, hour by hour

The first week is a rehearsal, not a production. Build the glyph in one sitting, charge it by forgetting the sentence, and then run the same short session at the same hour for five or six days with a count low enough that you cannot fail it. Three hundred words. Twenty minutes. A single page. The number should feel insultingly easy, because the thing you are installing is the habit, and the habit is what has to survive a bad day.

Write one line at the end of each session: the count, and one sentence about how it went. Nothing else. Most people add commentary here, and commentary is where self-flattery enters and the record stops being usable. The whole apparatus is one glyph, one repeated hour, and a column of numbers, and any addition to that apparatus has to earn its place by making the numbers more honest.

The three columns nobody fills in

A useful record has three columns and most people fill one. The first is the count: what you produced, in whatever unit is countable. The second is the state you were in beforehand, on a crude scale, because the same session on a good day and a bad day is not the same event. The third is what you actually wrote, in a line or two, which is the only column that lets you see whether the sessions are producing anything or merely producing something.

The third column is the one that gets left out, and leaving it turns the record into a mood diary. It also takes about fifteen seconds, which is the whole problem: the cost of the honest entry is close enough to the cost of the dishonest one that convenience decides. Fill it anyway. After a month the three columns together will tell you more about your own working conditions than any amount of introspection will, because they separate capacity from circumstance.

Where a creative working stalls

The common failure is escalation. The first count is too easy and gets raised, then the schedule gets tightened, then the aim is made more ambitious, and within a month the practice is a demanding instrument that only works on good days. Every step of that sequence felt like progress. The practice has been made harder at exactly the moment it needed to become automatic, and automatic is the only state in which it survives contact with a bad week.

The second failure is audience. As soon as the work is aimed at a specific reader rather than at the count, the verdict returns, and the verdict is the component you removed by hand. Public work is a different activity with a different aim and it should have a different name and a different hour. Mixing the two is the most reliable way to end up with neither a practice nor a following.

Evidence that is not a good mood

A good week is not evidence and a bad week is not a verdict. This is the single hardest habit in the whole practice, because the feeling of a productive session is genuinely pleasurable and it arrives immediately, and immediate pleasure is easy to mistake for evidence. The evidence is the column of counts, read months later, with no memory of which days felt good.

Set a review interval and keep it. Once a month, count the sessions, count the days you completed the count, and look at the third column to see whether the work has a shape. None of this tells you whether the practice is supernatural. It tells you whether you are producing, which is the only question a method of this kind can answer, and the only question worth answering before you go looking for signs.

Aiming at an audience without hollowing it out

You do not have to choose between working for yourself and working for readers, but you do have to run them separately. The private practice exists to remove the verdict, and anything aimed outward reintroduces it by the back door. The public practice is a different project with a different standard, and it is legitimate, useful, and often the thing that pays.

The workable arrangement is a named separation: two hours, two names, two records. The private one keeps a count and produces the material. The public one has a deadline and an actual reader, and it is allowed to be hard. What does not work is a single practice that serves both purposes, because the reader will be in the room during the hours when you are trying to install a habit, and the habit will not install.

When the constraint becomes the block

There is a point at which the apparatus is the problem. If you have spent more energy designing the glyph, refining the schedule, and preparing the workspace than you have spent writing, the method has become a way of not writing that feels like diligence. This happens to competent practitioners with good intentions, and nothing about the occult framing protects against it.

The tell is that the preparation has a ritual quality and the writing has an administrative one. The correct response is subtraction, not adjustment. Cut the form, cut the schedule, cut the workspace down to a page and a pen, and leave only the sentence and the number. If the work does not appear, the constraint was never the thing that was missing, and you have been using structure to postpone a decision that only a blank page will force.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a creative sigil actually improve my writing?

No controlled evidence supports the occult claim, and the practice still works for most people who keep it, which tells you which part is doing the work. The substitution of a scheduled commitment for a wait for permission is a behavioural change, and behavioural changes improve output. The sigil is a handle for that change, and a good handle is worth having, but the handle is not the force.

How do I know whether the sigil charged?

You cannot know directly, and any method that tells you otherwise is selling something. What you can do is check the indirect signs: the charge is complete when the sentence is forgotten but the behaviour persists, which is the test Austin Osman Spare described. If you remember the sentence and still do not write, the charge failed. If you have forgotten it and still write, it did not.

Should I aim my sigil at money or at a career?

Aim it at something countable that you personally control, which usually means output rather than outcome. Output is verifiable, outcome is not, and a target you cannot check will be resolved by wishful thinking within a fortnight. A money or career aim can be attached later as a reason, but the working itself should name an action you can tick off, because the action is the part that survives contact with a bad day.

What if I miss several days in a row?

Miss nothing, and miss less. The instinct after a lapse is to compensate with a larger session, and compensation is how a practice becomes a punishment. Restart at the original count, at the original hour, and treat the gap as data rather than debt. A record that includes the gap is more useful than one that has been tidied, because the gap tells you what the practice actually costs on a difficult week.

Is there a spiritual component I am missing?

Nothing in the method depends on one, which is the strength of the approach. You can hold that the glyph is a compression of intent, that it is a psychological artefact, or that it is a formality with no content at all, and the practice behaves the same way in all three cases. Practices that require you to protect a metaphysical belief from examination are fragile, and this one was built to survive being examined.
