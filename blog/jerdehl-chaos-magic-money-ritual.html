JERDEHL

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

There is a specific order to a money working, and almost every version of it that circulates online gets the order wrong. The failure is rarely in the sigil. It is in the fact that people start with the glyph, spend an evening on a beautiful design, charge it with real attention, and only then wonder what they should be measuring. By then the working has a shape and no baseline, which makes it unmeasurable forever. This piece lays out the sequence in the order it actually has to happen: ledger first, aim second, sentence third, glyph fourth, charge fifth, then the long boring part. The ordering is not stylistic. Each step constrains the next one, and a step done out of order destroys information you cannot get back. If you want to skip to the glyph because the glyph is the part that feels like magic, read the first two sections first, because they explain why the glyph is last.

The order of operations, starting with the ledger

Open the ledger before you write anything else. Not a budget, not a wish list: a record of the last three months of what came in and what went out, at the level of detail you would be willing to look at. A spreadsheet with dates, amounts and one line of context per entry is enough. The purpose is not to feel informed about your finances. It is to have a number that will still be true in thirty days, so that whatever happens afterwards has something to be measured against.

A person who begins here will almost always end up with a smaller and more specific aim than a person who begins with the glyph. That is the entire point of doing it first. Once you can see the shape of your own money, you know which part of it you can actually move with a decision, and which part is a matter of other people's decisions. A working aimed at the second part produces a feeling of having tried, and nothing else.

Naming the aim as something a bank would recognise

An aim that a bank would decline to understand is not an aim. It is a mood. Write the aim in the flattest possible language, as a single named figure or a single named event with a date attached. A number by a specific date. An amount of freelance work invoiced this month. The cost of a repair that has been deferred. Anything that could be typed into the ledger column without interpretation belongs in this category.

The test is whether someone who knows nothing about your life could check the result. If they could not, you have not written an aim, you have written a hope. Hope is a legitimate thing to bring to a working, but it belongs in the charge and in the practice, not in the sentence you are asking a glyph to hold. The sentence has one job, and vagueness in it disqualifies it.

The twelve, and what the borrowing is for

Many practitioners reduce an aim to digits before drawing, and a great many use 741 because seven plus four plus one is twelve, a figure that keeps reappearing in temperament charts and in numerological decks. Treat that as a borrowing and mark it as one in your notes. The sum is not a mechanism. It is a way of making the aim compact enough to survive being turned into a shape.

What the borrowing buys you is compression without loss of intent. Once the aim is a handful of characters, you can hold all of it in one glance, and holding the whole statement in one glance is the practical requirement for the charge to work at all. Write down in your notebook that the twelve is a conventional handle, not a claim that the universe is interested in arithmetic. That single line prevents you from having to argue with yourself later.

Reducing the sentence to a glyph

Now draw. Consonants tend to carry the skeleton of a word in written English, vowels tend to carry the filler, and a glyph built from the whole sentence by eye usually arrives at a recognisable reduction without much effort. If you want the traditional letter-removal method, use it; if you want to simplify by eye, that also works and takes less time. Consistency across a series of workings matters more than which method you picked for the first one.

Three checks before you move on. Does the shape look the same every time you draw it, so that you recognise it later? Can you say the sentence out loud and land on the same character set? And is it different enough from your other workings that you will not confuse the two at three in the morning? If any answer is no, fix it now. A glyph you have to re-derive every time is a glyph that will not survive contact with a bad week.

The charge, in one sitting

The charge is the part everybody wants to make ceremonial and the part that benefits most from being dull. Sit down, put the sentence in front of you, say it aloud, look at the glyph, and hold your attention on the connection between them. One sitting, roughly ten to twenty minutes, no music you would miss if it stopped, no candles, no costume. If the sitting goes well, you will feel the sentence differently afterwards, and that difference is the whole effect you are entitled to expect at this stage.

The most common mistake is escalating the ritual until the ritual becomes the point. A charge performed in conditions you find impressive will be more memorable than the same charge performed plainly, and memory is not the thing you are after. What you are after is the sentence staying attached to its shape. That attachment is dull to build and easy to lose, which is why the plain version is the correct one.

The week that decides whether it continues

Set a review date seven days out and put it in the calendar before you close the notebook. On that date, look at the ledger and at the aim sentence, and answer one question: is there any line in the ledger that would be different if the working were real? Almost always the answer on day seven is no, and that is the normal state of affairs rather than a verdict on the glyph.

The day-seven review is where the discipline lives, because it is the first occasion on which you can catch yourself adjusting the aim. The drift is usually small and always in the same direction. The amount goes up. The date moves out. The word that described a specific invoice becomes the word that describes a general improvement in circumstances. Each of those changes makes the working easier to keep and less possible to check, which is exactly the wrong trade.

What the ledger shows at day thirty

At thirty days, run the review properly. Write three columns. The first is what changed in the ledger. The second is what you did differently because of the sentence, since the sentence usually produces small concrete actions within a few days and those are the only things you control. The third, which is the one everybody skips, is what you would have done anyway in a month where nothing was going on.

The third column is what makes the first two readable. Without it, a good month reads as proof and a bad month reads as failure, and both readings are unfounded. With it, you sometimes find that a good month was market timing and a bad month was a bad month. That is not a disappointing finding. It is the difference between a working you can learn something from and one you can only argue about.

Where the sequence fails, and why the ledger goes first

There is one point at which this sequence reliably fails, and it is the second step. If the aim cannot be written in flat language with a figure or a dated event in it, the reason is nearly always that the aim is really about a feeling rather than a quantity, and the working has been pointed at the wrong target. Go back to the ledger and find the underlying action that the feeling would require, then aim at the action. Aimed at the action, the working is checkable. Aimed at the feeling, it can only be confirmed from the inside.

The second point of failure is skipping the ledger. It is tempting, because opening a spreadsheet feels like an act of pessimism and drawing a glyph feels like an act of authorship. But without a baseline there is no day-thirty column three, and without column three the whole exercise degenerates into self-assessment, which is the thing this tradition is supposed to be a method for escaping rather than a way of doing more of.

The follow-through that keeps the glyph attached

The charge is a single event and the working is a series of them. A daily ten-minute glance at the glyph with the sentence said aloud underneath it costs almost nothing and does the actual maintenance. What it maintains is not the connection to any external agency. It maintains your own recall of what you committed to, which is the only asset the working really has.

Skip three days and the sentence starts to lose its edge. Skip a week and you are effectively starting a new working each time you return, which is why practitioners report that sigils stop working after gaps with no explanation that satisfies anybody. The maintenance is not mystical and it is not optional. Decide in advance how many days of absence break the chain, and write the answer next to the glyph.

Small enough to repeat

The best abundance working is a small one you will run again. A figure you can plausibly move this quarter, attached to a sentence of eight words, held in a shape of a dozen strokes, reviewed on a date already in the calendar, is worth more than an ambitious construction you will run once. The first version exists to teach you the sequence, and the sequence is the only thing that transfers.

After the third completed run, the ledger and the three columns will tell you more about how you actually make money than any amount of further design work. That is the point at which the practice stops being about abundance and becomes about your own operating instructions, which is a considerably more useful thing to own.

What you are not allowed to claim

The line between a practice and a promise is drawn at the ledger. You may claim that you acted differently, that you noticed a pattern, that you caught yourself drifting. You may not claim that a glyph attracted a sum of money, because the glyph is a compression of a sentence you wrote and a sentence cannot pay an invoice. Where those two claims blur, the practice becomes a superstition rather than a method, and superstitions are difficult to give up once the hope is attached.

The same restriction applies to the numbers that people build around these workings. A figure that appears in a numerological deck is a handle for your attention, not a mechanism in the world. Treating it as a mechanism is the specific error that turns a workable technique into something you have to defend against people who have read the literature.

Writing the next one

Before you begin anything else, write four lines: the aim sentence, the figure or event, the review date, and the number of consecutive days of absence that breaks the chain. Four lines, no prose, no interpretation. If those four lines are on paper, you have everything the sequence needs and nothing it does not. If you find yourself adding a fifth line about what the working is meant to achieve, that fifth line is the one that will make it fail.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why start with the ledger instead of the sigil?

Because the ledger is the only element that gives you something to measure the rest against. Without a baseline, a good month can be read as proof and a bad month as failure, and neither reading is information. Starting with numbers also forces the aim to become specific, and a specific aim is what makes the glyph worth drawing in the first place.

Is 741 actually doing anything?

No, and it is fine to use it anyway. The sum is a compression handle that makes the aim compact enough to hold in one glance, which is what the charge actually needs. Write down once that it is a conventional handle rather than a physical mechanism, and the question stops coming back every time the month goes badly.

How often should the charge be repeated?

Daily, briefly, and with the sentence said aloud under the glyph. One ten-minute glance maintains the attachment between the sentence and the shape, which is the only thing being maintained. The useful number is not the total count of charges but the longest run of consecutive days, because that is what predicts whether the working still means anything at the end of the month.

What if the ledger shows nothing at thirty days?

Then write it down as nothing and complete the third column anyway. A month with no movement and a month with movement you would have caused anyway are both valid results, and recording them is what makes the next review readable. The working is not a bet that this specific month pays out; it is a way of finding out what you do differently when you have written the sentence down.

Can I aim at a large sum?

Not on your first run, and probably not on any run until you have three completed. A large figure makes the aim unfalsifiable, because there is no way to attribute a change that size to anything you did. Start with a figure small enough that a plausible month of ordinary effort could reach it, since the sequence is what transfers and the figure does not.
