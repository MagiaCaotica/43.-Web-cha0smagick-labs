Wealth and Abundance: An Honest Chaos Magic Money Ritual

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

There is no evidence that a sigil moves money. Any honest article about money magic starts there, and the good news is that what remains after you remove that claim is still worth doing. What a sigil reliably does is commit you to one specific action, remove the friction of doubt, and keep the goal in front of you at the moment a decision becomes available. That is a behavioural mechanism, it is real, and it accounts for the majority of the results people report. Everything below is built around that: a target phrased as a command, a plain sigil, a thirty to ninety day window, a review date fixed in advance, and an honest account of the one real danger in this work, which is that tying your sense of self to a number eventually produces anxiety, compulsive checking and financial decisions made from panic rather than judgement.

Why Money Is the Favourite Target

Occult literature has used money as a target for centuries for a set of reasons that are about symbolism rather than physics. Money is universally legible, precisely quantifiable, and socially ranked. A ritual with money as its object can be described to anyone without explaining the cosmology first.

That legibility cuts both ways. It is why money sigils are popular and it is also why they are the most heavily gamed area of the practice, with an enormous volume of guaranteed-outcome material sold to people who are anxious about their finances. Anyone promising a specific amount by a specific date is selling certainty, and certainty is the one thing this method cannot deliver.

The tradition's own framing is more useful than the marketing. Abundance work is generally about opening a channel rather than pulling a specific sum, and the traditional practices attached to it are domestic, ordinary and repetitive. Keeping the ledger, titling a share of income, clearing a debt. That is what the symbolism was pointing at.

The Sigil Is a Command, Not a Wish

A wish asks the world to supply something. A command assumes you are the agent and specifies the action. Phrase a money target as a wish and the glyph becomes a decorative object; phrase it as a command and it becomes a written instruction you are obliged to follow.

The difference is concrete. I would like more money is not actionable and produces no behaviour. I send three cold proposals this week is a command, and it can be counted, and you will know on Friday whether you did it. Everything that works about money sigils is downstream of that grammatical choice.

So the target must carry a verb, a number and a date. If you cannot tell from the target alone what you will physically do on Tuesday, the target is not finished. Most disappointing results in this practice come from targets that were wishes wearing a sigil.

  1. Verb: what you will actually do
  2. Number: how many, or how much
  3. Date: the deadline that ends the window
  4. No soft language, no maybe, no almost

Spare's Reduction and Why It Endures

Austin Osman Spare formalised sigilisation in the 1900s. The method strips a statement of intent down to its consonants, arranges them, and draws the result, with the consonants then discarded. The claim was that the glyph carries the statement more durably than the sentence does.

Why it endures is not mysterious. Writing something in a form you will not be able to read later is a strong commitment device. The statement becomes unruinable by doubt, because you cannot revisit the wording and renegotiate it. That is closer to the real mechanism than anything about the glyph itself.

The variant question comes up constantly. Some practitioners keep the vowels, some use a different letter reduction, some use a cipher. These are different encodings of the same sentence and the choice is essentially aesthetic. The part that carries the effect is the act of writing a command down and destroying the source text, not the particular reduction you used.

What the Glyph Does and What You Do

Split the claim honestly. The glyph does: it fixes one specific statement, removes the option of quietly rephrasing your goal later, and gives you an object you can carry. It does not: move money, alter anyone else's decisions, or open a channel in any sense that can be measured from outside.

What you do is where the result comes from. The salience of a goal at the moment a decision becomes available is a real and measurable effect on behaviour, and a glyph is a good way to keep that goal salient without repeating it constantly. Most people who report success with money workings can describe a specific decision they made differently, and almost none can describe an outcome that had no behavioural component.

That is not a deflation. It is the difference between a practice that works and a claim that does not, and the deflation is preferable because it tells you where to spend your effort. You cannot work on the glyph. You can work on the action.

Thirty to Ninety Days as a Realistic Window

Set the window at thirty to ninety days. Faster than thirty is probably coincidence, and slower than ninety should trigger a review rather than a stronger working. A review looks at whether the target was ever specific enough to act on, which is the question most people skip.

Before the window opens, write down what you expect and by when, and put it somewhere you will not reread it daily. After the window closes, read it and write down what actually happened. Three cycles with the same style of target tells you more than any number of separate workings, and it is the only way to distinguish a method that works for you from a method that produced one coincidence.

The temptation during the window is to add workings. Do not, at least not in the first cycle. Adding a second sigil doubles your attention to the symbolic layer and halves your attention to the action, and the action is the part doing the work.

Timing Matters Less Than a Measurable Target

You can time a working. Whether you should is a different question, and the answer is that a precisely timed sigil aimed at a vague goal underperforms a plain sigil aimed at a specific measurable action this week. Timing adds a layer of structure, and structure helps when the underlying target is workable and merely distracts when it is not.

This is the point where most abundance material goes wrong. The lunar calendars, the planetary hours, the favourable dates: they are well-researched, internally consistent, and largely irrelevant to whether a proposition got sent. A technically correct timing for a wish is still a wish.

The practical order is target first, action defined, then timing if it is interesting to you. Doing it the other way round produces a beautifully executed working with nothing underneath it, and that is the most common failure pattern in this corner of the practice.

741 and Other Borrowed Numbers

The number 741 is used constantly in abundance work because seven plus four plus one equals twelve, and twelve recurs across temperament charts and numerological tables. The arithmetic is not in dispute. The significance is borrowed from a numerological framework and carried into this one, and the borrowing is rarely stated as borrowing.

Other numbers get attached the same way. Compound numbers get meanings from the Chaldean system, which was itself produced in the eighteenth century as a reference table for omen interpretation and is not ancient. It is a coherent invented framework that works well as a memorisation device, which is a legitimate use and a different claim from saying the numbers carry power.

The practical effect of a number is small and mostly real. It narrows a vague intention to a specific figure, which forces you to do arithmetic about your own situation. That arithmetic is often the first time in months a person has looked at their actual numbers, and it is often more useful than the sigil.

The Compulsion Risk and How to Bound It

The real danger in wealth work is psychological and it is specific: tying your sense of self to a result produces anxiety, compulsive checking, and financial decisions made from panic rather than judgement. People start this practice during a hard period and it becomes a way of managing distress, which is the point at which it stops being a practice and starts being a symptom.

Three bounds handle it. Fix a review date before you begin and do not open the record before it, so the checking has a scheduled slot rather than an open one. Write down what you did each week, because a written action log converts a vague feeling of effort into a countable one. And decide in advance what you will do if the number arrives faster than thirty days, because an unexpected win is the most likely route to doubling a working that should have been finished.

The stopping rule is simple. If you find yourself checking more than once a day, stop the practice and keep the log. The log is the part that has value, and the sigil is not going anywhere. If money is genuinely the thing causing you distress right now, a sigil is the wrong tool, and budgeting, or asking someone qualified, will do considerably more.

  1. Fix the review date before you begin, and do not open the record before it
  2. Write down what you did each week in a one-line log
  3. Decide in advance what happens if the result arrives inside thirty days
  4. If you check more than once a day, stop the working and keep the log
  5. If money is the source of real distress, the sigil is the wrong instrument

Frequently Asked Questions

Do money sigils actually work?

There is no evidence a sigil moves money by itself. What it reliably does is commit you to one specific action, remove the friction of doubt, and keep the goal salient when a decision becomes available. Results come from that behavioural effect plus ordinary opportunity, not from the glyph. The framing worth keeping is a sigil is a command, so it obliges you to do the thing it names.

How long should I wait for results?

Treat thirty to ninety days as a realistic observation window. Anything faster is probably coincidence. Anything slower should trigger a review of whether the target was ever specific enough to act on, rather than a stronger working. Run three cycles with the same style of target before drawing a conclusion, and do not add a second sigil during the first cycle.

Does the day I do the sigil on matter?

You can time it, and there is well-researched material on lunar cycles and planetary hours. It is secondary. A precisely timed sigil aimed at a vague goal underperforms a plain sigil aimed at a specific measurable action this week, because timing adds structure that cannot rescue a target you were not going to act on. Target first, action defined, then timing if it interests you.

Is it dangerous to make a money sigil?

The practical risk is psychological rather than metaphysical. Tying your own worth to a result can produce anxiety, compulsive checking and financial decisions made from panic. Bound it by fixing a review date in advance, keeping a weekly written log of what you did, and deciding what happens if the result arrives faster than expected. If you find yourself checking more than once a day, stop the practice and keep the log.

Does abundance work justify buying anything at all?

The apparatus is a pen, some paper and a private calendar, so the ceremony costs nothing. A paid purchase changes only the bookkeeping around your practice: generated sigil glyphs, so the reduction stops being a chore; a charge log you cannot quietly rewrite after a bad fortnight; and a thirty-day review that fires whether you feel like it or not. That is data management, not technique, and the technique is public. One payment suits a practice you expect to run repeatedly; a recurring charge would attach a bill to a log you keep anyway.
