Ancient Mythology and Speculation

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

Reading a cuneiform tablet without a teacher is more approachable than the reputation of the field suggests, mostly because the obstacles are boring rather than forbidding. What stops people is the sense that a specialist body of knowledge stands between them and the first sentence. It does not, for a tablet with a published transliteration, because the hard work has already been done by somebody and published. This piece is a route through the material: which text to start with, what the first few sessions actually involve, what the apparatus notation is doing, and what the resulting understanding is worth. The famous conclusions about ancient astronauts are not the point and are not reachable by any honest route through the tablets. The tablets are the point, and they are good.

Which text to begin with

Start with something short, dated and published in a scholarly edition with a transliteration. A single administrative tablet, or a section of a well-known composition, will teach you more than a long narrative because you can check every sign against the apparatus on the facing page.

Avoid beginning with a popular book about the tablets. The translations are good, but the framing arranges the material around a conclusion, and once you have the conclusion in your head you will read the signs to confirm it rather than to see what they say.

What the first sessions involve

Learning the sign inventory, which is a few dozen shapes you recognise on sight, and then reading a transliteration aloud and matching each element to a sign you can find in the photograph. That is genuinely the whole process, and it is a great deal less mysterious than the reputation implies.

The notation will look impenetrable for a week. The vertical wedge is determinative and marks a following sign, the horizontal wedge marks a following determinative, and a single sign can carry several meanings depending on what sits next to it. Push through that week rather than around it, because everything after it is recognition rather than decipherment.

  1. Learn the sign inventory from a chart you can keep beside you.
  2. Read a transliteration aloud, slowly, twice.
  3. Match each element to a sign on the photograph.
  4. Write the transliteration out by hand at least once.

The apparatus, which is the key to everything

A scholarly edition prints the sign, its transliteration, its meaning, and a translation, with notes underneath. Those notes are where the argument lives, and skipping them is the mistake that turns a reader into a collector of fragments.

Where a sign is damaged, the note will tell you how many signs are missing and what the parallel text reads. Where the reading is uncertain, it will say so and give the alternatives. This is the machinery that lets you have an opinion without pretending to more certainty than the evidence supports.

A sequence you can actually complete

Week one, learn the sign inventory and read a transliteration without the translation. Week two, read it with the translation and notice where you would have guessed differently. Week three, take a second text on the same subject and see where the two editions disagree.

Week four, read one modern scholarly article about something you have now read the primary source of. The article will be hard and half of it will be unfollowable and that is fine, because the parts you cannot follow are the parts where you will learn the most in the following year.

  1. One week on signs and an untranslated reading.
  2. One week comparing your guess with the translation.
  3. One week on a parallel text and its disagreements.
  4. One week on a modern article about what you have read.

Where the argument is, rather than the answer

The interesting part of this field is not what the tablets say about the beginning of things. It is where competent scholars disagree about a damaged sign, and what each reading would imply for the rest of the composition.

That kind of disagreement is a gift. A settled question is worth nothing to a learner, and a contested one gives you somewhere to stand and something to change your mind about. The flood story that interrupts the Sumerian King List is a good example: the structural oddity is the interesting part, and the various explanations for it are better material than any conclusion about what the flood was.

The flood, the list and the interruption

The Sumerian King List sets out a sequence of kings and then, without warning, records a flood and begins again with a different line. Nobody in the composition explains it, and several explanations have been proposed since including deliberate historical memory and scribal crisis.

This is the sort of detail that survives a thousand arguments because the evidence for it is structural rather than interpretive. If you can see it in the arrangement of the text, you have found something. That is a very different experience from reading a claim in a popular book and trying to decide whether to believe it.

The translation as an argument

Every English rendering of a cuneiform text is somebody's decision, and the decisions matter most exactly where the interesting things are. A god's name might be rendered as a proper name, a descriptive epithet, or an explanatory phrase, and each choice pushes the meaning somewhere different.

Comparing two translations of the same passage and asking why they diverge is the useful thing for a beginner is comparing two translations of the same passage, and it holds two editions. Most disagreements turn out to be conservatism rather than conflict, but the ones that are not are where the scholarship lives.

What the material is good for, practically

The Mesopotamian ritual corpus is technical, procedural and largely indifferent to your beliefs. Foundation rituals, boundary constructions, offerings, the correct sequence of an invocation, the words said at the laying of a brick. All of it is preserved and all of it can be used without a conversion.

That is a rare property in an ancient corpus and it is the most honest reason to spend a month on it. There is no confession required and nothing to believe, only a procedure and a text that tells you what the procedure was. The historical interest is a bonus on top of work you could have done with it.

How to keep going after the first month

Choose a narrow question and stay with it. Signs for a particular material, or a single period, or one category of text. Breadth at this stage produces the feeling of having surveyed a field without acquiring any of it.

Then read one scholar you disagree with. Not to reach their conclusion, but because the shape of a serious opposing argument teaches you more about the field than a survey ever will, and because being able to state the best case against your own view is the thing that separates a reader from a collector.

Whether a digital tool helps here

A tablet is one of the few ancient objects you cannot usefully approach from a screen, because the sign order and surface are exactly the information that matters and both are flattened by a photograph. For most purposes a printed plate is better than any viewer.

What a digital tool is genuinely good for is the reference layer. Sign lists, vocabularies and searchable corpora are worth having and are frequently free, and they remove exactly the drudgery that makes beginners quit. Nothing on a screen replaces having the object, and nothing needs to.

The honest summary

The ancient astronaut reading is not supported by anything you will find in a scholarly edition, and a month of honest reading will show you why, which is more convincing than any argument. The material underneath is genuinely fascinating and genuinely accessible, and it is a real historical record rather than a story about visitors.

What you end up with after a month is not an answer about the origin of humanity. It is a working reading of a dead language, an awareness of which questions are open, and one text you can actually take apart. That is a better outcome than the one the videos promise.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which tablet should a beginner translate first?

Something short, dated and published in a scholarly edition that includes a transliteration and an apparatus of notes. A single administrative document is ideal because every sign has a checkable counterpart on the facing page. Do not start with a narrative text, and do not start with a book about the tablets, since the framing will bias what you see in the signs.

How long before I can read anything on my own?

A few weeks for the basics, using a published transliteration, and the honest version of being able to read is recognising signs on sight rather than deciphering them. Six months of regular practice will get you to the point where an edition with notes is usable without constant reference. Nobody reaches fluency, which is a relief as much as a disappointment.

What is the apparatus notation doing?

The vertical wedge marks a following determinative and the horizontal wedge marks a following determinative, and they exist to tell you which reading of a multi-meaning sign applies. The notes underneath give damaged signs, missing signs, alternative readings and cross-references to parallel texts. Those notes are the argument, and skipping them is how a reader ends up collecting fragments instead of understanding a composition.

Can a tablet actually show signs of an extraterrestrial origin?

The tablets are a written administrative and literary record in a language with a long deciphered tradition, and nothing in them functions as a report of visitors. Any reading along those lines requires reading a divine epithet as a place name and a creation poem as a technical report. Reading a year of the primary sources shows why far more convincingly than any secondary argument against it.

What does buying a cuneiform tool or a text bundle actually get me?

A reference layer, which is genuinely useful: sign lists, vocabularies and searchable corpora remove the drudgery that makes beginners quit, and much of it is free through libraries. A printed critical edition is the purchase that pays for itself if you will read it more than once. A digital tablet viewer is the least useful option, because the sign order is the information that matters and a screen flattens it.
