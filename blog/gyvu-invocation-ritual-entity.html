GYVU

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

GYVU does not appear in the Ars Goetia, in the lesser keys, or in any of the grimoire lists most working practitioners actually consult. It is a coined handle, and treating it as a private name rather than a documented spirit is the first decision the working requires. What follows is an operational sequence rather than a theory: a distinction that matters, a preparation stage with a defined end, an invocation with language chosen in advance, a single question, a shutdown, and a grounding practice that decides whether any of this is repeatable. The classical sources distinguish two things that modern usage has merged, and separating them is what makes a working with a name like this defensible rather than merely theatrical.

Nothing is recorded under GYVU

The named lists are finite and checkable. The seventy-two spirits of the Goetia are enumerated with rank, office and form. The lesser keys of Solomon and the pseudepigrapha material have their own rosters, including names that surface constantly in online working circles. GYVU is not in any of them, and it does not correspond to a documented name in a dead language that was transliterated into something resembling it.

So the honest position is that you coined it, or adopted it from a coinage, and it functions as a handle for a fixed intention. That is a legitimate use. Spare's whole technique depends on privately named symbols acquiring force through repetition, and a name attached to a working is no less functional for having no ancestor. What would not be legitimate is inventing a biography, a lineage or an office for GYVU in order to make the working feel better sourced than it is.

Calling and inviting are different operations

Classical ceremonial usage keeps two words apart. Evocation means calling something to appear, in a circle, with the operator positioned outside or contained within a defined boundary, and the entity is treated as present and separate. Invocation means inviting the influence to operate through the operator, closer to how a working with a deity or an archangel was described in some of the same literatures. The evocation implies a distance and an appearance; the invocation implies a channel.

Most contemporary writing collapses the pair and uses both words loosely. For a working with an undocumented name the collapse is convenient, because there is no independent entity to appear. The operation is a channel exercise with a fixed structure, and calling it an invocation describes what is actually happening more precisely. If you want the classical evocation instead, you need a documented target with a described form, and GYVU is not one.

What the catalogues give you and why the absence matters

The value of a documented entity list is that it is decision-complete. The Goetia, for all its grim framing, tells you rank, visible appearance, office and specific powers for each of its seventy-two, which removes the guesswork from the design of a working. You are not inventing an effect; you are selecting one that was described to you by a source you can inspect.

That structure is the argument for working inside documented catalogues rather than inventing names, and the argument is practical rather than traditionalist. A documented target gives you a specification. An invented target gives you an intention you have already written yourself, and you can do that directly, without the intermediate fiction of a spirit you have corresponded with. Naming GYVU does not add information. It adds a sound to refer to, which some people find useful and some find ornamental.

Preparation, with a defined end

Stage one is a written statement of what the working is for, in one sentence, using an imperative verb. Not a description of the effect you would like, but a command the working will attempt. Write it before anything else, because a working whose aim drifts during the ritual is a working with no aim, and the drift is usually toward whatever you were worried about that afternoon.

Stage two is the reduction of that sentence to a glyph, following the ordinary sigil technique: vowels removed, repeated letters collapsed, remaining letters assigned shapes, duplicates dropped. Stage three is setting the date. Pick a date at least a week out so the working has a future tense, then close the notebook. Having an end condition written down is what stops a working from becoming an open-ended mood that lasts for years.

The invocation, in language fixed beforehand

On the working day, sit somewhere you will not be interrupted, with the statement and the glyph in front of you. Read the statement aloud once. Then address the name twice, using the same wording both times, and state what you are invoking it to do. Keep it short and operational. The text that works is the text you wrote in advance, not something composed in the moment, because improvisation at this stage produces vagueness and vagueness produces nothing you can evaluate later.

Then stop speaking. This is where most workings are abandoned. Sit for twenty minutes without filling the silence with commentary about what might be happening. If a sensation arrives, note it in one word and keep going. If an image arrives, note it in one word and keep going. The purpose of the silence is not to receive something; it is to find out whether anything arrives at all when the pressure to produce something is removed.

One question, asked once

A working that asks three questions gets nothing distinguishable, because any outcome can be read as an answer to any of them. Restrict the session to a single question, ideally one with a yes-or-no shape or a small numeric range, and write it down before you begin. If you cannot phrase it that way, that is a sign the aim is still too broad, and the correct move is to go back to stage one and narrow it.

Divination after the silence is optional and should be recorded either way. A yes-or-no draw, a small dice throw, whatever instrument you already use, gives you a datum independent of your expectation during the working. Recording a null result is as valuable as recording a hit, because it is the only part of the process that can accumulate into an honest record.

Closing, and not skipping it

Stage four is the shutdown, and it is the stage that gets dropped. State plainly that the working is closed, that the question is withdrawn, and that nothing remains open between you and whatever the name refers to. Read the statement once more as a receipt. Put the glyph somewhere deliberate rather than back in a drawer with everything else, because a deliberate location is what makes it possible to check it later.

This closing has a function beyond tidiness. It establishes that the channel is shut rather than left ajar, which is what makes a second working with the same name safe to attempt. A working left open tends to leak into unrelated days, and people interpret the leak as evidence the working is still active. It is more likely that nothing was ever closed.

Grounding, which is the actual discipline

Afterwards, ground before you do anything else. Physical movement, then something ordinary and sensory: washing a dish, walking a familiar route, writing the date and a one-line account of the session. The grounding is not a formality added to protect you from spirits. It is the procedure that keeps a private symbol from taking over a disproportionate share of attention, which is the failure mode people actually report after months of intensive working with named entities.

Watch for the specific signals that the practice has become a problem: checking the glyph compulsively, experiencing ordinary setbacks as messages from the name, or reorganising your life around the next working. Any one of those is a reason to stop for a month and do ordinary creative work with no symbolism in it at all. A channel metaphor you can switch off when it is inconvenient is a metaphor doing its job.

Reading the result honestly

Judge the working the way you would judge any other experiment you ran once. Ask what you expected, what happened, and whether anything you observed would have happened anyway. The answer to the third question is almost always yes, and acknowledging that is not cynicism, it is calibration. A single invocation with a coined name and no external verification has essentially no evidential weight, and treating it as though it does is how practitioners end up with decades of unexamined impressions.

The use of a working like this is not to obtain information from GYVU. It is to run a fixed sequence under defined conditions and find out whether you can repeat it without cost. That is a real skill, it is learnable, and it does not require you to believe anything about the name to acquire it. If you later want the classical operation, choose a documented spirit with a described form and do the same sequence again.

Whether to use a servitor instead

A servitor is a different construction with a different risk profile. It is usually a piece of designed material, often a sigil given a task and a limited vocabulary, and it is understood to be an aspect of the practitioner's own pattern rather than an independent intelligence. That framing removes most of the hazard without requiring you to abandon the practice, because nothing in it claims a separate will.

If your interest is the technique of focused intention, the servitor route will teach you the same things more cheaply and with fewer interpretive problems. If your interest is specifically the entity correspondence format, then a documented catalogue entry is the version of that format with a specification attached, and a coined name is the version without. Both are defensible; they are simply different experiments, and mixing them produces confusion rather than depth.

What to keep from a first working

Keep the written statement, the glyph, the date and the one-word notes. That is four lines, and in a year it is the difference between a practice with a record and a practice with a memory. The record will tell you what actually happened, and memory will tell you what you hoped happened, and the two diverge faster than most practitioners notice.

You will also keep the sense that the whole thing was thin. That sensation is correct and it is not a failure of faith. A coined name carries no information, and a single session produces one data point. What the sequence can deliver is a repeatable structure you can apply to something documented, and that is where the durable value sits.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is GYVU a real spirit or an invented name?

It is an invented name with no catalogue entry in the Goetia, the lesser keys or any other standard list. It is usable as a private handle for a fixed intention. Inventing a biography or an attributed office for it would be fabrication, and the working does not need one to function.

What is the difference between invocation and evocation here?

Calling something to appear in a circle is evocation, and it presupposes an entity with a describable form. Inviting an influence to operate through the operator is invocation. With a coined name there is nothing to appear, so the operation is a channel exercise and invocation is the accurate word.

How long does the working take?

Twenty minutes of silence in the middle, with maybe five minutes of speaking on either side of it. Total time including preparation and grounding is under an hour. The twenty minutes is the part that cannot be shortened, because it is the part where anything might arrive.

What if I feel nothing during the silence?

Record a null and close the working as planned. A null is a real datum and it is the one that most improves a practice, because it shows you whether the sequence is doing anything beyond the expectation that you bring to it.

Is it safer to work with a documented spirit instead?

For some people yes, because a documented entry supplies a specification rather than requiring you to write the intention yourself. For others the servitor route is safer still, since it carries no claim of independent intelligence. Neither is required for the technique, and the risk in any of these is behavioural rather than metaphysical.
