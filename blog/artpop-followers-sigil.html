A Sigil for Followers: Why Reach Is the Wrong Target

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 6 min read

The request arrives in the shape of a sigil for followers, and the honest answer is that this is the least effective possible target for the method. It is unmeasurable in the short term, it rewards you for nothing you control, and it produces a number that does not convert into anything. Follower count has become an extraordinarily poor predictor of revenue, and it is a poor predictor of anything else too. That is not an argument against making sigils about creative work. It is an argument for pointing them at the part of the pipeline you actually control: the output schedule. Recommendation systems on every major platform weight retention and completion far more heavily than follower totals, which means the same post sent consistently outperforms a better post sent erratically. A sigil is well suited to that, because a sigil is a commitment device dressed in a glyph. The other half of the answer is visual. Chaos magic has always had a practice of deriving related glyphs from a shared root so a body of work reads as one body of work. That practice maps directly onto brand systems, and it is the reason the aesthetic has become so visible among people who do this publicly.

Followers Are a Vanity Metric

A follower is a person who clicked once, on a platform that has no way of knowing whether they will ever see anything else you make. It is a count of past curiosity, and past curiosity does not compound.

The number everyone wishes they had instead is the number of people who finish one piece and come back for the next. That is a conversion, it is measurable weekly, and it is the only reach metric that predicts revenue with any consistency across niches.

If you want a sigil that changes a number, aim it at the conversion. If you want a sigil that changes how you work, aim it at the schedule. Both are honest. A sigil for follower count is neither, because nothing about casting one changes either.

What Recommendation Systems Actually Reward

Platforms distribute content based on predicted engagement per impression, and the inputs they weight most heavily are completion rate and return rate. A piece that holds attention and brings people back is worth more to the system than a piece with a large audience.

This is why the same creator with 4,000 engaged followers out-earns one with 60,000 passive ones, and it happens often enough to be a rule rather than an anecdote. The small account has a higher percentage of its viewers arriving from a single piece, which the system reads as a strong signal.

None of this is secret and none of it is mystical. It means the sigil target that matters is the one your behaviour controls, which is when you post and whether the piece is finished.

One Visual Root, Many Derivatives

Austin Osman Spare described deriving related sigils from a common reduction so a practitioner accumulates a recognisable visual identity across many operations. The logic is a brand system's logic, and it predates the software that eventually made brands necessary.

Do not derive one sigil for every platform from scratch. Reduce a single visual root, then vary it deliberately for each destination, keeping enough shared structure that someone who has seen two of them recognises the third. That consistency is what people mean when they say a creator looks serious.

The derivative is not a copy with a variation bolted on. It is the same grammar producing a different sentence, and the discipline is keeping the grammar intact while the sentence changes.

Charging a Sigil Aimed at Output

Write the imperative as a command that names a countable action, not an outcome. The statement is the working part of the whole operation, and a statement you cannot check has given you nothing to commit to.

Then charge it the way you charge any sigil, and the method genuinely matters less than the state you are in. Gaze until the glyph stops being a shape. Trace it. Let the charge end when your attention lapses rather than when a timer rings.

Afterwards the forget operation matters more than the charge. The point of casting a sigil for a publishing habit is that you stop thinking about the habit and start doing it, which means the forget has to actually take.

The Meme Case: Shared Symbols as Practice

The KEK archetype is the clearest live example of a symbolic object doing real psychological work across thousands of unrelated people who have never met. Nobody in that movement is running a grimoire, and the reinforcement is entirely social.

It is worth taking seriously for a reason that has nothing to do with the occult: it is a clean demonstration that a shared symbol, repeated in a community, acquires meaning that no individual put there. Whatever is happening there, the observation about collective reinforcement is solid.

The practical transfer to your own work is consistency. A visual root, repeated across every piece, produces the same effect at small scale, without requiring anything supernatural to be true.

Why Aesthetic Consistency Reads as Serious

In a saturated field, the visible proof of practice is the practice itself. Nobody can check whether your sigils work, but everybody can see whether you have thirty of them or two.

This is why the aesthetic has become so pronounced among public practitioners. It is not deception; it is the only externally legible evidence available in a domain where outcomes are private and often unverifiable.

The failure mode is spending the effort on the glyphs and none on the output. A beautiful root with an irregular publishing record reads as a hobby. A rough root with a daily record reads as a professional.

Turning Sigil Work Into a Publishing Habit

The mechanism here is unusually well supported. Commitment devices work when the commitment is public, specific, and costly enough to be embarrassing to break. A sigil supplies all three properties if you use it properly.

Write the schedule into the statement, not just the activity. A command that names the day and the time produces a different person on a Tuesday morning than one that says publish regularly.

Log each cast against each completed piece. A month of that log is worth more than any amount of theorising about whether sigils work, because it is your own data about your own behaviour.

When Visibility Becomes the Problem

There is a documented risk in making a visibility practice central, and it is worth stating plainly. Fixity on being seen, checked, or measured is a recognised warning sign in the same literature that describes obsessive practice in body and appearance work.

The marker is compulsive checking. If you are casting sigils for reach and then measuring reach more than once a day, the practice has stopped being about the work and started being about the number.

The fix is the same one that works elsewhere: fix an audit date, write down what you did, and if the checking has not declined by then, reduce the intensity or stop. A creative practice that costs you sleep is not paying.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a sigil actually get me followers?

Not by any mechanism we can demonstrate. What it can do is commit you to a specific publishing rhythm, and a consistent rhythm is the single strongest lever on every recommendation system. The result comes from the behaviour plus ordinary opportunity, not from the glyph.

Should I use one sigil across all my platforms?

No. Derive one sigil per platform from a shared visual root, which is precisely the derivative practice Spare described for a body of work. The shared structure gives you recognition; the variation gives you the flexibility that a single rigid mark does not.

Why do magic influencers lean so hard on the aesthetic?

Because in a practice with private and often unverifiable outcomes, visual consistency is the only evidence other people can check. It also happens to be the only part of the work a viewer can evaluate, so it carries disproportionate weight in how serious you read.

What should a sigil for creative work actually say?

A command naming a countable action rather than a result. Four pieces published this week beats grow the audience, because you can verify the first one on Sunday and you cannot verify the second at all.

Is there a paid tool that helps with this, or is it all manual?

Deriving the glyphs is fully manual and quick. What consumes real time is the log of which cast went out when and what it produced, and that is bookkeeping rather than ritual, so a spreadsheet handles it. The paid side of this work is rarely where the value sits.
