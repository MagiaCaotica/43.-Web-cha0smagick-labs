CAOTIZADOR

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

A coined name has one advantage over a catalogued one and it is a real advantage: nobody can tell you that you have got the details wrong, because there are no details to get wrong. That is also the problem. When you build an invocation around a word you invented, four of the fields normally filled from a reference have to come from you, and three of them are decoration. This is an account of which field actually does the work, what a first evening can honestly claim, and the three ways these workings fail quietly enough that you will not notice for a month.

A name that is only a sound

Most working names in this area were made up. The catalogues are finite and heavily used, and the coinages are what happens when a practitioner needs a name for a job that no existing entry covers. There is nothing wrong with that. The name is a handle, and a handle does not need a biography. What a handle does need is for you to know that it is a handle.

The failure starts when the handle acquires a character. If you have begun to hear a personality in the name, ask where it came from. Most of the time the answer is your own expectation, projected onto a sound, and the expectation then does real work in the working by making you attentive to anything that confirms it. That is a mechanism worth knowing about, because it operates whether or not you intend it.

Why a coinage cannot be wrong about itself

A catalogued entity can be checked. The reference tells you a rank, a form, an office and a set of powers, and if your working contradicts those, you have made an error. A coinage offers no such resistance. Whatever you write in the fields becomes true by definition, which makes the reference a source of friction that the coinage has conveniently removed.

Friction is worth something. The one field that can be tested on a coinage is the office, because the office is a description of a function, and a function can be checked against your own behaviour. Rank cannot be checked, because a rank is a claim about standing. Form can be drawn but not verified. Powers are, in a coinage, entirely borrowed language with your name attached, and treating that as information is the most common overreach in this form of working.

The four fields, and which of them you invented

With a name from a catalogue, four fields arrive pre-filled. With a coinage, all four are yours, and they are not equal. Rank is decorative. Form is a drawing you will look at, and its value is in giving the attention somewhere to land rather than in describing anything. Powers are borrowed, and the borrowing is honest as long as you label it. Office is the field that carries the working, because it is the only one that describes something you could do.

Writing that out changes what you do. If you know that three of the four fields are your own invention, you spend your time on the fourth. Most of the time people spend on this sort of working goes into the form and the powers, because those are the parts that feel like the practice, and the part that actually determines whether anything happens is the one that reads like a job description.

The office is the only part that can be tested

An office stated in a way that can be tested looks like a verb phrase naming a behaviour with a boundary attached. Answer three mail a day by four. Read for twenty minutes before anything else. Stop checking the thing you have been checking hourly. These are checkable because you and someone else can agree on whether they happened.

An office stated as a state cannot be tested. Be more decisive. Be clear about what I want. Improve my situation. These read as aims and function as weather. A working built on one of them cannot fail, and a thing that cannot fail cannot teach you anything, which is the whole reason to keep a record.

What the catalogue lends and what it hides

Working with a catalogued entry has an obvious benefit and one less obvious cost. The benefit is a description you did not write, which means you can disagree with it. The cost is that the description is itself a product of a particular medieval and early-modern context, and its terms carry assumptions about standing, office and service that do not transfer cleanly to what you actually want.

Modern practice has largely dissolved that frame, treating entities as independent pattern fragments that can be borrowed to interrupt a repetitive thought. That is a defensible move and it changes what the catalogue is for. You are no longer petitioning an official. You are using a description as a focusing device, and once you have made that shift the borrowed language should be marked as borrowed, because it is no longer being taken literally and pretending otherwise only adds confusion.

Carroll's version, and its practical cost

The influential modern position is that entities are aspects of the practitioner's own mind. It is attractive because it removes the risk without requiring you to be a sceptic, and it is more honest than either a literal reading or a dismissive one. As a working stance it is often enough.

The cost is that it makes the entity's independence unavailable to you, and some of the best parts of this practice depend on that independence. Having a description that is not yours gives you something to push against, and the friction is part of what produces attention. Fully deflationary readings lose the friction. The middle position is to use the description as a borrowed focusing device, keep the deflationary view as your private account of what is happening, and never present the borrowing as information.

The first evening, and what you can honestly claim

On the first evening you can honestly claim almost nothing about the name. What you can claim is that you set down an office in a sentence, drew a form to look at, held attention on a glyph for as long as you could manage, and wrote down what you intended. That is a complete account of what happened. It is a thin claim about the world and a solid one about the evening.

The temptation on the first evening is to claim more, because something usually happens in the first hour. Something always happens in the first hour, because you have just spent twenty minutes concentrating on a novel object, and the resulting state of unusual attention will produce sensations, images and coincidences. None of that is a result. It is a change in attention, and the honest thing is to write it down as one.

The three ways these workings fail quietly

The first is the office that is too wide. You write something like clearing a persistent obstacle, and no action could count as doing it, so nothing can be recorded either way. The second is the charge that was never held. You drew the glyph, you looked at it, and you went back to what you were doing, and the whole apparatus depends on attention that was not there. The third is the slow one, where the name acquires a biography through repetition and starts generating answers to questions you did not ask.

None of the three produces an obvious failure signal. A wide office cannot fail. A missing charge feels like nothing happened. An inflating name feels like insight. This is why the record matters so much in this form of working, and why the record needs to be written in a form you did not design after the fact.

A second charge with a narrower office

When the first month produces nothing you can point at, the response is to narrow the office rather than to charge harder. Take the wide aim and find the smallest behaviour inside it that you could do on a bad day. If the office was clearing an obstacle, the narrower version might be one specific conversation or one specific document. Then charge again with that.

This is worth doing formally, by writing down the first version and the second and keeping both. The exercise is more useful than the second charge, because the gap between the two versions is usually where your actual uncertainty was. Most people discover that the first office was written to avoid naming a thing they did not want to do, which is worth knowing before the third version.

Where the framework starts producing answers

There is a point in this kind of practice where the structure starts generating material faster than you are checking it. You find yourself interpreting small events, drawing connections between the name and events in your life, and feeling the fit tighten. That tightening is pleasant and it is also the signature of a system that has started confirming itself.

The check is simple and it works: write down the next three things that happen that you would have noticed anyway, and mark whether you connected them to the name. If the answer is more than one, the framework is running. Reduce it to fewer connections per week as a deliberate constraint. The constraint feels wrong at first and it is the cheapest protection available.

The entry worth keeping

Of everything you write for a coined name, one entry earns its place. It is the office, written narrowly enough to fail, together with the date you would review it and a column recording what you would have done in any case. Everything else you write around the name is, in the end, material for the attention to rest on, which is a real function but not the one you will point to later.

If you keep only that entry and the drawings, you have kept the working. If you keep the biography and drop the entry, you have kept the part that cannot be wrong and lost the part that can teach you anything.

The name, and what you owe it

A name you invented has no one else's interpretation to respect, which frees you, and it also means nobody is holding you to anything. That obligation runs outward rather than inward. If you write about the working, someone will read it and take it as a description of how the name operates, and the borrowed language will have been quietly upgraded into a claim by the time it reaches them.

So the debt is to mark the borrowing. Say that the rank and the powers are yours and not from any source, that the form is a drawing and not a likeness, and that the office is the only part with a claim on the world. Three sentences. Once you have written them, the working can be described honestly, which is the condition for it being worth doing again.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I work with a spirit name I invented myself?

Yes, and many practitioners do. A coined name is a handle rather than a description, which frees you from having to reconcile it with a reference. The requirement is that you know which parts are yours: the rank and the powers are decoration you supplied, the form is a drawing to focus attention, and the office is the only field that describes something you could be observed doing.

What is the difference between evocation and invocation?

Evocation calls something to appear. Invocation asks its influence to operate through the operator. Most modern usage flattens the two into a single act, which loses the useful part: under invocation, the changes you should be watching for are in your own behaviour rather than in something arriving in the room, and that is a difference you can record.

Why did nothing happen after a month?

Three explanations, and from inside they are hard to tell apart. The office may have been too wide to fail, the charge may never have been held because you drew the glyph and moved on, or the name may simply have nothing behind it. The narrowing test is the useful one: rewrite the office as the smallest behaviour you could do on a bad day, charge again, and see whether the second version behaves differently from the first.

Is it better to use a catalogued entity or an invented name?

A catalogued entry gives you a description you did not write, which means you can disagree with it and there is something to push against. An invented name has no such friction. Neither is better. The advantage of the catalogue is that the office can be tested against something external, and if you use an invented name the office has to carry that weight on its own.

How do I know when a name is inflating?

Watch for the point where small unrelated events start being connected to the name, and where you find yourself answering questions you did not pose. That tightening is pleasant and it is the signature of a self-confirming system. The countermeasure is a constraint: write down the next three things you would have noticed anyway, and limit yourself to one connection per week for a while.
