Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Chaos magic is the only occult tradition that built its own epistemology into the founding documents. Peter Carroll and John Crowley both wrote, plainly, that you are not supposed to inherit anybody's claims, including theirs. That promise is the tradition's real contribution and it is also the part people are most comfortable ignoring. This page sets out where the movement actually fails, because a method that promises you may test its claims and then discourages you from doing so is not offering you a method. It is offering you a permission slip. The sections below cover the four places the criticism lands hardest: the founding texts themselves, the way results get reported, the habits that make self-deception easy, and the boundary between discipline and obsession. It is written for practitioners who have already started and want to know which parts of their practice are load-bearing.

The founding texts promised more than the movement delivers

Read Liber Null and psi next to almost anything else in the field and the difference is stark. Carroll tells you to test, to write things down, to accept that you might be wrong. The surrounding material asks you to accept. The promise is not that chaos magic works better, only that it is honest about what it does not know.

The movement has been very good at the first half of that and consistently bad at the second. A published null result is close to unheard of. Practitioners who run a series and get nothing are far more likely to quietly change the target than to write it up. The gap between the founding instruction and the observed behaviour is the single most damning fact about the tradition, and it is not resolvable by insisting that the founders meant something else.

Results get reported backwards, and that is not an accident

There is a systematic reason nulls do not get published. A sigil that produces nothing is boring. A sigil that coincides with a good week is a story, and stories circulate, get commented on, get re-told with the details adjusted slightly each time. The distribution of published accounts is therefore skewed by the mechanics of social transmission rather than by anything about magic.

This is not fraud. It is the ordinary behaviour of humans retelling favourable events, and the fact that it operates here does not tell you whether the original claim was true. It tells you that the published evidence base is worth close to nothing on its own, and that only a private record kept by the practitioner can do any work at all.

The habit that makes self-deception easy

The core habit is naming the target after the result rather than before it. A sigil for more money is safe, because more money can mean anything at all. A sigil for a specific figure by a specific date sets up a test, and a test can be failed, which is exactly the property that makes people avoid writing it down in that form.

The second habit is retroactive revision of the intent. The statement you reduce to a glyph is often written after the disappointing week, when you already know what you wish had happened. Nothing about sigilisation prevents this. The only defence is to write the sentence, seal it, and date it before charging anything, which is boring and works.

Confirmation bias is the mechanism, not the excuse

Confirmation bias is well documented and it is not a personal failing. It is what a prediction system does when it has been handed a rule that can be satisfied by almost any outcome. If the sigil counts as working when the goal is approached, missed when the goal is abandoned, and irrelevant when the goal was wrong anyway, then the rule is satisfied by the world regardless of whether the glyph did anything.

This is the argument for narrow targets, and it is stronger than practitioners usually let it be. A specific, dated, externally checkable prediction forces the mechanism to survive a condition it cannot be talked out of. The tradition has that tool available. It is used rarely, because it is uncomfortable.

Where the discipline stops being useful

There is a real cost to the testing habit that gets mentioned rarely. A practice built around pre-registered predictions is an instrument pointed at your own life, and instruments like that can be worn. The log becomes a place where you score yourself, the scoring becomes the activity, and the original goal recedes behind the apparatus built to serve it.

The distinguishing sign is not the amount of record-keeping. It is what the record is for. A record kept so you can learn is a tool. A record kept so you can prove you were right is a mirror with a score attached, and the two feel identical from the inside. If reading your own log produces a small lift in self-regard rather than a clearer picture, the instrument has been repurposed.

  1. Sleep disruption is the first and most common warning sign in this material.
  2. Withdrawal from people is second, and it is usually disguised as focus.
  3. Ritual starts crowding out the ordinary duties the ritual was meant to serve.
  4. The log gets edited rather than written, which is the clearest tell of all.

What the founders would say if asked about the failure rate

Carroll's own answer, in effect, is that nobody knows, and that the point is to find out rather than to be reassured. He assumed people would run the experiment and report honestly, and the assumption was reasonable about the person and optimistic about the species. Crowley, in a different register, assumed the problem was the audience rather than the practitioner.

Neither of them imagined the modern arrangement, where the same sigil is recommended to several hundred thousand people and every one of them is being asked to believe their own version worked. That is a social problem, and it has no solution inside the method. The method has to be applied individually, at small scale, with an honest log, or it becomes something else.

Three claims worth checking before you commit to any of it

Any statement in this field that makes a prediction about money, health or other people should be treated as a claim to be checked rather than a method to be applied. The checking is cheap and the practice of checking is what separates a method from a superstition.

Where the sources are old, check the source. Where they are recent, check the date. Where they are personal, check whether the person is describing a result or describing a practice. Most bad material fails one of those three tests, and running them takes less time than reading another chapter.

  • Does the claim name something that could have gone the other way?
  • Is the target specific enough that failure would be visible?
  • Does the author record what did not work anywhere in the account?

A working version that survives its own criteria

The version worth keeping is small. One statement of intent, written in the imperative, with a number and a date in it. One reduction, done by hand. One charge, short. One log entry, written the same day, including the parts you do not like. One review, on the date you fixed before the working rather than on a date chosen afterwards.

Run that on a single question for a season and then read the log without interpretation. Whatever the log shows, it shows. That is the whole method, and it is more than most people actually do.

The honest summary of the limits

There is no evidence that sigils influence external reality. There is clear evidence that writing a specific, dated, externally checkable statement changes what a person does next, which is not nothing and is not what the claims say. The tradition's own best idea is that you must find out for yourself, and its most common failure is that almost nobody does.

Anyone who tells you the method is guaranteed is selling something. Anyone who tells you it is a total placebo is also selling something, and the honest position is narrower and more useful than either. Test the small claim. Keep the record. Believe what the record supports, which will be less than you want and more than nothing.

Where the criticism lands hardest, and who has noticed

The strongest objection to this field is not that it is fraudulent, which is a charge almost nobody has made seriously, but that it is structurally unable to produce knowledge even when the practitioners involved are entirely honest. Both halves of that matter and the second is the harder one.

A method that depends on a private, unshared experience producing a decision that then changes nothing observable will drift, over time, toward whatever its adherents already believed. The drift is invisible from the inside, which is why it happens, and it is the reason the instruction to write things down has to be enforced by the practitioner and by nobody else.

The people who notice this are almost always the ones with the least incentive to carry on. A practitioner whose results are small and mixed can think clearly about the method. A practitioner whose results are large and consistent has no reason to ask whether the measurement was any good at all.

What the two founding texts actually promise

Peter Carroll's short pamphlet and John Crowley's Liber Kaos agree on almost nothing about the nature of the world and agree completely about method. Read either and the instruction is the same: treat your own claim as a hypothesis, decide in advance what would count against it, and keep the record.

That is a modest programme and a defensible one, and it is a real contrast with everything around it. What makes it unusual is not the ambition, which is small, but the willingness to make the whole thing falsifiable at the level of a single private decision about a single afternoon.

The gap between that programme and the practice that followed is the most interesting object of study in this area, and nobody has done it systematically. A serious history of how a method promising measurement came to be discussed almost entirely in terms of results would be worth more than another fifty accounts of individual workings.

A record that is capable of disagreeing with you

Six fields, and the sixth is the one everybody leaves out. Date, target sentence, charging date, the pre-registered prediction, the deadline, and what actually happened, including the parts that do not fit.

The sixth field is what separates a record from a diary. Without it, everything passes through the mood of whoever is writing at the time, and a log kept over several months ends up being a log of changing moods rather than of changing circumstances.

  • One line per working, written in the evening on the day it happened.
  • Every prediction dated before the charge, and never revised afterwards.
  • Misses recorded in the same hand and the same tone as the hits.
  • A note whenever the reading changed after the deadline had already passed.

How to run a defensible season of this

Pick one question, small enough that the answer is checkable by somebody who believes in none of it. Pre-register the outcome and the date. Run four weekly workings with an uncharged control on the intervening days, so the comparison lives inside your own record rather than inside your memory.

Review at the end of the month, once, and write the conclusion before re-reading the individual entries. That ordering matters more than it sounds. Reading first and concluding second lets each entry negotiate with the conclusion, and that negotiation is where most honest-seeming records quietly go wrong.

The whole exercise takes about an hour a week including the writing, and it will produce a null more often than not. That is the expected value and it is no reason to change the method. It is a reason to keep running it long enough for the nulls to average out.

The two things this tradition is genuinely good at

The first is that it makes the practitioner answerable to themselves. Very little else in the esoteric field asks you to write down what you did and then read it back, and the discipline of doing so produces better results than the technique, which is an awkward thing for a system built on techniques to have to admit.

The second is that it lowers the cost of being wrong. There is no membership, no initiation, no fee and no authority whose permission is required, so the entire downside of a failed working is a wasted afternoon. Few traditions manage that, and the absence of stakes is what makes honest testing possible in the first place.

Both of those survive even if every metaphysical claim in the corpus turns out to be wrong, which is the strongest practical argument for the method and the one almost never made aloud. A practice that makes you more accountable to your own attention is worth something regardless of what the attention reaches.

Frequently Asked Questions

If the founders wanted testing, why does so little get tested?

Because testing is uncomfortable and its results are unshareable. A working that produces nothing is not a story, and the social machinery that circulates occult material has no use for it. The founding texts are genuinely clear about testing, which makes the gap a fact about human behaviour rather than about the doctrine.

Does the failure rate tell us the method does nothing?

No, and it does not tell us it does something either. The honest position is that the claim has not been tested at the scale that would settle it, and the cost of testing it properly is a lot of private, unglamorous record-keeping over months. Most practitioners are not doing that, so the evidence base is thin in both directions.

Is testing it compatible with taking it seriously?

Yes, and it is more compatible than the alternative. A sincere commitment does not require suspending the ordinary tools of evaluation, and a practice that punishes you for checking it is asking for a kind of faith that is difficult to justify on any grounds the tradition itself would accept.

What should I do if my practice has started costing me sleep?

Reduce it, and reduce it immediately rather than gradually. The tradition's own advice here is a short daily routine rather than one that escalates, and that advice is good regardless of what anyone believes about the rest. Sleep loss changes perception, and a log written on broken sleep is not data.

Is it worth buying anything to support a testing-based practice?

The testing is free and stays free. A purchase buys the record-keeping and the arithmetic, which is the unglamorous half, and if you already keep a disciplined log on paper then nothing on sale will improve it. Save the money unless the bookkeeping is the part you have been failing at.
