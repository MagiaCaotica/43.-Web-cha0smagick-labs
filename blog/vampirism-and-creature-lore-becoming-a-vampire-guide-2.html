Vampirism and Creature Lore

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Vampiric practice is more ordinary than its imagery suggests, and that is worth saying first because the aesthetic does a lot of work in recruiting people to a bad version of it. What a modern occultist means by vampirism is an energy-work framework with a particular vocabulary, borrowed from a literary creature that itself was assembled from several folk traditions in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries rather than from any single ancient source. The practice has real components, real boundaries, and real failure modes. This article sets out the whole sequence as a procedure, with the parts that people skip and the part where genuine harm is possible both left in. Nothing here replaces diagnosis or treatment, and if you have a health concern, a clinician comes first.

What the practice is, stated as a procedure

Vampiric practice as a modern occult technique draws on two sources that are usually merged. The first is the vampire's life-energy motif, the fiction of taking vitality from another source. The second is a much broader tradition of energy-work framing in which a practitioner manages, absorbs, or redirects their own vitality through contact with a scene, a place, or a person. The second is the operative part. The first is the branding.

Stated as a procedure, the work has roughly seven stages: choosing and taking on a name, establishing a boundary, selecting the material you will work with, performing the working in parts, a metaphorical feeding, a withdrawal period, and an ongoing obligation that keeps the practice from being a costume. The stages are not ancient. They are a modern reconstruction, and treating them as a recovered tradition rather than a design is the first error to avoid.

Step one, the name you are taking on

A name is not decoration here. It does the work of a container. Naming the practice gives you a boundary condition and a shorthand, and it lets you refer to a mode of being rather than a mood you might be in on a given afternoon. A practitioner who has not named what they are doing tends to drift, and drift is the main risk in this material.

Choose something you will still recognise in two years, write it down with the date, and do not change it because a better one occurs to you. A name that keeps getting replaced is a symptom of the same instability that identity-absorbing practice carries as a documented psychological risk. If your practice is requiring a costume upgrade every few months, that is information about the practice rather than about your taste.

Step two, the boundary that makes it safe

The boundary is the part that is most often skipped and the part that makes the rest defensible. It is a stated limit on what the practice touches, and it must exclude other people's autonomy entirely. Work on your own availability, your own boundaries, your own behaviour. Do not work on overriding somebody's stated preferences, and do not construct a frame in which their wanting becomes a thing you can influence.

This is not an ethical ornament. It separates a practice from a compulsion, and identity-absorbing work carries documented risk particularly in people with dissociation or psychotic vulnerability. If your practice starts involving the inner life of people who are not in the room, stop. That is a hard boundary, not a guideline, and it is the one that distinguishes this from a good deal of what circulates under similar names.

Step three, choosing the material you work with

By material I mean the substance of the practice, and choosing it deliberately is a meaningful step rather than a formality. Some practitioners work with blood, some with sexual energy, some with attention and image alone, and some with a landscape or a place. The modern energy-work framing does not require the most dramatic option, and choosing the most dramatic one is a reliable way to build a practice you cannot sustain.

The stronger argument against blood work is not moral and not supernatural. It is that it concentrates a symbolic load on a real, irreversible, biological act, and it puts you in proximity to other people in a way that makes the boundary harder to hold. Attention and image are available to anyone, cost nothing, harm nobody, and can be refined indefinitely. If you want the practice, the expensive version is not the demanding one.

  • Attention and image, which cost nothing and can be refined indefinitely.
  • A place or a landscape, which locates the practice outside your body.
  • Sexual energy, which is real work and needs the boundary more than any other option.
  • Blood, which concentrates an irreversible act into a symbolic role. Avoid.

Step four, the working, in parts

The working itself is short and the shortness is the design. A reduction, a construction, a brief period of sustained attention, and a dismissal. Practitioners who extend this into an hour are usually extending it because the attention has not landed, not because the working needs more time. The energy-work framing says the effect is in the state, and a state that has to be maintained for an hour is not a state you have reached.

Keep the parts separate and keep them brief. Reduction, then construction, then attention, then dismissal, each as its own act, none of them bleeding into the next. The separation is what makes the practice reproducible, because a reproducible practice is one you can test and abandon. An hour-long improvised working cannot be evaluated, which is how people end up with years of practice and no idea whether any of it did anything.

Step five, the feeding, and why it is metaphorical

A feeding is a short, deliberate withdrawal of attention from the ordinary noise of the day in order to take in more. It is metaphorical in the sense that nothing is drained from anyone. What is withdrawn is your own engagement with the ambient demand of a world that is mostly asking things of you, and what is taken in is whatever you actually want in that moment.

The metaphor is not a dodge. The part of this that has any evidence behind it is the attentional one. Practising a selective narrowing of attention, away from distraction and toward a chosen content, is a trainable skill with measurable effects on mood and impulse control. Everything built on top of that is a vocabulary. The vocabulary is useful, and it is also where most of the nonsense accumulates.

Step six, the withdrawal period

After a working you reduce input. This is the least glamorous stage and the one with the clearest payoff, because it is where the state you reached gets consolidated. Practitioner accounts describe it as returning slowly, keeping the world at arm's length for a day or two, and doing nothing demanding.

Skipping it is the most common procedural error in this material, and the reason is that the withdrawal is defined by what it lacks. There is no artifact to produce, no record to write, nothing to show anyone. It survives or fails entirely on whether you treat it as a stage. If you remove it, you have removed the part that would have told you whether the working had any effect.

Step seven, the ongoing obligation

A vampiric practice in the energy-work sense implies a standing arrangement, and this is the part that distinguishes the practice from a technique you ran once. The obligation is not sacrifice. It is a commitment to a way of being available, usually to a scene, a place, or a community, that continues whether or not anything supernatural is ever demonstrated.

This is where a lot of people discover they never wanted a standing arrangement. That is a useful discovery, arrived at cheaply, and it is much cheaper than discovering it after two years of escalating intensity. The practice is not the creature. The creature is the imagination that lets you avoid noticing you have committed to something.

What the sources support and what they do not

The European literary vampire is a comparatively modern construction, assembled in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries out of several separate folk traditions, some eastern European, some from folklore about disease and outsiders, some from accounts of burial practices that read alarmingly in a later century. There is no single ancient source and no continuous tradition of practice underneath the fiction.

What that means practically is that you cannot appeal to antiquity. There is no unbroken lineage to inherit, no lost practice to recover, and no historical warrant for any specific technique. The vampiric energy-work framework is a modern construction with useful psychology inside it. Treating it as a rediscovery is a rhetorical choice that makes it feel more authoritative than it can support, and it is the main reason this material attracts people who are looking for permission.

The identity question, asked carefully

As a practice and an identity, yes. As a physical transformation, no. There is no mechanism, and the historical sources do not support it, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something. That question comes up in almost every conversation about this material and it is worth answering in a single sentence so it stops consuming attention.

The identity half deserves more care than the metaphysical half. Taking on a sustained vocabulary for how you relate to your own vitality is a real commitment, and commitments that reorganise self-concept carry documented psychological weight. For most people the practice is stable. For some people, particularly those with a history of dissociation or psychotic vulnerability, identity work of any intensity can destabilise, and the responsible version of this advice is that intensity is the variable to control rather than the imagery to worry about.

What failure here actually looks like

Failure in this material does not look like a spiritual crisis. It looks like a practitioner who cannot stop narrating their life as a vampire, who experiences ordinary social contact as a kind of feeding, who finds the boundary harder to hold each month than the last, and who describes the practice using the vocabulary of compulsion while insisting it is chosen.

The diagnostic question is simple: is the practice making you more available to the world, or less. The energy-work framing, done properly, produces someone with more capacity and more discernment. If it is producing someone with a narrower life and an expanding sense of special obligation, that is not intensification, and it is not what the sources describe either.

  1. The practice has become a frame you cannot stop narrating with.
  2. Ordinary contact has been recoded as a form of taking.
  3. The boundary has loosened each month rather than holding steady.
  4. You describe compulsion in the vocabulary of choice.

Why the Liber Lvpinux is not the text people think

The Liber Lvpinux circulates in online occult content as though it were a manual for becoming a werewolf. It is not. There is no werewolf transformation ritual in it, and the claim recurs often enough that a source asserting it should be read with suspicion. The text is a fifteenth-century manuscript, and what it contains is a set of transformations, recipes, and protections that people have mapped onto the werewolf legend rather more aggressively than the evidence supports.

This matters beyond the narrow point, because the same pattern runs through most of the online material in this field. A genuine historical document gets recruited to a modern claim it does not make, usually because the claim is more interesting than the document. If you are reading this field at all, the first useful skill is checking whether the text says the thing the post says it says.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you actually become a vampire?

As a practice and an identity, yes, and plenty of people sustain one for years. As a physical transformation, no. There is no mechanism, the historical sources do not describe one, and the literary creature is a modern construction rather than a real species. Answering this once, plainly, frees up a surprising amount of attention for the parts of the practice that work.

Is vampirism harmful in practice?

For most people it is stable and low risk, provided the boundary holds and the practice stays on your own behaviour and availability rather than anyone else's. The documented risks cluster around intensity, identity reorganization, and losing the ability to treat the practice as a choice. Keep it short, keep the withdrawal period, and stop if ordinary contact starts feeling like taking.

Does the Liber Lvpinux cover werewolves?

No. It contains no werewolf transformation ritual, and the claim that it does is a recurring error in online occult content. The text is a fifteenth-century manuscript of transformations, recipes, and protections that later writers mapped onto the werewolf legend. Any source telling you otherwise is telling you something, and it is not about the manuscript.

What does the paid upgrade actually change about the method?

Nothing about the method, which is attention, a boundary, a short working and a withdrawal period. What a purchase can buy is a printed reference, a proper journal, or a one-time generator for the reduction. A recurring subscription is a poor fit for a practice whose value lies in a record you own and a boundary you set yourself, because both of those are worse when they live somewhere you could lose access to them.

Is there a paid tool that does this better than a notebook?

No, and the honest answer is that this material needs almost no equipment. A notebook is where the practice lives, because the record of what you did, when, and what followed is the only part of it that can be examined later. Software can hold that record too, but it cannot hold the boundary, and the boundary is the part that keeps the practice safe.
