Horror Fiction and Weird Cosmology

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 16 min read

H. P. Lovecraft did not invent the idea that something older than humanity is paying attention, but he built the most internally consistent version of it in English, and then a century of other writers moved into the space he left. What exists now is not a religion and not a system. It is a shared fictional cosmology: a set of premises about the world that a large number of writers, readers and illustrators hold in common, and that can be cited, extended and deliberately broken. The interesting part for a practitioner is not that any of it is true. It is what a shared fiction does to a vocabulary. Lovecraft and the writers who followed him gave the English language a stock of images for scale, indifference and illegibility that the tradition of magic did not previously have, and that stock has been borrowed so thoroughly by occult writing that the borrowing is now invisible. This is a reading sequence rather than a defence of anything. It takes the material at its strongest, sets out the condition under which it can be read honestly, and asks a narrow question at each step: what is this actually supplying me, and am I able to notice when the supply runs out?

A shared fiction, and what that makes it

The clearest way to hold this material is as a shared fictional cosmology. That phrase is doing more work than it looks. A fictional cosmology is not a claim about the world. It is a set of premises that a body of work holds consistently enough that readers can predict what a new instalment will do, and can be irritated when it does not. The premises here are few and unusually stable. Something predates humanity. It is not hostile in the way a human enemy is hostile, because hostility requires a comparable scale of concern. It is indifferent, or closer to it. Human institutions and individuals are, at best, marginally interesting to it. Knowledge itself is dangerous, not because it is forbidden but because a mind that understands too much becomes visible to the things that notice minds. The novels, the stories, the illustrations and the games that have grown out of this are all working inside the same premises, which is what makes them legible to each other across eighty years and across dozens of authors.

What this gives you as a working material is a vocabulary of scale. When a practitioner wants to talk about something vast, indifferent and outside human concerns, the shared fiction already contains an established set of figures for it: the geometry that is not architecture, the light that is not illumination, the archives that are not libraries, the mathematics that is not mathematics. You can borrow those figures because a reader in any English-speaking culture will already have the context to hold them. That is a genuine practical asset and it is worth being honest that it is the whole of the asset. Nothing in the corpus reports an event. Nothing in it constrains anything. What it does is supply images that compress a large and difficult thought into something a working ritual can hold in a glyph or a paragraph.

The 1928 essay, and why it comes first

Before any of the stories, read the essay. Supernatural Horror in Literature, published in 1928, is Lovecraft's own statement of what the weird tale is and what it is not, and it runs to well under a hundred pages. It is the most efficient introduction to the material available, and it is efficient for a specific reason: Lovecraft wrote it as an advocate rather than a commentator, and it therefore has to argue rather than describe. His case is that the weird tale is a distinct literary mode with its own technique, distinct from horror, from fantasy and from science fiction, and that the technique consists largely of describing something through its effects rather than through its appearance. He is making a formal argument about atmosphere, and the argument generalises well outside his own subject.

Reading it first also shortens the rest of the corpus considerably. Once you have his account of what the mode requires, you can read the stories as instances of a technique instead of as a series of revelations about how strange the world is. That reframing matters for a practitioner, because the stories stop being warnings and start being demonstrations. A demonstration of how to write a sense of scale into four paragraphs is a reusable skill. A warning about what lies beyond the walls is not, unless you intend to be frightened, and being frightened is a poor basis for making decisions. There is also a practical detail: the essay tells you which later writers he considered the mode's real practitioners, which saves you from treating the canon as a closed list.

The intelligences that predate humanity

Every version of this cosmology puts something old at the centre, and the versions differ less in what the thing is than in its temperament. Some of it is indifferent, which is the most common. Some of it is actively malevolent, though malevolence is usually a secondary reading imposed on a being whose actual stance is closer to astronomical. Some of it is a question rather than an answer, which is the more disturbing version and the one that does more interesting work. The category is stable across the corpus: these are not demons with a moral character, not gods with a personality, and not angels with a rank. They are structures. The most useful single premise is the one about scale, because scale explains their behaviour without requiring any psychology from them at all. If you are not on the scale of their concern, then your suffering is not cruelty, it is incidental, the way a storm does not hate the fence it takes down.

The second stable premise is the relationship between knowledge and visibility. In this cosmology, understanding something is what makes you perceptible to it, and being perceptible is the beginning of being acted upon. That premise is doing a lot of work in the fiction, because it produces the specific dread of the competent researcher who realises the research is not private. It is worth noticing that this is not a claim about the world. It is a narrative device that converts a very human experience, the feeling of having been noticed by something with more power, into a cosmological frame. A reader who enjoys the frame can still check the experience against ordinary causes, and a reader who cannot do that has been handed a story about themselves and may mistake it for information.

What the mythos actually gives you

Stripped of the atmosphere, the corpus supplies four things. The first is a stock of images for the numinous, defined negatively rather than positively, which is a more workable form for a ritual than any positive definition. The second is a set of narrative structures for the specific experience of being a small thing in a large arrangement: the scholar, the inheritor, the ship that went somewhere, the signal that came from the wrong direction. The third is a vocabulary of artefacts, documents and entities that are described with enough specificity to be referenced and too little to be tested. The fourth, and this is the one most people miss, is a technique for writing scale, which is a craft skill and transfers to any context where you need to make a reader feel something large.

None of these is a ritual, and this is where most occult borrowings from the corpus go wrong. A fictional entity described across ninety stories is not a being you can address. It has no office, no jurisdiction and no response that is not composed by a human author. When a working ritual borrows a name from the fiction and then claims an encounter, the claim is not being supported by the corpus, because the corpus contains no encounters. It contains descriptions of encounters, written by people who were in no danger. The honest position is that the corpus is a supply of figures and a training ground for a particular kind of writing, and that anything built out of it should be described in those terms rather than in terms of contact.

Imagery against structure, and the difference you will feel

The distinction that does the most practical work is between a body of imagery and a body of structure. Imagery gives you a felt sense of a situation. Structure gives you an ordered procedure with a defined start, a defined end and a point at which you can check whether you followed it. A tradition like ceremonial magic has plenty of the second and comparatively little of the first, and practitioners often notice the absence as a lack of meaning. The fictional corpus is the reverse: an enormous supply of the first and essentially nothing of the second. That is why the borrowings tend to produce work that is atmospheric and vague. Nothing in the fiction tells you what to do on Thursday.

You can feel the difference directly if you try the exercise of turning a piece of the imagery into an operation. Take a described artefact, a fictional text with a title and a summary of contents, and ask what the procedure is that would engage with it. The answer will be thin, because the fiction never needed one. Now take an actual procedure from a working tradition and ask what image would represent it. The answer comes quickly, because procedures are concrete and images are cheap. This asymmetry is the reason the direction of borrowing in occult practice has almost always been from the fictional corpus outward into ritual description, and the reason the resulting rituals feel atmospheric and unfalsifiable. They have inherited the qualities of the source they came from.

The reading condition you cannot skip

Lovecraft's racial views are explicit in his private correspondence, and they are not incidental to his public work. This is a condition of reading, not a footnote. The premise of the shared cosmology is that some intelligences are less real than human beings, that certain groups of human beings are degenerate rather than merely different, and that this hierarchy of substance is natural. That premise is load-bearing across the fiction and it is also the reason the material became so useful to writers who wanted images of hierarchy without having to argue for it. Any reader who takes the imagery and leaves the hierarchy has taken the most portable part and discarded the part that would have made the whole thing cost something.

This has a practical consequence rather than only an ethical one. The corpus is unusually good at producing a feeling of having glimpsed something forbidden, and that feeling is exactly what a working ritual is often built to produce. If the forbidden feeling is built on a hierarchy of worth, then a working that borrows the imagery is quietly borrowing the hierarchy, whether or not the practitioner endorses it. The way to avoid this is not to avoid the material but to read it in full, including the parts that will cost you the ability to use it casually. If after that reading the material is unusable for your purposes, that is a legitimate result and it is better than a working that was never examined.

A sequence for the first month

Week one is the essay, read straight through, with no stories alongside it. The purpose is to get his formal argument in your head before the atmosphere starts doing the work of convincing you. Week two is three stories of your own choosing from the period he considered central, with the essay still nearby for reference. Week three is a single long work from the same period, read in one sitting if you can manage it, because the sustained version is what teaches you the pacing and the long-dread effect. Week four is one modern work that uses the same premises and a completely different technique, which is the step that converts the material from a set of borrowed figures into something you have opinions about.

The sequence has one rule that matters more than the order: write while you read. A short entry after each item, three lines, is enough. First line, what image stuck. Second line, what the image is for. Third line, whether you could use it. The third line is the one that matters, and the one people skip, because it feels dismissive. It is not dismissive. It is the difference between collecting and working, and a month of this is more likely to produce something you can use than a year of reading with no questions asked.

The vocabulary ledger, and what it is for

The ledger is a list of the figures you have actually taken, one per line, with a note on what each one is for. This is a more useful object than a reading list because it is short. A reading list accumulates; a ledger commits. Most people who read this material end up with a reading list of forty items and no working practice, and the reason is structural: the material is more rewarding to read than to use, because reading it produces an experience and using it produces an obligation. The ledger interrupts that by requiring you to state, in advance, what a figure is for. If you cannot state it, the figure does not go on the list.

Keep it in one place and keep it short, under forty lines is plenty. When the list gets past that, you are no longer using the material and you have started collecting it again, which is a hobby with a much lower rate of return. The second function of the ledger is diagnostic. After a few months you can read down it and see a pattern: if every entry is about vastness, you have a figure problem and no technique. If every entry is about documents and archives, you have something you might genuinely build a working out of, because procedure is what you are reaching for and procedure is what the corpus does not supply. That pattern is worth more than any individual entry.

Where the imagery starts doing work

There is one place where this material earns its keep in a working context, and it is not where most people expect. Once you have read enough of it, you develop an unusually quick sense for the difference between a symbol that means something and a symbol that is merely impressive. That discrimination transfers. A practitioner who has spent a month with a corpus built entirely on the principle that size defeats comprehension arrives at their own sigils with a much better instinct for which marks are carrying weight and which are decoration. The same is true of written material. A month of reading prose that is trying to make a reader feel small will make you much harder to impress with a page of formal invocations.

The second place is in the description of states rather than the achievement of outcomes. A working that says what a practice is like to be inside, in language borrowed from this corpus, will be more useful as a description than a working that promises a result. The tradition this sits inside has no good technical vocabulary for subjective states, which is a real gap, and the corpus fills part of it well. If your aim is to write a practice that someone else can recognise from the inside, this is where the material earns its place. If your aim is to obtain a result, you want a document with instructions and this is the wrong source.

The one thing the corpus will never give you

It will never give you a sequence. This is worth stating plainly because the temptation to fill the gap is the source of almost all the bad borrowings. A fictional corpus contains no steps, no order of operations, no account of what to do if a step goes wrong, and no account of what the practitioner should expect. When an occult text supplies a numbered ritual set in a voice borrowed from this material, none of the numbers come from the fiction. They have been invented to fill a structural gap, and they are therefore someone's untested invention wearing the clothes of a canon. A reader who cannot tell the difference will believe the sequence is as old as the imagery, and will therefore attribute to it a weight it does not have.

The second thing it will never give you is a reason to continue. Fiction ends. A working tradition exists precisely because the practice has to be repeated to be useful, and repetition requires something to come back to, which is a document with instructions rather than a mood with images. The people who last a long time in this field are the ones who have a boring source they keep going back to and an interesting one they read for pleasure. People who have only the interesting one tend to last about a year, and they tend to describe the loss as a failure of belief rather than as a missing file.

What the influence on practice really consists of

The influence is real and it is almost entirely at the level of figure and register. If you look at modern occult writing you will find, everywhere, a vocabulary of vast indifferent intelligences, forbidden archives, geometries that imply mathematics, and signals arriving from an unacceptable direction. None of this is inherited ritual. It is a shared cultural inheritance, arriving through fiction, illustration, film and games, and being mistaken by some writers for a lineage. Where a modern working ritual uses one of these figures as the thing it is addressing, the address is to a literary construct, and the result is atmospheric rather than effective.

There is a version of this influence that is genuinely worth having. Writers working in this field have spent a century developing ways of writing that are legible to each other across enormous differences in belief. That shared register is a genuine literary achievement and it is available to anyone. A practitioner who can write a working that another practitioner takes seriously, using figures both of them understand, has learned something transferable, and that owes nothing to whether the figures are true. The tradition this sits inside has always been better at this than most of its critics give it credit for, and it is the part that does not require you to believe a single sentence of the fiction.

The entry point worth using

If you want one recommendation: read the 1928 essay, then read one long work from the same period, then stop and go and find an actual procedural text from a real working tradition, and let the two sit side by side. The comparison is instructive in a way that neither can manage alone. The fiction will show you what a serious treatment of scale looks like when it is not trying to instruct anyone. The procedural text will show you what instruction looks like when it is not trying to impress anyone. Most of the confusion in this field comes from having read one of these and quietly assumed the other one was supposed to be like it.

If you want two, add the modern novel that uses the same premises and a different technique, because it is the only item in this material that will tell you what a new generation thinks the premises are for. And if you want to know whether any of it survives contact with a working practice, the test is simple, and it requires no belief: write down three figures you took, and try to build a working out of one. If you can, and the working is legible to someone who has never read the fiction, you have extracted something. If you cannot get past the point of naming the figure, you have read some good writing and that is a fine outcome as long as you know which one it is.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Lovecraft mythos a religion?

No, and the difference is not a technicality. A religion has a body of practice that is answerable to a community, a set of claims about what is the case, and a requirement that the practitioner conform to something outside their own preferences. The shared fiction has a body of images and a body of narrative, and it has no practice that anyone is obliged to perform. Calling it a religion in conversation tends to be a way of making other people uncomfortable rather than a way of describing the material accurately.

Should I use figures from the fiction in my workings?

You can, and the honest way to do it is to know what you are using. Figures from the fiction are compressed images, and they work well for describing states and establishing register. They do not work as addresses, because there is nothing at the other end of the address. If you use one as the thing you are addressing, say so in your own notes, because the distinction matters when you later read your own record and try to work out what happened.

What is Supernatural Horror in Literature and should I read it?

It is a 1928 essay by Lovecraft arguing that the weird tale is a literary mode with its own technique, distinct from horror, fantasy and science fiction. It is short, under a hundred pages, and it is the most efficient introduction to the material because it argues a case rather than describing a canon. Reading it before the stories also reframes them as demonstrations of technique rather than as revelations about the world.

Why is his racial thinking part of the reading rather than a footnote?

Because the hierarchy it asserts is load-bearing in the fiction, and because the corpus has been used to supply images of hierarchy to writers who did not want to make the argument themselves. A reader who takes the imagery and leaves the hierarchy has taken the portable part. The practical cost is that a working built on those images is quietly borrowing the premise, which is a reason to read it carefully rather than a reason to avoid it silently.

Is any of this evidence for anything?

No. The corpus reports no event and constrains nothing. It is a shared set of premises about a fictional arrangement, which makes it a good source of figures and a good training ground for writing at scale, and nothing more. A working that claims a result on the authority of a story is making a claim the story cannot support, and you will find that out the first time you try to repeat it.
