Rune Casting and the Futhark: Reading the Marks Properly

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

Runes get taught badly, and the usual reason is that people are taught to read them before they learn what they are. The Elder Futhark is a writing system. Twenty-four characters, arranged in three groups of eight, developed in northern Europe over centuries for naming, accounting and inscription. That it was also used for questions is not a separate magical fact. It is a separate historical fact, from the same source material. Getting the order right removes most of the fog. Once you know the aettir, you know where each rune sits relative to its family, and family relationships carry real interpretive weight that a flat keyword list throws away. What follows covers the alphabet honestly, compares the common casting methods by what they actually bias, and gives a reading practice you can start tonight without buying anything.

The alphabet came before the oracle

Runes are letters. Fehu looks like an F because it stands for the sound that begins the word feather, and it is a writing system before it is anything else. The 24-character Elder Futhark predates both the Anglo-Saxon futhorc and the 16-character Younger Futhark, and it was used across northern Europe for ordinary inscription long before the surviving divinatory texts were written.

The word rune appears in Old Norse in both a writing context and an explicitly mystical one. That matters, because it tells you the mystical reading was attached to a real and ordinary object rather than invented by modern practitioners. What the historical sources do not support is the far larger system of correspondences that later traditions built on top: planets, zodiac signs, tree names, phonetic values, astrological timing and so on.

None of that added material is worthless. It is a symbolic vocabulary assembled by practitioners, and it works the way any symbolic vocabulary works, by making certain questions easier to formulate. The honest position is that the alphabet is historical, the divinatory use is historical, and the layered correspondence system is a later and explicitly constructed addition.

Aettir: why the runes come in threes

The Elder Futhark divides into three aettir of eight: Fehu through Hagalaz, Naudiz through Sowilo, and Tiwaz through Eihwaz. The word futhark is a mnemonic built from the first letter of each of the first six runes, which tells you the sequence is meant to be memorised, not looked up.

Family membership is genuinely useful at the table. When three runes land, an interpretation that treats all three as unrelated keyword bundles is leaving the structure unused. An aettir that appears three times suggests the same domain has to be addressed from several angles. A single rune from a family with two others present is usually the specific instance of a larger pattern.

Some lineages assign each aettir a theme, and the assignments vary. Fehu through Hagalaz is frequently read as resources, movement and disruption. Treat any single assignment as a starting hypothesis for your own system rather than a fixed fact, and keep notes, because your own consistent associations will teach you more in a month than a borrowed table will.

Casting methods compared by bias

A rune staff gives you a roughly uniform distribution across the runic characters, and it is a nice object to handle. Throwing a set onto a surface produces a distribution that depends on how the runes are shaped and how they land, so short-stroke runes appear more often than complex ones. Neither is wrong, but the bias is real and worth knowing.

A bag with mixed tokens and a handful drawn blind gives good uniformity and is the easiest method to reproduce. Drawing from a fixed spread without casting gives you a position-by-position read, which is really a different operation: the layout carries the structure and the runes supply vocabulary.

A staff is an aesthetic and a handling aid, not a requirement. People who cast from a bag get results indistinguishable from people who cast from a staff, because the interpretive work happens in the reading and not in the throw. Pick the method you will actually use every week, since consistency of method is worth more than elegance of method.

Reading by position instead of by keyword

Most beginner reading errors come from treating a rune as a fixed answer. Fehu does not mean wealth. It means a specific thing with a specific origin, and how much of that meaning is present depends on where it landed and what it landed next to.

Position-based reading gives you a scaffold. With three runes you can assign them yourself: situation, obstacle, direction. Or past, present, future when the question is genuinely forward-looking. Or the first as what you are asking about, the second as what you are not seeing, the third as the material you have not considered. Any consistent assignment will outperform a keyword list, because consistency is what makes the next reading interpretable against this one.

The aettir layer sits on top of position. First read positions, then notice the family pattern, then read the individual runes. Doing those three passes in that order is the whole method, and it is more than most people ever bother to learn.

Younger Futhark and the futhorc, briefly

The Younger Futhark reduced the character count to sixteen by using a shared character for a group of related sounds. The Anglo-Saxon futhorc took a different route, adding characters to cover sounds the original system did not have. Both are legitimate alphabets; neither is a prerequisite.

Learn the Elder Futhark first, in aettir order. The reason is not that the others are wrong but that the divination literature overwhelmingly uses the 24-rune set, so the material you will read, compare and interpret with assumes it. Anglo-Saxon runes are an addition you can pick up later if a specific practice calls for them.

If a deck you have bought uses a different system, check which. The Norse Rune Oracle uses a modern rune set with a different ordering and a designed interpretive scheme. It is a well-made deck, and it is a reading system rather than a historical reconstruction, so its readings are meant to be learned rather than derived.

Bindrunes are the ancestor of sigilisation

A bindrune combines two or more characters into one compound glyph. This is attested in the historical record on inscriptions and in later magical material, and it is the direct ancestor of the practice we now call sigilisation.

The mechanism is identical in both cases. A statement of intent is reduced to a compact glyph that carries the statement without spelling it out. The bindrune compresses a name or a word; the sigil compresses an imperative phrase. Both work on the reader, who is usually the author, and both work by removing the friction of having to articulate the full text every time.

This is a useful historical anchor for anyone sceptical about sigils. The practice is not a modern invention with no lineage. It is a documented letter-reduction technique that predates the entire chaos magic movement by centuries, which is worth knowing when someone tells you sigilisation is a recent trend.

Building a working vocabulary

Read each rune's name, its sound value, and the modern English words that contain it. Fehu gives you feather, felon, flood, frame. Sowilo gives sunlight, sword, sister. This etymological work takes about an hour for the full set and it changes how the readings land, because you are no longer reciting a definition you did not choose.

Then write your own gloss for each rune in your own words and keep it in one page. That page is more valuable to you than any published table, because it will reflect how you actually think and how you actually use language. Reread it monthly and revise it as your usage shifts.

The layer to add last is the correspondence system: planets, elements, trees, days. Add it deliberately, a few runes at a time, and test each addition against a real reading before you keep it. A correspondence you have never tested is a decoration, and a decoration is worse than nothing because it looks like content.

A daily practice that builds skill

Fifteen minutes a day with a single question, cast and read in the same position scheme, produces more competence in two months than an hour a week of casting whatever comes to mind. The gain is not mystical. It is that you accumulate a comparable series, and a comparable series is the only thing that lets you notice anything.

Keep the question in present tense and specific. What is the actual decision here. What is the actual obstacle. Vague questions produce vague readings, and vague readings feel meaningful for a day and then evaporate, which teaches you nothing about the system.

The digital option is worth considering for the casting and journalling, not for the learning. An app can deal the runes, record the position scheme, tag the aettir and keep your history searchable in a way paper cannot. It cannot teach you the etymologies or check whether you are reading the same rune the same way every week, and that is the part that does the work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which runes should I learn first?

The 24-rune Elder Futhark, in aettir order. That covers the set used by most divination literature and gives you the family structure that makes positional reading work. Anglo-Saxon runes are an addition you can pick up once the base set is fluent, not a requirement for starting.

Do I need a rune staff?

No. Casting from a bag or drawing from a spread produces the same interpretive material, and a staff is a handling aid and an aesthetic rather than a functional component. Choose whatever you will actually use several times a week, because a method you enjoy is worth more than a method that is theoretically more uniform.

Are runes divination or an alphabet?

Historically both. The same characters served writing, naming and divinatory use, and the surviving evidence does not cleanly separate them. Modern rune divination is a revival that borrows from both, which is why the honest description is that the alphabet is historical and the interpretive system is a mix of recovered material and deliberate construction.

How do I keep my rune meanings from going stale?

Keep one page of your own glosses and revise it monthly against your journal. Your personal vocabulary drifts as you use the system, and the drift is where most of the learning actually happens. A published table you never amend will keep telling you things you stopped meaning months ago.

Is a paid rune app worth more than a free online caster?

Rune software is the clearest case for a purchase you make once. The deck is a fixed artefact, the upkeep is nil, and there is nothing that renews, so a flat price is the honest shape of the transaction. A free caster is fine for learning, and a one-off app earns its place when you want history you can search and a consistent position scheme applied automatically.
