History of the Occult

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

The instruction usually given for this subject is to study three threads and see where they meet. I set out to trace three of them properly, one at a time, and write down what I could actually source. The three were necromancy, robotics, and chaos magic, which is a strange company and the one that produced the most useful result. Chaos magic is the only thread of the three that documents its own recent history in detail, because its practitioners wrote things down and argued about them in public. Necromancy is largely undocumented at the level of practice, and robotics turned out to belong to a different century than the one I had in mind. What follows is what survived that attempt, what did not, and the three places where the connective tissue everyone draws turns out to be twentieth-century invention. If you are looking for a lineage, you will find one. It is just not the one the framing implies.

What this report set out to trace

Three threads, joined at a single claim: that modern practitioners are working with continuities older than the twentieth century. I wanted to know, for each thread, what the earliest located source was, what it actually said, and where the practice survived in between. I kept a source column in a single document and refused to add a claim to the prose until the column had something in it.

The method matters because the field has a strong pull toward synthesis. Every one of the three threads has a genuine scholarly literature and a much larger literature of amateur synthesis, and the two are hard to tell apart in aggregate. I lost roughly a week to claims I could not source, which is a cheaper outcome than the alternative and considerably more useful to write down.

Necromancy, and where the trail goes cold

The scholarly position is comfortable and the practitioner position is not. There is good work on how the word shifted, on what late antique and medieval sources actually describe, and on why the modern image of a raised corpse with a familiar face is a nineteenth-century literary construction rather than a documented practice. That much I could source without difficulty.

What I could not source is any continuous practical technique. The trail goes cold between the medieval and the modern period, and the honest description of that gap is that practitioners were transmitting something the record does not capture, or transmitting a repertoire that has since been reassembled from fragments. Both claims are unevidenced. I would rather write that than assert continuity I cannot document.

Robotics, and the wrong century

This thread produced the clearest correction in the report. The interest in automata as an occult subject is genuinely nineteenth century, running alongside the mechanical figures in the period's technical literature. Attempts to push automata into older magical corpora produce dates that do not hold up, and the plausible-looking parallels turn out to share only a preoccupation with motion without a mechanism.

That is not a debunking. It relocates the thread, and the relocated version is more interesting because it sits next to real material: the Theosophical and ceremonial interest in mechanical symbolism, the early twentieth-century fascination with automata as an occult object, and the later convergence with computing. A genuine lineage, just not the one the popular version claims.

Chaos magic, and the thread that documents itself

The reason this thread is tractable is a decision the movement made in the early 1970s. The foundational texts insisted on experiment over doctrine, which had the useful side effect of generating an enormous published record about its own development, including the arguments and the splits. Most occult movements leave a thin paper trail because they hide it. This one wrote it down.

The cost is that the record is unusually self-conscious, and reading it requires accounting for a movement that was partly constituted by the pose of testing things. The claims about method, the founding date and the relationship to the two foundational books are all well documented. The metaphysical claims are also all well documented, which in this case means they are well documented as claims.

Where the three threads genuinely connect

In one place only, and it is a modest one. Each thread involves a practitioner claiming access to something the surrounding culture does not treat as accessible, and each involves a documented method for making the claim testable or at least repeatable. The occult method and the engineering method turn out to share a shape rather than a substance, and that shared shape is the whole of the connection I can defend.

I would not build a practice on that. I would build a practice on one thread, taken seriously, with the others left as reading. The version of this material that treats three separate fields as one continuous discipline is doing something rhetorically, and the rhetoric is what people find compelling rather than what the sources support.

  • One thread taken seriously beats three threads gestured at.
  • A method that can be repeated is worth more than a lineage that cannot be dated.
  • A movement that publishes its own history is easier to check than one that does not.
  • Similarity of method is not evidence of shared origin.

Where they were stitched together, and by whom

Almost entirely in the last forty years, and largely through a handful of people who were writing for a general rather than a specialist audience. The stitching takes a real fact from each field, drops the date, and presents the join as though it had always been there. This is not fraud. It is the ordinary way that popular history is produced, and the individual claims in it are frequently accurate.

The failure is cumulative. Three accurate claims joined by two undocumented joins produce a lineage that no source supports, and the join is usually the interesting part, which is why the lineage gets repeated. If you want to spot this in material you like, ask what the source is for the join rather than for the pieces.

The three claims I could not source

First, that any modern practice descends from a documented pre-modern technique of the same name. Second, that a specific mechanism was understood in antiquity and lost. Third, that the popular image of the occult magician is derived from a historical figure rather than assembled from literary and theatrical sources. Each is plausible. None of them is evidenced, and the third is the one most often presented as settled.

I record them because unsourceable claims are useful. A claim you know you cannot support is a boundary you can work inside, and a claim you have quietly assumed is a boundary you will cross without noticing. Having three named ones makes it much easier to notice the fourth when it arrives.

What a modern practitioner can honestly use

The documented parts. The 1970s emergence of the experimental current, the two foundational books, the deliberate refusal of doctrine as a requirement, the difference between a claim about mechanism and a claim about method. None of that is glamorous and all of it is usable, because it tells you what the practice was for. It was for testing things, and a testing practice that stops recording results has stopped being the thing it was.

The older material is usable as reference rather than as precedent. Nineteenth century ceremonial manuals are clear about their own assumptions, written by people who knew what they were drawing on, and reading them is genuinely informative about how a formal system is built. Reading them as instructions for a modern life is where the mischief starts.

A working built from the traceable parts

Take the method claim, drop the lineage claim, and run something you can record. Choose a single question, write it as an imperative sentence, reduce it to a glyph, charge it briefly, put it away, and write the log entry the same day. Then set a date four weeks out and decide in advance what evidence would count. That sequence needs no history at all, and it is a direct inheritance from the thread that documents itself.

The reason to build it this way is that it removes the temptation to use history as permission. If your practice stands or falls on its own record, then a nice lineage is a pleasure rather than a support. Most of the difficulty in this field comes from the arrangement being the other way round.

  1. Choose one question and write it as a single imperative sentence.
  2. Reduce it, charge it briefly, and put the object out of sight.
  3. Write the log entry on the same day, while the sequence is still legible.
  4. Set a review date in advance and decide what evidence would count.

What the record has to contain to be worth keeping

A source column and a result column, kept separately, and never allowed to support each other. The result column should be able to say no without the source column disagreeing, which sounds obvious and is difficult in practice because the writing is done in one pass and the results want to be interesting. Mine were not, and the value of the report is in the uninteresting parts.

The other requirement is a date on every claim. History is the field where the confidence of the prose drifts furthest ahead of the evidence, and dates are the cheapest correction available. Nine of the claims in my first draft had no date and four of them turned out to be nineteenth century, which is a better outcome than deleting them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where does the report actually find solid ground?

In the thread that documents itself. The experimental current of the early 1970s, its two foundational books, and its deliberate preference for testing over doctrine are all sourced from published material that practitioners themselves wrote and argued about. The other two threads are readable as history, and their lineages are not defensible from the record. A single well-documented method is worth more here than three partly-sourced ones.

Was the necromancy lineage simply invented?

Not invented, but unevidenced. There is excellent scholarship on how the word and the image both shifted, and the popular picture of a raised corpse is demonstrably a nineteenth-century literary construction. What is missing is any continuous practical technique across the gap. That could mean an undocumented transmission, or a repertoire reassembled from fragments, and neither claim has evidence behind it.

Is the nineteenth-century automata connection worth anything?

More than the version it is usually used to replace. The mechanical figures in that period's technical literature sit next to genuine ceremonial interest in mechanical symbolism, and the later convergence with computing is a real and traceable line. It is a relocation rather than a debunking, and the relocated version has better sources than the claim it corrects.

Would a paid history course be worth the money?

A source-led one, occasionally, and I would still check its citations myself. What is worth paying for is a bibliography you did not have, because a course that gives you the sources is doing the useful half of the work. A course that gives you conclusions without the references is just a more confident version of the free material, and confidence is the thing this field supplies for free.

How do I tell a documented lineage from a stitched one?

Ask what the source is for the join rather than for the pieces. Popular history in this field takes a real fact from each tradition, drops the dates, and presents the connection as though it were original. Three accurate claims joined by two undocumented joins produce a lineage no source supports, and the join is always the interesting part, which is why the lineage gets repeated.
