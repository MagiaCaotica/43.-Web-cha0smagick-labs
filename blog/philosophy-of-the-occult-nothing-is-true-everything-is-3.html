Philosophy of the Occult

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 11 min read

Nothing is true, everything is permitted is one sentence carrying at least four incompatible positions, and almost every use made of it in the last century has quietly chosen one of them and pretended the sentence said so. The line comes from the third stanza of the first verse of The Book of the Law, and the context in which it sits is a discipline of honesty rather than a licence for anything. Taking the sentence apart matters practically, not because the positions are all interesting but because three of them oblige you to very different things and you cannot tell which one you are operating from unless you have decided. The version worth holding is a structure: four positions, a test that distinguishes them, and a working that each of them can and cannot support.

One line, three positions, and a discipline

The line is doing at least three distinct jobs, and the jobs are not compatible. Epistemological relativism says there is no privileged standpoint from which claims are true. Moral nihilism says there is no binding standard of what ought to be done. Practical occultism says the operator's own commitments are what determine the operation and the consequences follow from them. These are three claims about three different subjects and they are routinely collapsed into one because they arrive in one sentence.

The fourth thing in the line is not a position at all and it is the one most often lost. The stanza does not stand alone, and the writing around it is about a method of stating things precisely and refusing flattery. A person who reads only the line and not the discipline gets a sentence with a much longer reach than the one the author wrote, and this is not a case of a text being misquoted deliberately. It is a case of a quotable fragment surviving while its conditions did not.

What the stanza actually says

The third stanza of the first verse reads as a declaration that no belief is to be held on authority and that no act is to be performed for a stated reason that is not a real reason. The standard translation is closer to do what thou wilt, and its parenthetical clause, in the fuller versions, is the instruction that no other shall say why. The difference between that and a licence is the whole subject of this article, and it is a difference in grammar rather than in tone.

The grammar is a conditional. Do what thou wilt is a statement about the source of an action's authority being the operator, and the parenthetical is a prohibition on misrepresenting that authority to other people. Read as a conditional, the line is a demand for accuracy about one's own motives, which is a discipline. Read as an unconditional, it is a permission slip, and the same words produce the permission slip only by dropping the condition.

Relativism, and what it commits you to

Epistemological relativism is the position that truth claims have no privileged standard, and holding it consistently has consequences that practitioners rarely want. If no standpoint is privileged, then your own practice has no privileged status, and neither does the practice of the person who dismisses it. The position is stable but it is expensive, because it removes the ground on which most arguments about methods are conducted.

Consistent relativism also makes the line inert as a permission. You cannot use no truth as a reason to do whatever you like, because the move from a claim about knowledge to a claim about permission is exactly the move relativism denies is available. This is a well-known move in moral philosophy and the point is worth making directly: the sentence is used as a permission slip far more often by people who are not relativists than by people who are, which suggests the line is being recruited to do work its content does not support.

Nihilism, and why it is not the same claim

Moral nihilism says there is no binding standard, which is a claim about values rather than about knowledge. The two are routinely merged and the merge is the source of most of the trouble. A person can hold the epistemological position and be entirely unwilling to abandon ordinary moral commitments, and a person can hold the moral position and be entirely willing to say that some claims are true and some are not.

Nihilism as a working position has a specific difficulty for practice, which is that it removes the reason to keep a record. If nothing binds, there is no reason to document what you did, and the record is the only part of a practice that can be checked. The pragmatic tradition's answer to nihilism is the one that survives contact with a year of workings: the operator still keeps the record, not because the record is obligatory but because the record is the only thing that distinguishes a practice from a habit.

Practical occultism, the position most people hold

The position almost everyone actually holds, including most people who quote the line, is neither of the two above. It is that the operator's own commitments determine what happens, that this fact is not news, and that the only discipline available is accuracy about what you are doing and honest reporting about what followed. This is a working position rather than a philosophy and it is far more defensible than either, because it makes no metaphysical claim at all.

It also has a virtue that the other two lack: it can be evaluated. A working built under this position produces a record, a record produces a reading, and the reading can come out negative. Relativism and nihilism both remove the ground on which a negative reading would mean anything, which is why the position that survives contact with practice is the modest one rather than the grand one.

The three framings: idealism, dualism, monism

Alongside the epistemological and moral readings sits a third family, which concerns the relation between practitioner and world. Idealism holds that the world is a construction of mind and therefore a practitioner who alters their own attention is altering something real. Gnostic dualism holds that there is a material world and something else, and that the two are systematically confused with each other, which makes most of the apparatus in this field a navigation device between mistaken levels. Mystical monism holds that there is one substance and the appearance of separation is the error.

These three give materially different advice to the same working. Under idealism, the aim is real and its alteration is the event. Under dualism, the aim is a navigation instruction and the practitioner should be careful about which level is being addressed. Under monism, the aim is a description of an existing situation and the useful operation is comprehension rather than command. Choosing between them is not a matter of finding the true one, since the practice does not adjudicate it, but it is a matter of being consistent, because a working that mixes all three gives an interpretation of any result at all.

Paraconsistency, and where it earns its keep

A paraconsistent logic is one in which some propositions do not have to resolve to true or false, and it is a live topic in the philosophy of language rather than an esoteric curiosity. Its relevance here is specific and practical. Most of what practitioners assert is a compound of a symbolic layer and a material layer held together by a connective, and the connective is where the incoherence lives. A sigil says this is the state of affairs. The ledger says it is not. A paraconsistent treatment allows both to stand without requiring either to collapse.

The cost of that permissiveness is immediate and worth naming: a logic in which contradictions are permitted will also permit a contradiction between a practice's stated aim and its observed result, which is exactly the case you most need to resolve. Paraconsistency is useful as a tool for keeping a symbolic statement from being read as a physical claim, and it is dangerous as a global stance, because it removes the pressure that would otherwise force you to say the aim was wrong.

Why the discipline is in the line and not around it

The parenthetical clause, no other shall say why, is the operative part and it is routinely quoted as though it were decorative. Read as a discipline it is a prohibition on false explanation, and it binds the practitioner in a specific way: you are not permitted to tell someone else a reason for your action that is not the real reason. This is a much narrower prohibition than a licence and it is a prohibition, which means it can be obeyed and broken.

The reason this matters operationally is that the discipline is the only part of the sentence that is falsifiable. A permission can be tested only by asking whether something was done, and anything can be done. A prohibition on false explanation is tested every time you report a working, and it is tested by a reader who asks a follow-up question. The line therefore contains a check, and the check is the part that has been dropped in almost every use made of it.

Where the quote gets used as a permission slip

The permission reading appears wherever the line is deployed to settle an argument the speaker is losing. Its function in those contexts is identical every time: it converts a question about a specific action into a question about the legitimacy of any questioning, which is a rhetorical move rather than a philosophical one. Anyone who can produce the line at the moment of a specific uncomfortable question is not using it as a philosophy.

There is a second, subtler use in which the line is cited to inoculate a practice against criticism. If nothing is true, then the failure of a practice to produce results cannot be a real failure, and the practice becomes unfalsifiable. This is the single most damaging consequence of the reading and it operates at the level of the community rather than the individual: it removes the standard that would let a method be retired, and a tradition that cannot retire a method accumulates methods.

A test you could apply to any claim

The test is short. State the claim as a sentence. Ask whether anything in the world would be different if the sentence were false, and if so what. Then ask whether you would be willing to stop if you had a year of records showing nothing. A claim that passes all three is operational even if it is metaphysical, and a claim that fails the third is a mood, regardless of how it is phrased.

Applied to the line itself, the test is interesting. Nothing is true, everything is permitted passes the first question trivially and fails the second in an interesting way, since almost anything in the world would be different if it were false. It fails the third decisively, which is why the line, taken as a claim about practice, cannot be the operating principle of anyone who keeps records. This is not an argument that it is wrong. It is an observation about the kind of thing it is.

The working this philosophy can support

The working this philosophy can support is a small one with a written aim, a compressed charge, a fortnight of countable follow-through, and a record that opens before the outcome. Nothing in that sequence requires any of the four positions, which is the reason the tradition keeps functioning while the arguments about its philosophy continue. The sequence is a set of commitments about behaviour and the commitments are available to an idealist, a dualist, a monist, a relativist, a nihilist, and someone who holds none of them.

What the philosophy adds is not power and it is not permission. It adds a requirement of consistency, in the sense that you should know which of the framings you are operating under and should not switch between them mid-working to accommodate a result. The one result that is theoretically impossible is the one that supports all four positions at once, and the arrival of such a result is the signal that something in the interpretation has been adjusted to fit.

What the line cannot tell you

It cannot tell you which of the framings is correct, and there is no procedure for finding out. It cannot tell you whether a given working should be continued, because continuation is a decision about method and the line makes no claims about method. And it cannot tell you that a result was meaningful, since the sentence contains no criterion for meaning and supplies none. Everything it appears to supply on these questions has been supplied by the reader.

This is the honest limit and it is worth stating in a field where limits are usually presented as failures of conviction. The line is a statement about the source of authority for action and it is one of the sharper ones available. It is not a method, a criterion, or a warrant, and the enormous literature that treats it as all three is a literature about the uses people have found for a sentence rather than about the sentence.

The sentence worth keeping

The sentence worth keeping is the fuller form with its condition attached, because the condition is where the work is. Do what thou wilt, and say no other reason than the true one. That version obliges you to know your own motive, which is difficult, and to report it accurately, which is more difficult, and it survives the year. The truncated version obliges you to nothing and is therefore comfortable, and comfort is the main thing a permission slip is for.

If you want a single test for whether you have understood the line rather than borrowed it, use this: describe, in ordinary language, why you did a specific thing in a specific working, and check whether the description would survive being read by the person it concerns. If it would, you are operating inside the discipline. If it would only survive inside your own head, you are operating on the truncated version, and the truncation is the whole problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the phrase nothing is true, everything is permitted actually come from?

The third stanza of the first verse of The Book of the Law, whose standard translation is do what thou wilt, with a parenthetical clause in the fuller versions forbidding the speaker from giving any other reason for an action. Read as a conditional it is a demand for accuracy about one's own motives. Read without the condition it becomes a permission, which is the version that circulates.

How do relativism and nihilism differ as interpretations?

Relativism is a claim about knowledge: no standpoint is privileged, so truth claims have no external standard. Nihilism is a claim about values: no standard binds conduct. They are routinely merged and the merge causes the trouble, because relativism removes the ground on which arguments about methods are conducted, while nihilism removes the reason to keep any record of what you did.

What is paraconsistency and why is it relevant to occult practice?

A paraconsistent logic allows some propositions to stand without resolving to true or false. It is relevant because a sigil's symbolic claim and a ledger's material claim are usually joined by a connective that the practice cannot support. The tool is useful for keeping a symbolic statement from being read as a physical claim, and it is dangerous as a global stance, because it removes the pressure that would otherwise force you to conclude an aim was wrong.

Why is the line in the same sentence as a discipline rather than a licence?

Because the operative clause is a prohibition on false explanation, and a prohibition can be obeyed and broken while a permission cannot be tested at all. It is also the only falsifiable part: it is tested every time you report a working, by anyone who asks a follow-up question. The sentence contains a check, and that check is the part that has been dropped in nearly every use made of it.

What test would distinguish an operational claim from a mood?

State the claim as a sentence. Ask whether anything would be different in the world if it were false. Then ask whether you would stop after a year of records showing nothing. Claims that pass all three are operational even when metaphysical; claims that fail the third are moods regardless of phrasing, which is why the line itself fails this test as a claim about practice.
