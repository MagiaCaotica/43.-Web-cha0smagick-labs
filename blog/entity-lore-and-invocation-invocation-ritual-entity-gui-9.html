Entity Lore and Invocation: Field Notes on Working With Names

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

The most useful habit in entity work costs nothing and takes ten minutes: read the entry before you work with the name. Most of what gets described as a frightening encounter with a spirit turns out to be a practitioner's reaction to having summoned something they had not read. The Ars Goetia is the most structured entity catalogue in Western grimoire literature, and that structure is exactly what the popular version throws away. Each spirit is given a rank, a visible form, an office and specific powers, and the office is the part that matters operationally. It tells you what the thing is described as doing, which is what you need in order to know whether you picked the right instrument. These are field notes, gathered from working through catalogues and from the ways entity practice drifts.

Evocation and Invocation Do Different Jobs

In classical ceremonial usage, evocation calls a spirit to appear and be perceived, and invocation invites its influence to operate through the operator instead. Two different operations, not two words for the same one, and most modern usage collapses them into a single vague idea of calling something up.

The distinction has practical consequences. Evocation puts the entity in the room, which in the classical scheme means apparatus, containment, a proper circle and a lot of preparation. Invocation keeps the entity out of the room and routes its quality through you, which requires far less and gives a much narrower result.

Chaos magic collapsed this distinction on purpose. The reduced method is a defined intention, a sigil and a stated frame, and that has always been sufficient for the operations it was written for. Working with a full classical evocation in a modern self-directed context is mostly a way of importing risk that the framework you are using was designed to remove.

The Catalogue Is the Point

The Ars Goetia lists seventy-two spirits, each with a rank, a form and a stated office. The office is the functional content, and it is the part the popularised version drops. An entry that says a spirit grants riches, or teaches a craft, or moves a horse, is telling you what the operation is for.

Treat it as a tool catalogue rather than a bestiary. Nobody uses a hammer by reading up on the folklore of hammers. The office is the specification, the form is the packaging, and the rank tells you how much effort the classical authors thought the thing required.

This also dissolves a lot of the mischief. A spirit described as a King of the East who is also the bringer of losses is not an inconsistency to be frightened of. It is a description of a domain, and the classical authors were not writing a psychology textbook. Read the office, pick the spirit whose office matches the domain you need, and stop there.

Entities as Pattern Fragments

Peter Carroll's position was that entities are best regarded as aspects of the magician's own mind rather than external beings. It is a functional definition more than a metaphysical claim, and it does more work than any scepticism about the entities themselves, because it neutralises the fear without requiring you to reject the method.

The modern version of this is a pattern fragment. A named entity is a bundle of associations with a particular flavour, and invoking it is a way of routing your attention through that bundle rather than working it out from first principles each time. The catalogue is a library of shortcuts, and the value is the same value any vocabulary provides.

That framing also explains the classic use: disrupting repetitive thought. A rigid loop, an obsession, a stuck position, and a strongly-different named quality is applied deliberately. The description that works in practice is that you borrowed a perspective that was not in the room, which is why the operation is useful even under the least generous reading of the mechanism.

The Actual Hazard Is the Unread Name

There is no reliable evidence that working with a name produces external danger. The hazard is procedural and it is very real: working with an entity you have never researched, which means working with a symbol whose office, framing and any relevant warnings you do not know.

A modern video essay, an app listing and a grimoire page can give you the same name and the opposite guidance. One says a spirit is generous with resources, another says it demands payment first, and the popular version usually omits both. That is a practice hazard, not a supernatural one, and it is entirely preventable by reading.

The ten-minute rule is this. Read the entry in a source you can actually check. Note the rank and the office. Note anything the source says about conditions or cost. Then decide whether the office matches the domain you need. If it does not, pick a different name rather than interpreting the failure as a message.

Reduced Apparatus Was a Real Decision

A defined intention, a sigil and a stated frame is historically sufficient, and practitioners who add a full circle usually report that the extra apparatus produces more self-consciousness rather than more effect. The circle was never the mechanism; it was a way of concentrating attention in a room full of other people doing the same thing.

The one thing worth keeping from classical practice is the declared frame itself. Saying what is happening, to whom, and for how long is the operative move. Everything else is optional decoration, and decoration that takes twenty minutes to build is decoration that is competing with the work.

The recorded version of the classical form is a useful reference anyway. Knowing what the elaborate version looks like tells you which elements were functional and which were social, and the answer is usually that the declaration, the containment and the closing were functional and the rest was atmosphere.

Grounding Is Not Optional

The tradition's own advice is short daily practice rather than escalating intensity, and that advice exists because the failure mode is well known: fixation, sleep disruption, and a general narrowing of attention that is easy to mistake for progress.

Grounding after entity work is not a formal banishing ritual in most modern practice. It is something much simpler: food, water, daylight, movement, and a note about what you did. A written record is grounding in itself, because it moves the operation from a state you are in to a thing you did.

The line for reducing intensity is straightforward. If practice is interfering with sleep, work or relationships, the practice is too large, and the traditional instruction and the practical instruction agree. Frequency first, duration second, intensity last, and never the other way round.

A Working Method

The sequence matters because step one refuses the first name that comes to mind. A domain-first approach means the entity serves the operation, which is the correct order and the reverse of how the popular version frames it.

The written frame in step four is the piece most people skip and most people benefit from. Writing what you are doing, to whom, and on what date turns a state you can misremember into a record you can check, and it also makes the closing step automatic.

  1. Name the domain first. Write the operational problem in one sentence without any entity in it.
  2. Search the catalogue for an office that matches that domain, and check at least one alternate source for the entry.
  3. Read the rank and note any conditions, costs or warnings the source actually states.
  4. Build the operation as a sigil with a stated frame, and record the frame in writing before the charge.
  5. Close with food, water and daylight, and write the entry the same day rather than the next morning.

Where Entities Come From in Practice

In use, an entity work is usually a redirect of attention. You take the question, name a domain, select an entity whose stated office matches, and then do something with the resulting state: write under it, act while holding it, or use it to break a loop you were stuck in.

The entity is doing the same work a prompt would do in a writing exercise. It supplies a distinct flavour quickly, and distinctness is the active ingredient. This is why the catalogue is genuinely worth having even under the least generous reading of what the entities are.

Recording Entity Work Properly

A dated entry with the entity named, the office you selected for, the frame you declared and the outcome is the whole requirement. Four fields, a minute of writing, and it turns a series of vivid experiences into something you can look back on without the memory rewriting itself.

Without it, entity work is unusually prone to a specific distortion, because the experience is vivid and the memory is generous. What you remember a year later is the version that has already been shaped by what you expected, which is precisely the part you would want to check.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I work with demons?

No reliable evidence points to external danger, so the question is better asked as a procedural one. The real hazard is working with a name you have never researched, which means not knowing the stated office, the rank, or any conditions the source mentions. Read the entry first, in a source you can verify, and confirm the office matches the domain you actually need. Most of the fear in this field comes from working blind rather than from the work.

What is the difference between evocation and invocation?

Evocation calls the entity to appear and be perceived, which in the classical scheme means apparatus, containment and a circle. Invocation asks it to operate through you instead, which needs almost nothing and produces a narrower result. Modern usage collapses the two, and chaos magic collapsed them on purpose, dropping to a defined intention, a sigil and a stated frame. The distinction is worth keeping even in reduced practice, because it tells you which operation you are actually describing.

Do I need a full ritual circle?

No, and adding one mostly adds twenty minutes of setup and a good deal more self-consciousness. The mechanism was never the circle. What the circle provided was a declared boundary, a stated frame and a definite end, and all three survive without the apparatus. Build the working out of a defined intention, a sigil and a frame you say out loud, then write the frame down.

How is entity work different in chaos magic?

The classical framework is relaxed rather than rejected. Instead of a formal relationship with a being, you get a named pattern you route attention through, and the catalogue functions as a library of shortcuts with distinct flavours. That is why Peter Carroll's framing as aspects of your own mind works so well: it removes the fear without asking you to drop the method, and it makes the catalogue's usefulness the point rather than an afterthought.

Which catalogue app or tool is actually worth paying for?

The catalogue is old and widely available, so you are not buying information and there is little reason to rent access to a text that has been public for three hundred years. What is not free is the bookkeeping around it: the entry you wrote, the frame you declared, the date, the outcome. If a paid tool keeps your entity work in one dated log with its office notes attached, that has a real use, and it is the use I would pay for. I would buy that once rather than subscribe for access to a grimoire, because the grimoire was never the scarce part and the log is.
