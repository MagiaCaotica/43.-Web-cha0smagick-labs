Wealth and Abundance

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 13 min read

A money sigil is a compression of one imperative sentence, charged once and then dismissed. That is the whole of the technique, and the whole of the confusion in the popular material comes from inflating it. This guide sets out the sequence end to end, in the order that actually produces results, with the numbers left in rather than rounded off. It covers how to name a target you could actually check, how to reduce that target to a glyph, when to charge it, what to write down while it is still fresh, and what to do on the date you set for review. It is written for a practitioner who has done one or two workings already and now wants the money version done properly rather than theatrically. No part of this requires you to believe that the sigil moves money. The claim being made here is narrower and checkable: the method commits you to one specific action and keeps the goal available at the moment a decision arrives, and that is worth testing on your own record.

Part one, name the amount rather than the feeling

The most common first step is also the most commonly skipped: nobody writes down what they actually want. A sigil aimed at having more money is aimed at nothing, because more than what, and by how much, and received by when, are all unstated. The reduction then produces a glyph for a mood rather than for a target, and afterwards nobody can say whether it did anything.

Write one sentence in the imperative with a number in it. Not a figure you would feel good about, a figure you would actually bank. A specific amount by a specific date, from a specific source if you can name it. This is uncomfortable for most people, and the discomfort is informative, because it usually means the number is larger than the plan supporting it.

If you cannot name a number, name the action instead. A specific application sent, a conversation had, a price raised, a debt cleared. Action targets are easier to run and far easier to evaluate, and they carry most of the benefit of the method. A sigil for a job interview and a sigil for sixty thousand dollars are not equally good targets, and it is not obvious from the outside which is which.

  • Weak, because it cannot be checked: I want more money, I want abundance, I want to feel secure
  • Acceptable, because it names an act: send the invoice, ask for the raise, make the call
  • Strong, because it names an amount and a date: bank three thousand by the end of the quarter

Part two, reduce the request to a single imperative

Reduction is the compression step: taking the sentence you wrote and turning it into a glyph that stands for it. This is the method formalised by Austin Osman Spare in the early 1900s and it remains the standard charge method in modern practice, which is not a coincidence, since it is the only step that does the actual work of making the target a single object you can hold.

The word that matters in your sentence is imperative. Requests, hopes and wishes compress into something vague, because a wish has no tense and no direction. Commands compress cleanly, which is why practitioners phrase targets as commands, and why the form of the sentence is a practical matter rather than a stylistic one.

Write the reduced sentence on the back of the sheet as well as in the glyph. When the result arrives, or fails to, you want to be certain that the glyph you charged was the one you meant. Writing it down costs three seconds and separates a record from an anecdote.

Part three, choose the window before you choose the day

The honest position on timing is that it is secondary, and the popular position is that it is primary. Both can be partly right, and the reconciliation is worth having. A carefully scheduled glyph pointed at a vague aim loses to an improvised one pointed at a target you could act on this week, and the sky plays no part in the arithmetic.

That is not an argument against timing. It is an argument for spending your effort on the target first and the calendar second. Once the sentence is sharp, choosing a day costs nothing and gives you a fixed point to work from, which is worth something on its own terms even if the date itself is arbitrary.

Fix a window rather than a day. Thirty days is too short for most money situations and ninety is long enough to see something without losing the thread. Write the start date, the review date, and the specific measurable thing you will look at on the review date. Three items, on paper, before anything else happens.

Part four, charge it, briefly and on purpose

Charging does not have a fixed duration and no source specifies one. What the sources do specify is a shape: you address the target, you attend to the glyph, and you finish. Everything else is elaboration. Practitioners who describe forty-minute charges are describing something they invented and then found difficult to maintain daily, which is the most common way a practice gets abandoned in the first month.

Keep it short enough to do on a bad day. Five minutes is enough for most workings. The reason brevity is the right choice is not mystical, it is that the thing you are actually building is a habit of attention, and a habit that survives a bad week is worth more than a better charge you only perform when everything is going well.

Do it the same way each time. Same place, same time if you can manage it, same posture, same closing action. Repetition is doing more work here than intensity, and it is the one variable that reliably improves results when people report on what changed.

  1. Sit somewhere you already associate with being deliberate, not somewhere you associate with scrolling
  2. Read the reduced sentence aloud once, so the glyph has a referent in your own voice
  3. Attend to the glyph for the length you decided, without adding anything to the original sentence
  4. Close by putting the glyph somewhere specific and physically out of sight, and note the time

Part five, dismiss it and put it away

The dismissal is the step nearly everyone skips, and skipping it is the most likely single cause of a money sigil producing anxiety rather than results. The working is not finished when the attention stops. It is finished when the glyph is put away, and until it is put away the request is still live in your attention and will be picked up every time you wonder how it is going.

That wondering is the mechanism by which a sigil becomes compulsive checking, and compulsive checking is the failure mode that actually damages people. The practice is supposed to remove the need to think about the goal, because thinking about the goal is what you were already doing and it was not working.

Decide now where the glyph goes and write it down. A drawer, an envelope, a box, anywhere you will not see it. Then do not look at it again until the review date. If you find yourself wanting to check, that is the signal that the practice is being run backwards, and the correction is to stop rather than to check.

Part six, log the entry the same day

Write the record on the day of the working, not later. Three lines: the sentence, the date, and one sentence about what you did in the surrounding hours if anything unusual happened. The reason for same-day is not sentimentality, it is that later reconstruction is heavily biased toward the memorable, and the memorable is not what you are trying to measure.

The key discipline is writing the entries with nothing in them. On most days of a ninety-day window you will not be thinking about the target, and those days do not belong in the log. A record of contact only will confirm any belief you started with, which is precisely the failure this whole method is designed to avoid.

If you skip a day, mark it skipped rather than leaving a gap. A gap is ambiguous and will be read as a success in hindsight, and a marked gap is a fact. This is a small bookkeeping habit and it is the one that determines whether your record can tell you anything at review.

Part seven, review on the date you fixed

On the review date, before you extend anything, answer three questions in writing. What was the specific measurable thing I said I would look at. What is the value of it now. And what would I tell a friend in exactly this position, which is a more useful question than it sounds because it removes the sunk cost of your own framing.

Whatever the arithmetic says, write the arithmetic. If the number moved, say by how much and in which direction. If it did not, say it did not. A second cycle on a target that produced nothing is a reasonable thing to try, but only after you have recorded the first result accurately, because the alternative is running the same experiment until one of them works.

Then decide what changes. Usually it is the target rather than the technique. A vague target, a window that was too short, an action that was never taken, or a target that depended on somebody else deciding yes. All four are common, all four are fixable, and the record is what tells you which one applies.

What the tradition claims, and what it does not

Occult literature has treated money as a favourable target for centuries, and there is a reason for that which has nothing to do with money's spiritual properties. It is a strongly symbolic quantity: universal, quantifiable, and socially legible in a way most goals are not. You can say whether you have more or less. Almost nothing else in life offers that.

That is a reasonable observation about a good target and it is not a mechanism. No source in the tradition describes a sigil creating money from nothing, and the sources that do discuss money describe access, luck, discovery, or the removal of an obstacle. Those are four different outcomes and none of them is fabrication.

Modern practice tends to be explicit about this. Chaos magic as a system was built to be pragmatic, and the position it inherited is that you do not need to hold any belief about mechanism for the operation to be valid. That is a stronger claim than it first appears, because it puts the burden of proof on the method rather than on your agreement, and the method is testable.

The number in the abundance material, and where it comes from

A specific number recurs across abundance practice, usually written as three digits, and it is worth knowing its actual status. The practice uses it because the digits sum to twelve, and twelve is a figure that recurs across temperament charts and numerological systems. The connection is drawn from the numerology, not from physics.

Nothing in the material claims a physical mechanism, and you should be suspicious of anyone who supplies one on your behalf. The number functions as a focus, the way a repeated word functions in a mantra: it gives the attention something to rest on, and it is harmless. What is not harmless is the confident explanation attached to it by people selling something.

If you use it, use it as a focus and say so. If you would rather not, the method works identically with a plain imperative sentence and no numerology at all, which is a useful thing to know before you buy anything that suggests otherwise.

Why sigilisation rather than a spoken request

If a sigil is a compression of a sentence, why not just say the sentence? The honest answer is that you mostly can, and many people get comparable results from writing the request down once and putting it in a drawer. The sigil adds three specific things and no others: it compresses, which forces the target to be sharp; it makes the request an object you can hold, which separates it from a thought; and the charge gives it a definite boundary in time.

The compression is the part that earns its keep. Any request with a vague element in it will lose that element in the reduction, which forces you to notice it and either fix it or drop it. People report this as the sigil clarifying their goal, and it is, but the clarification is happening in the writing rather than in the glyph.

The boundary is the second real gain. A request with no end point stays live, which is how a practice turns into a worry. A charge with a defined start and end does not, and the dismissal is what closes it. Everything else people attribute to the glyph is either the compression working or the habit working.

A worked example with the figures left in

A practitioner writes: I send the outstanding invoice of two thousand four hundred within fourteen days and receive payment in full. The sentence is imperative, has a number, a window and a defined completion, and it can be marked true or false on a specific date by opening an accounts screen. That last property is what makes it a good target, and it is not a property most money sigils have.

The reduction produces a glyph. The charge takes five minutes on a Thursday morning. The glyph goes in an envelope in a drawer. The log records the date, the sentence and the first three days with nothing in them, which is most of what happens in a fourteen-day window, and the fourteenth day arrives.

If the invoice was not paid, the honest conclusion is that the sigil failed to move money, and the useful conclusion is that the target depended on somebody else. Which of those two conclusions you draw determines whether the next cycle is the same experiment again or a different one, and only one of them produces improvement.

The compulsion risk, named before it arrives

The practical risk of wealth work is psychological and it is specific. Tying your sense of worth to a result produces anxiety, then compulsive checking, then impulsive decisions made to force the outcome faster. None of that is supernatural and all of it is well documented as a response to uncertainty about money.

The defences are structural rather than motivational, which means they have to be built in before the anxiety arrives. Fix the review date and do not move it. Hide the glyph. Keep the log daily rather than reviewing on demand. None of these require willpower during a bad week, which is the only time willpower is actually being asked for.

And set the actual stop condition in advance, in writing, while you are calm. If you are checking daily, stop. If the practice has started displacing work, sleep or relationships, stop. Those are the criteria the tradition uses, they are simple, and deciding them in advance is the only way they will be honoured.

  1. Daily checking means the practice is being run backwards, and the fix is to stop rather than to check
  2. Sleep, work or relationships being displaced means reduce the intensity, not increase the discipline
  3. A target that only ever depends on somebody else deciding yes should be replaced, not retried
  4. Wealth work that reliably produces a calm, planned week is working; one that produces dread is not

What to change on a second cycle

On a second cycle, change one thing. Not the glyph, not the timing and the number and the duration. One variable, because a change you cannot attribute teaches you nothing, and attribution is the entire reason for keeping the log.

The most useful single change is usually the target. Sharper, more measurable, and less dependent on a third party's decision. The second most useful is the window. Most people under-run it and conclude the method failed when they had simply not given it long enough to produce a slow result.

If the first cycle produced a result, resist the urge to amplify. The temptation is to go bigger, which is how a well-functioning small practice becomes an obsession with a number attached. Keep the size, keep the schedule, and let the accumulated evidence rather than the ambition decide what the third cycle looks like.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a money sigil take to work?

Thirty to ninety days is the working window most people use, and the choice matters less than fixing it in advance. Thirty days suits actions you control and ninety suits anything that involves another party's schedule. The important part is setting the date before the charge and writing down what you will look at, because an open-ended window produces a practice you check daily and abandon by the third week.

Should I charge a money sigil on a particular day?

Secondary, and the evidence for a timing effect is thin. Clock precision buys you nothing at all when the underlying request has no clear object: a beautifully scheduled sigil pointed at a vague desire underperforms an untimed one aimed at something you could actually do on Tuesday, because the clarity of the target is doing far more work than the calendar. Fix the sentence first, then pick a date for the scheduling benefit rather than the cosmic one.

Is wealth magic ever dangerous?

The practical risk is psychological, not supernatural. Tying self-worth to a result produces anxiety, compulsive checking and impulsive decisions. That risk is managed structurally rather than through willpower: fix a review date, hide the glyph, log daily, and decide in advance that daily checking means you stop. A practice that leaves you calmer and more planned has worked; one that leaves you checking is being run backwards.

Do money sigils actually work?

No evidence exists that a sigil moves money by itself, and the honest claim is narrower. What a glyph actually does is narrow the field of possibilities to one thing you have already agreed to do, and keep that agreement close at hand when a decision point arrives. That is a real effect and it is measurable, which is a better position than a claim you cannot check.

What does a paid sigil generator actually add to this?

The reduction is a hundred characters of letter shapes and a drawing, and a paper and pen do it perfectly well. What a paid tool reliably gives you is a rendered output worth looking at, a reusable library so you can find last month's glyph, and no transcription errors. Those are conveniences rather than mechanisms, and the method is complete without any of them.
