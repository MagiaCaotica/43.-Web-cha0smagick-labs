Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Somebody arrives at chaos magic having already been sold to it, usually by a video that skipped the first two years of reading in order to get to the sigil. This page is the other route. It assumes nothing, it does not ask you to believe anything, and it is written in the order the sequence actually has to happen in rather than the order that makes for good television. Over the first three months you will do very little that looks like magic, and by the end of it you will have a small practice with a written record, which is the only thing this tradition reliably produces. Everything that follows is arranged so that each step only makes sense once the previous one is done, and the order is not negotiable.

The first month is reading, and nobody makes that clear

Peter Carroll's Liber Null and psi, published in 1977, is short, cheap and still the entry point. John Crowley's Liber Kaos is longer and covers the same ground from a different direction. Reading both is not a scholarly exercise; it is how you find out what the founders thought they were proposing.

What you will notice is how little metaphysical doctrine either of them insists on. The emphasis is on method, on writing things down, and on being willing to be wrong. If you arrive expecting initiatory mystery you will find the tone flat, and that reaction is itself useful information about what you came looking for.

Where the name comes from, and why it matters less than it looks

The name is a deliberate echo of chaos magic and of the Chaos enthroned motif in Crowley's Book of the Law, and the movement treated the affiliation as intentional rather than borrowed. The conventional starting point is the 1976 founding of the Ordo Templi Ouroborus, though the writing that shaped it predates that by several years.

None of this settles anything. What is worth taking from the history is the shape of it: a small group, a pamphlet, a refusal to require belief as a condition of membership. The tradition's most durable contribution is methodological rather than metaphysical, because it made it acceptable to test a claim rather than inherit it.

Week one, one sentence and nothing else

Write one sentence describing something you want, in the imperative mood, with a number or a date in it. This is harder than it sounds, because most of the sentences people actually want to write have no number in them. Keep rewriting until one of them does.

Then stop. Do not reduce it, do not draw anything, do not buy anything. A week spent on the sentence alone will teach you more than a month spent buying equipment you do not yet need, and the equipment is genuinely optional for the first several months of any practice in this field.

Week two, reduce the sentence by hand

The traditional method is letter reduction: drop the vowels, keep the consonants, and arrange what remains into a shape you like. Draw it badly. The gesture matters, the result does not, and people who spend a weekend photographing their glyph have not started.

Encoding and activation are meant to be separate acts, and keeping them apart is the single most useful piece of the method. Reduction is a thinking step. Charging is a doing step. Confusing them produces a practice that feels busy and produces no record worth reading.

Week three, charge it badly and finish anyway

The charge should be short. Concentrate on the sentence, not the shape, and stop before you are tempted to add more. Then put the object somewhere specific and out of sight, and do not look at it again for the length of your observation window.

Forgetting is the step everyone skips and it is the one that makes the practice work at all. An object you keep in your pocket gets inspected, re-interpreted and doubted daily, and daily inspection supplies more anxiety than the charge ever supplied conviction.

Week four, write down what you would count

Before anything can be assessed, you have to say in advance what would count. One line, dated, next to the sentence. A specific outcome by a specific date, or a specified absence of one, and no revision once the date has passed.

This is the step the founders emphasised and the one that gets abandoned first, usually because it reveals that the target was vague. That revelation is the point. A pre-registered prediction is uncomfortable precisely when the goal was not really a goal.

What belief is doing in all of this

Not very much, and that is a feature. The system was built on the assumption that you need not hold any particular view about mechanism for the method to operate. A sceptic can run every step in this page and keep whatever results come out, and the results are usually behavioural rather than supernatural.

What belief does change is compliance. If you expect a sigil to do something, you will write the sentence, reduce it, charge it and log it. If you expect nothing, you will skip the logging, and the logging is where any of this becomes knowable.

The three risks, named in advance

Fixation is the first. A symbol becomes load-bearing for a mood or a self-image, and adjusting it feels like changing yourself. The signal is that you check it more often than your stated window allows.

Isolation is the second. Practice that only makes sense to other practitioners tends to narrow the world. The signal is that explaining the practice to anyone has become impossible. Sleep disruption is the third, and it is the most physiological and least voluntary of the three.

  • If the practice starts costing you sleep, reduce it immediately, not gradually.
  • If you cannot explain it to a friend, it has narrowed too far.
  • If a symbol starts carrying your sense of self, put it away for a season.

What the second month is for

Nothing new. A second sigil, a second window, the same schedule. The temptation at this point is to add tools, correspondence tables, planetary timing, and any of it will feel like progress because the calendar is filling up.

Add exactly one thing in the second month, and add it only after you can show me a first window's record without embarrassment. The tools are the cheapest and least important part of this, which is the opposite of how most people start.

What you should have at the end of ninety days

A notebook with a sentence, a date, a prediction, and an outcome in it, several times over. Probably no results you would put in a story. A working knowledge of what your own attention does when it is directed at a specific written target, which is more than most people can say.

If you have that, you have the tradition's actual product. Everything after this point is refinement, and refinement is a much less urgent problem than the first ninety days were.

Correspondence, and the honest case for ignoring it

The tradition has tables linking planets to metals to colours to numbers to days, and those tables were codified in the printed synthesis that came out of the sixteenth century. Used consistently they function as a memory system: they give a structure to build work inside, and they make your own choices legible to you six months later.

They do nothing to the outcome, and the reason people gravitate towards them is that the alternative is sitting still with an uncertain target. A table gives you something to decide, and deciding something is not the same as the target being real. That substitution happens very quietly.

Keep the tables if you like having a system, and drop them if the system is not helping you write things down. That is the whole test, and it is a better test than whether the correspondence is traditional or reconstructed, which is a question very few people actually need answered.

Timing, and the specific error beginners make with it

Timing a working on a chosen date is not harmful, and it does commit you to a start, which is harder to reverse than a mood. The error is spending more attention on the date than on the sentence. A carefully chosen hour aimed at a vague target produces less than an arbitrary one aimed at something you could act on this week.

If you want to use timing, decide the sentence first and treat the date as a scheduling convenience. If you find yourself computing transits while the target is still undecided, the calculation is functioning as a way of not deciding, and it will feel exactly like diligence while it is happening.

The instruction the tradition actually gives on this point is closer to common sense than to doctrine, and the version most often quoted is the one saying the clarity of the target matters more than the calendar. That is not mysticism. It is simply true about attention, and it is the one piece of practical advice in the corpus nobody disputes.

Writing the ninety-day review honestly

At the end of the quarter there are three questions, and the answers go in writing. How many of your pre-registered predictions were decided in your favour. Which of the three steps you most often skipped. And what, if anything, you would change in the procedure rather than in yourself.

The third question is the one that matters, because the first two describe a record and the third produces a plan. Most people at this point want to change the target, and almost nobody wants to change the logging, which is where the problem usually was all along and which is the part nobody enjoys revising.

Write the review in one sitting and do not open the notebook while you do. The review is a document about your method rather than a summary of your entries, and the two collapse into each other the moment you look back at what you wrote.

The part where the plan stops being about sigils

By week seven most people in this position have a written record and a specific target, and the natural next move is to ask what else the tradition offers. That is the point at which the reading list expands fastest and the return drops sharpest, because nearly all of the material past this stage is interpretation rather than method.

The tradition's own position is that you already have every tool. A statement, a reduction, a charge, a record, a deadline. Everything else in the corpus is decoration on top of that, and some of the decoration actively gets in the way, because interpretation is far more satisfying than bookkeeping and nobody is ever made to do the second one twice.

Where most beginners stop, and why

The common stopping point is the first null. A window closes, nothing happened, and the practice is abandoned as though a failure had been demonstrated. The second common one is boredom in week three, before any window has closed at all, which is less rational and considerably more frequent.

Both stoppages are survivable and neither is evidence that anything has been discovered. The tradition's own framing helps here: you are running a test, tests are mostly negative, and the person who ends up with a usable result is almost never the one whose first two attempts worked.

The remedy is not more practice but a smaller one, done more reliably, which is the advice the tradition gives everywhere else in this document and applies just as well to the abandonment problem as to the rest of it.

What ninety days is supposed to leave you with

A notebook with a sentence, a date, a prediction and an outcome in it, several times over. Probably no results you would put in a story. A working knowledge of what your own attention does when it is pointed at a specific written target, which is more than most people can say about any practice they have attempted.

If you have that, you have what the tradition actually produces, and everything after this point is refinement. Refinement is a much less urgent problem than the first ninety days were, and most of the people who stall do so by starting it too soon.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does the first ninety days actually cost?

Two cheap books and a notebook. Everything in this page can be done with a pen and paper indefinitely, and the sequence works better that way because the steps stay small enough to finish on a bad week. Buy equipment only once you know which of your own actions you actually repeat.

Do I need to believe any of it for this to work?

No, and the founders were explicit about that. The system does not require you to hold a view about mechanism. What belief does change is whether you complete the steps, and the steps are where anything knowable comes from.

Is it dangerous to start?

The material itself is not, and the ordinary risks are fixation, isolation and sleep disruption rather than anything dramatic. All three are reduced by a short daily routine rather than one that escalates, which is the tradition's own advice and is worth taking literally.

What should a beginner buy first, if anything?

Nothing for the first few months. If you want one thing, a notebook that nobody else will ever read is worth more than any app, because the record is the only part of this that produces knowledge. A purchase that stores the record for you is reasonable; one that designs sigils for you is not where the value is.

What if the first window produces nothing?

That is a result, and it is the most common one. Write it down as a result rather than as a failure of the method, run a second window with a different target, and resist the urge to change the practice. The people who learn anything here are the ones whose first several attempts produced nothing.
