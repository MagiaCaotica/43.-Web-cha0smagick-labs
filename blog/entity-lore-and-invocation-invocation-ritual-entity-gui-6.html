Entity Lore and Invocation

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

A reference sheet for this material is easy to buy and hard to use. The problem is not information. Each entry in the standard catalogue carries a rank, a visible form, an office, and a listed set of powers, which is more structure than almost any comparable body of material offers, and structure is exactly what a reference needs. The problem is that most people read the columns in the wrong order and end up working with the name instead of the function. This page is organised around the function. It treats the catalogue as a filing system rather than a bestiary, keeps the evocation and invocation distinction intact rather than flattening it, and spends most of its space on the one column that does the work. It also includes the exit route, and the price of taking it, because a reference that only describes the traditional reading is not much use when the traditional reading is what you need to step around.

Ninety seconds before you start

The job of a reference is to let you find something under pressure. You have a problem, you have ninety seconds before you start, and you need to identify which entry describes that problem. That is the only requirement, and it is much narrower than people assume when they buy a book about this subject expecting to be taught something.

Everything else is a bonus. Understanding the history of the material, the theology behind it, the differences between manuscript versions, and the reception history are all worth having and none of them are the function of a reference. Keeping the two categories straight is what stops a beginner from spending a year learning context and never once using the catalogue.

The two words, and the distinction worth keeping

The traditional usage separates calling a spirit so that it appears from summoning that influence to work through the operator. Most modern writing collapses these, and the collapse is not harmless. An appearance is something you observe and record. An influence is something that works through you, which means it is indistinguishable from your own action unless you are careful about how you write it down.

Keeping the distinction is a practical safeguard rather than an etymological nicety. If you build a working on the appearance model, your record contains events you can describe. If you build it on the influence model, your record contains your own behaviour, and you have to write it in the third person or you will attribute it to something else. The second is the more common modern practice and it needs more discipline, not less.

The four fields as a filing system

Rank, form, office, powers. Treated as a table, this is one of the most usable structures in the whole of Western grimoire literature, and treating it as a table is the whole trick. Each column answers a different question: where does this entry sit, what does it look like, what is it for, and what is it said to be able to do.

The columns are not equally reliable. The office column is usually the most specific and the least paraphrased in careful editions, because it is the load-bearing part. The powers column is where paraphrase runs wild, because the language is dramatic and second-hand sources amplify it. Rank is nearly useless on its own. Form is thin but reliable. Build your working from the office and treat the rest as flavour.

The office is the only actionable field

An office is a job description, and job descriptions have properties that make them usable. They are narrow, they have a boundary, and they can be stated in a single sentence. A power is none of those things: powers are open-ended, unbounded, and stated in language that resists paraphrase, which is precisely why they are the least trustworthy column despite being the most quoted.

The working test is simple. If you cannot write the office in your own words without adding anything, you have inflated it, and you should cut the addition before charging. Inflated offices produce working that tries to cover too much, and they produce it in a way that cannot be diagnosed later, because nothing in the record will show you where the extra scope came from.

What the catalogue was assembled from

The material is a composite with visible seams. Its oldest layer comes from Jewish and Christian demonological writing, substantially traceable to a pseudepigraphic apocalyptic book that circulated widely in antiquity and was later excluded from most canonical collections. A seventeenth-century compilation of protective conjurations contributes the procedural frame, and medieval and early-modern cleansing literature contributes the ritual vocabulary.

None of these sources was a reference table. They were warning texts, protective instructions, and theological argument, and the table format is a later editorial development that arranged existing material into something searchable. That is a real convenience and it also means some of the table's apparent authority comes from formatting rather than sourcing, since a row that looks official because it sits in a neat column may be a fragment that was never meant to stand alone.

Why a repeated thought is easier to break with something borrowed

Modern practice deliberately loosened the frame. Instead of treating the entries as independent intelligences with their own wills, the influential move was to treat them as fragments of pattern, useful precisely because they are not you. A repetitive thought is hard to interrupt on purpose, and an external figure described in a few words gives the attention something else to hold, which is a different and easier task than trying to notice your own looping.

This is a real technique, and it asks no belief at all about the entity's nature. What it does require is a description specific enough to interrupt with, which brings you back to the office column. A vague name gives the attention nothing to hold and you will find yourself standing in a room with an intention and no object, which is the most common experience of a failed working.

Carroll's exit and its price

The most influential modern position is that these entities are aspects of the practitioner's own mind. It is elegant, it removes the risk entirely without requiring scepticism, and it fits the pattern-fragment method exactly. Many practitioners adopt it as a working assumption and lose nothing operationally.

The price is real and worth naming. If the entity is a facet of you, then so is your experience of contact, and the interpretive frame begins to work on you rather than the other way round. A practitioner who has taken the exit and who starts describing rich, responsive intelligences has drifted, whether or not they would put it that way. The safeguard is the record. If your log entries are becoming more detailed and more satisfying without anything else changing, that drift is happening.

Filling out a row for yourself

For a name with no catalogue entry, build your own row. Choose four fields, keep the same headings, and fill them honestly. Rank you can set to whatever placeholder you like since you are not claiming catalogue membership. Form should be visual and short. Office should be one imperative sentence with a countable completion condition. Powers you can leave empty, which is itself informative, since inventing them is the failure mode.

The exercise is worth doing because it shows how little the borrowed entries actually contain. Once you have written your own four fields you will notice that the office took ten minutes and the other three took most of an afternoon of invention. A reference is most useful when you know what it is worth, and this is what it is worth.

A working drawn from the reference

The short version. Select an entry by matching the office verb to your problem, not the name to your taste. Reduce that office to a clause, cut it to letters, and treat the result as your mark. Charge it for ninety seconds by holding it while saying the office once. Then do the actual work the office describes, because the office describes a function, and a function is something you do.

The reference's role ends once the mark exists. This is the step people skip, and skipping it produces a charged mark and no results, which gets interpreted as a charge problem. The mark is a prompt. The work is the intervention. If the mark is doing the work, then the work is the thing that was missing.

Where the reference is most likely to be wrong

The powers column, as already noted. Also anything that treats the set as a fixed total, because the count varies between editions and the variation is not evidence of anything except that the table was assembled and revised over a long period. Also any secondary source that gives precise detail without a citation, which in this material is most of the widely circulated content.

The subtler error is assuming the office descriptions are more literal than they are. They were written in a register that compresses heavily, and a phrase like a spirit described as being forbidden to appear before certain figures is not a technical constraint you can rely on. Read offices as thematic summaries and you will rarely be misled. Read them as specifications and you will build working that cannot be satisfied.

Auditing a working against the sheet

Monthly, take the active working and compare it against the four fields. Does the office still say what you are actually doing, or has it drifted into a general improvement you would like? Has the form changed at all, and if so why. Has the rank or the powers been used at any point, and if so what happened when you relied on them. Has the completion condition been met in the last month.

That last question is the audit. Everything else is housekeeping. A working whose countable condition has not been met once in a month is either badly specified or not being done, and the fix is the same in both cases: make the condition smaller and easier, and then do it. Holding an audit is more useful than holding a belief, because the belief cannot be checked and the audit can.

The row worth keeping

Keep one or two rows, not seventy. The value is in the quality of your relationship with a description you have read closely enough to state without embellishment, and that quality does not scale. Practitioners who hold a short list and rotate it tend to get more out of each entry than practitioners who work through a long list once.

This is the least satisfying conclusion in the subject, because it means most of the material goes unread. It is also the one that produces results. A catalogue is a tool for finding things, and tools do not work better when you own more of them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Classical usage splits evocation from invocation. Where does the line fall?

The traditional usage separates calling a spirit so that it appears from summoning that influence to work through the operator. Most modern writing collapses the two, and the collapse matters practically: an appearance gives you something you can observe and write down, while an influence is indistinguishable from your own behaviour unless you record it carefully in the third person.

Do I need the whole catalogue?

No. The office is the only column that reliably does work, and you need the one or two rows whose office verb matches the problem in front of you. Practitioners who hold a short list and rotate it tend to get more from each entry than practitioners who work through a long list once, because the skill being built is the reading of a description rather than the collection of names.

What if the spirit I want is not in the catalogue?

Build your own row using the same four headings. This is a normal thing to do and it is more honest than borrowing a description that does not fit. Keep the office to one imperative sentence with a countable completion condition, and leave the powers column empty, since inventing borrowed authority is the failure mode rather than the solution.

Is it safe to work with these?

The practical risks are mundane and worth taking seriously: attention spent on maintaining the working rather than on the work, gradual inflation where the practice becomes an identity rather than a procedure, and drift in the aim until it is no longer checkable. None of these require a metaphysical position to address, and all of them are handled by a written record and a monthly audit.

Do the entities have to be taken seriously?

That is the question the subject cannot settle, and the reference is more useful if you stop asking it in that form. What the catalogue gives you is a set of function descriptions, and functions are usable whether or not you think anything is doing them. What it does not give you is evidence about the nature of the entries, and pretending otherwise is what turns a working into an inflation.
