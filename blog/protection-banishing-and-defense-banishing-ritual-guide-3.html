Protection, Banishing and Defense

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 13 min read

Somebody comes to this material with a request, and the request has a premise inside it that nobody has checked. The request is to undo something. The premise is that the something exists, that it was done to you, and that undoing it is the right response. All three of those can be false at the same time, and the order of operations that most people follow assumes all three are true before any of the work begins. That is why the structural question comes first, ahead of the ritual, and it is a question with a checkable answer rather than a matter of belief. The alternative is to treat the three operations as a menu and pick the one that feels right, which reliably produces a person who has banished something that was never there and has not grounded the thing that is. The honest version of the practice starts by asking whether there is anything to annul at all, then asks whose problem the difficulty is, and only then chooses between removing, purifying, and re-establishing your own footing. That sequence is slower than the ritual and it changes the outcome more often than the ritual does.

The request, and the premise inside it

The structure of the request matters more than its content. A person arrives with a story that explains their difficulty and names a cause, and the cause is almost always external and usually another person. The material in this domain has a ready-made operation for undoing external harm, and applying it is a way of acting on the story without having examined it. The operation is not harmful. It is simply the wrong first move, because it confirms the premise in the same gesture that is supposed to be testing it.

This is the same asymmetry that appears in every tradition that takes diagnosis seriously, and the traditions that produced this material were full of practitioners who insisted on it. Divination before remedy, examination before banishing, the observation of the state before the declaration of the enemy. What has been lost in the modern transmission is not the technique but the ordering, and the ordering was carrying most of the value. Restored, it turns a ritual response into an actual process. Not restored, it turns a person into someone who has performed a decisive gesture in a situation they have not yet understood.

Whether there is anything to annul

The first question is not whether you were wronged. It is whether the specific thing described is there. Most people who arrive with a request for an undoing have not established the facts of their own situation, and the difficulty they are describing has at least four ordinary explanations that require no external agency: a change in their own perception, a change in circumstance, a coincidence that landed badly, and a pattern that only looks like a pattern because it is a single episode being examined closely.

There is a way to get closer to an answer that works without any appeal to hidden causes. Write down the events, in order, with dates, including everything that is inconvenient, not only the events that support the story. Most people discover during this exercise that the pattern has a shape they did not expect, and that shape is usually more actionable than the curse, because it points at a decision point rather than at a will. Occasionally something genuinely was done to you, and the same exercise makes that visible too. The exercise is diagnostic in both directions and it takes an evening.

The question of whose problem it is

The second question is about ownership. Even when something has been done to you, the remedy is not automatically yours to perform, and there is a version of this material in which the question never gets asked because the ritual appears to settle it. It does not. The person who made the declaration is the person who took on the obligation, and there are situations in which taking on that obligation is a worse outcome than the original difficulty.

This is most relevant when the suspected agent is someone with an ongoing relationship to you, and it is where the request is most often refused. An undoing aimed at a specific individual is a working whose efficacy and whose ethics are the same question. The material's own framework is not neutral here, and neither should your use of it be. The workable cases are narrower than people expect: your own space, your own practice, your own state of mind, and situations where the person is not going to be in your life in six months. Where none of those apply, the honest answer is that this is a conversation, not a working.

Why the most common cause is not a curse

Across a large number of requests for undoing harm, the most common proximate cause is a change in the person's own perception, and this is not a dismissal. A sudden shift in how you read a relationship, a run of coincidences that arrives at once, a loss of confidence in a situation that has not actually changed, a coincidence that resolves itself a week later. None of these are supernatural and all of them are experienced as an attack, because the interpretation of threat is doing the damage faster than the circumstances can.

This is why grounding is the operation most often skipped and the one most likely to be the answer. Grounding does not remove anything, and it does not address a person. It re-establishes your own footing in a situation where your footing has gone, and the effect of doing that is that the situation becomes legible again, sometimes within minutes. People who perform a banishing instead often find the difficulty intact two days later, having also added a sense that something was dealt with. Grounding costs an afternoon. It is the operation with the highest ratio of usefulness to dramatic satisfaction, which is exactly why it is last on the list people reach for.

Three operations, and the order to try them

The three operations in this domain do different things and contemporary writers have made the distinction because people need all three and rarely know which they have reached for. Banishing removes. Cleansing purifies. Grounding re-establishes your own footing. The first two act on a space or a situation, and the third acts on you. The common error is treating them as interchangeable intensities of the same action, which is why someone who has banished thoroughly may then find themselves still destabilised by whatever was there.

The order to try them is grounding first, then the specific operation, then cleansing if the space is involved. Starting with grounding is not a preliminary. It is the operation that makes the others interpretable, because a person whose perceptual state has not stabilised will read the result of a banishing as either total success or total failure, and both readings are produced by their own state rather than by the ritual. The sequence in the ceremonial material is usually the other way round, and it works for people whose footing is already stable, which is most of the people who write these books.

Grounding, which is the one people skip

Grounding has no drama in it. It is typically described as establishing your own position, marking a boundary for yourself, and returning attention to something you can verify. Done properly it is dull, repetitive, and difficult to do in the middle of the feeling it is meant to address, because the feeling is loud and the work is small. That difficulty is the mechanism, and it is the reason the operation works when a banishing does not.

The practical version is short enough to be done the same day. Find something that is measurably true and independent of the situation: the physical state of a room, a number in a bank account, a task with a finish line, a person who is not involved. Attend to it until the attention is genuinely on it rather than on the situation, and then note in writing what the situation looks like from there. Most people find that the situation has changed in some respect by the time they write the note, and almost none of them can say precisely what changed. The order of that sentence matters. The change comes before the explanation, and the reverse order is where the pattern gets built.

What the banishing is for, when there is nothing

The banishing in its standard form comes from nineteenth-century initiatory material, and it has been the default operation of Western ceremonial practice ever since, for a reason that is practical rather than symbolic. It is a short piece of text designed to be intelligible to any hostile intelligence, which means it declares a limit in ordinary language and then states the limit is closed. It is not a combat. It contains no attack, no adversary, and no claim about the relative power of the two parties.

Used when there is nothing to banish, it produces an odd effect that practitioners report often enough to be worth naming. The person has taken a decisive action in a situation they have not understood, and the decisiveness changes how they feel about the situation. Some of them feel finished with it, which is a real relief and is not the same as resolution. Others feel they have been told something is settled, and then discover over the next fortnight that the difficulty is exactly where it was. The rite is not harmed by this. It was designed for a different situation, and using it outside that situation is a category error rather than a failure of technique.

The declaration, and its limits

The declaration is where the structure of the request shows up on the page. A declaration that states a limit, names a place, and closes is within what this material is built for. A declaration that assigns an intention to another person is a different document, and the difference is easy to mark because the grammar changes: the first says what will not be permitted here, the second says what someone else will do. The first is a property of a situation. The second is a claim about a will, and the framework has no mechanism for it.

This is the point at which a lot of requests for undoing turn into something the practitioner should decline, and the material supports the decline more clearly than the popular transmission admits. The tradition that produced these rites was full of initiatory structures, which are full of conditions, and the conditions were not obstacles to be negotiated. They were the reason the operation was safe to perform. A declaration that ignores its own conditions has not become bolder. It has become a different operation, one with no history of careful use behind it, and the safest thing about it is that it is easy to notice.

The rising, and what it is doing

Many of the traditions that use this operation specify a physical movement alongside the words, usually a walk to the nearest door while the declaration is spoken. The movement is not incidental and it is not decorative. The spoken declaration establishes a limit in language. The walk establishes it in the body, at a cost that is measurable and immediate, and the two together are considerably harder to reinterpret afterwards.

The reason it works against a person in a frightened state is that the state is maintained by the situation, and the walk removes the body from the situation while the words are still being said. By the time the sentence ends, the person is standing in a different place. Nothing about that is mysterious and it is a considerably better answer to a frightened evening than a longer text. Practitioners who skip the movement and speak the words from where they are sitting are performing a version of the operation that has had its most reliable element removed, and they tend to report the resulting vagueness as an issue with the words.

Where a structure of undoing fails you

The structure fails in four ways, and all four are predictable enough to check for. It fails when the premise was never examined, so the working addressed a cause that was not there. It fails when the operation chosen did not match the problem, so a removal was performed on something that needed a grounding. It fails when the target was another person, so the working was doing something structurally outside its reach. And it fails when no record was kept, so the outcome was read from memory in whichever direction the evening had gone.

Each failure has a diagnostic and the diagnostics are quick. Check whether you wrote down the events before you wrote down the ritual. Check which of the three operations you chose and whether the choice followed from the problem or from the mood. Check whether the target was a person. Check whether there is a dated note. Four questions, five minutes, and they will tell you which part of the structure to rebuild. An evening spent this way beats a second working with a longer text, and it marks the difference between a practice that learns and one that accumulates.

The record that decides the question

The record is the part that makes the whole structure retroactively honest, and it is the part that everybody skips. It is short. The events in order with dates, including the inconvenient ones. The operation performed and the reason it was that operation. The state before, the state immediately after, and the state a fortnight later. Three dates and one paragraph, and the whole thing can be written on a single card in under ten minutes.

What the card does is make the outcome checkable by someone who was not there, which is the only standard worth applying to a practice like this. Most disputes about whether a piece of magic worked are disputes about memory, and memory in both directions is unreliable in exactly the situations where people most want to be right. A card with three dates and a paragraph cannot be argued with in the moment, and it can be read again in a month when the emotional charge has gone and the difference between a coincidence and a result is easier to see. It is a piece of paper and it is the entire epistemic apparatus the practice requires.

The first thing to do instead

If you came here with a request to undo something, the first thing to do is not the ritual. It is the evening of writing. Get the events down in order with dates. Name the specific thing you want reversed rather than the person who caused it. Then decide which of the three operations your situation actually calls for, and in most cases the answer will be grounding, because most situations that arrive as requests for undoing are difficulty of perception rather than difficulty of circumstance.

The ceremony is not postponed forever. It is placed after the diagnosis, where it belongs and where it works. A person who has written the events down, chosen the operation deliberately, and performed it with a walk to the door has a working that can be evaluated, and an evaluation is something almost nobody in this domain ever gets to do. That is the whole argument. Not that the rite is ineffective, and not that the difficulty is imaginary, but that the order the material was built with is the part that carries the value, and the order can be restored in an evening by anyone willing to write a paragraph before lighting a candle.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I have to check whether there is anything to annul?

Because the request contains its own premise, and the premise is that something was done to you. A person who performs a ritual in response has confirmed that premise in the same gesture meant to test it. The check is a short written account of the events in order, with dates and including the inconvenient ones, and it works in both directions: it sometimes reveals an actual harm and it more often reveals a pattern with a decision point in it, which is a more actionable thing than a curse because it points at something you can still do. The evening costs less than the rite and informs it.

What is the difference between banishing, cleansing and grounding?

Banishing removes something from a space or situation. Cleansing purifies it. Grounding re-establishes your own footing and acts on you rather than on the space. The distinction matters because most people reach for the dramatic one first and then find themselves still destabilised by whatever was actually there. Grounding usually goes first in practice, not as a preliminary but because it is the operation that makes the results of the other two interpretable, and because a person whose perceptual state has not settled will read the outcome of a banishing as total success or total failure regardless of what happened.

Can I use these rites to undo something another person did?

It is worth being blunt about this. An undoing aimed at a specific individual is a working whose efficacy and whose ethics are the same question, and the material's own framework treats claims about another person's will as outside its reach. The workable cases are narrower than people expect: your own space, your own practice, your own state of mind, and situations where the person is not going to be in your life in six months. Where none of those apply, the honest answer is that this is a conversation rather than a working, and the request should be declined rather than reinterpreted.

What does the rising in a banishing ritual actually do?

The physical walk to the nearest door while the declaration is spoken is not decorative. The words establish a limit in language, and the walk establishes it in the body at an immediate and measurable cost, which makes the two together much harder to reinterpret afterwards. The specific reason it helps a frightened person is that the walk removes the body from the situation while the words are still being said, so by the end of the sentence they are standing somewhere else. Skipping the movement and speaking from a chair removes the most reliable element of the operation.

How do I know afterwards whether any of this worked?

Keep the card. Events in order with dates, the operation performed and why, then the state before, immediately after, and a fortnight later. Three dates and one paragraph, written on the same evening. This is the whole epistemic apparatus the practice needs, and its value is that it can be read back by someone who was not there, which removes the dispute about memory that otherwise decides every question about whether the rite worked. Most people skip it, and the result is that they learn nothing from any working they have ever done.
