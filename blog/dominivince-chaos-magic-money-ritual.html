DOMINIVINCE

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Money is the first aim most people bring to a sigil, and it is almost always the wrong size. A first money working that asks for abundance tends to ask for too much, too soon, in too many directions, and then dies quietly in week three of never being looked at again. What works instead is smaller and duller: one specific number, one specific behaviour that could plausibly produce it, a ledger opened on the same day, and a glyph that is small enough to forget and specific enough to rebuild. This is the route through a first money working in the order that works, including the parts where it will not go well. Nothing here requires you to believe that a mark on paper causes a payment. It requires you to write the aim in the imperative, charge it once, then do the boring fortnight that either produces a number or does not.

A first money working, and why the first one should be small

The instinct with a money aim is to aim high, because a small number feels like an insult to the ambition. It is not. A first working exists to establish a method, not to move a balance, and a method that survives contact with a small number will survive contact with a large one. A number you can plausibly affect in a fortnight is a better teacher than a figure that would require a lucky break, because it lets you find out whether your own behaviour tracks your intention.

So the first aim should satisfy three conditions at once. It has to be specific enough that you would know on a given day whether it happened. It has to be connected to something you actually do, not to something that happens to you. And it has to be small enough that a bad week does not destroy your willingness to continue. Fail any of the three and the working stops being an experiment and becomes a wish.

The vocabulary: glyph, charge, aim, office

Four words carry most of the work, and having them straight in your head is worth more than any correspondence table. The aim is the sentence, written as a command addressed to yourself. The glyph is the compressed form of that sentence, drawn or written and then deliberately forgotten, so that the sentence has to be recovered from the mark rather than from memory. The charge is the single act of committing the glyph. The office is the one-sentence description of what the thing is for.

People routinely swap these and then wonder why nothing happens. If the office is vague, the aim cannot be written. If the aim is not a command, the glyph compresses a wish instead of an instruction. If the glyph is never forgotten, it stays a drawing. If the charge is repeated until it feels significant, it becomes a ritual of reassurance rather than a commitment. Keeping the four separate is unglamorous and it prevents most of the common failures.

Writing the aim as a command

The single most consequential decision in building a sigil is how the aim is phrased. A wish describes a state that might arrive: money arrives, clients appear, the debt clears. A command describes something you will do: send the invoice, follow up on the quote, make five calls before lunch. The first version hands the outcome to the universe. The second hands the outcome to your own calendar, where it can actually be influenced.

This matters even if you hold no particular metaphysics. Whatever the sigil is doing, it is not doing your dialling. The measurable chain runs from a written instruction to a scheduled action to a transaction, and a sigil is useful precisely because it compresses the first link and makes the second harder to forget. Write the aim so that a stranger could tell from the sentence alone what you are supposed to do this week.

Why nobody needs to believe the mechanism

The tradition this practice comes from is explicitly pragmatic about mechanism. You are not required to hold that the glyph causes money, or that it opens a channel to anything. The useful claim is much narrower: a compressed, charged statement of intent is harder to drop than an unrecorded intention, and committing to it produces a change in what you do with your attention on an ordinary Tuesday.

This is the freedom that lets the practice work for people who would find a supernatural explanation embarrassing or impossible. Take the mechanism out and nothing load-bearing is removed. What remains is a written commitment, a scheduled behaviour, and a record you can check. If you later decide the glyph has powers of its own, nothing in the method breaks, because the method never depended on that.

The twenty-minute build

Build the glyph in one sitting, with a timer, and do not allow yourself to redraw it afterwards. Set out the aim in a single sentence in the imperative mood, strip every word that is not carrying weight, take the first letters or the letter shapes, and reduce them until the mark has no recognisable words left. Twenty minutes is long enough to do that carefully and short enough that you cannot spend the evening hunting for a prettier arrangement.

The reason for the limit is that a sigil is a compression, and compression cannot be optimised indefinitely. A mark that took four hours to design carries the memory of those four hours, and you will keep improving it instead of using it. A mark that took twenty minutes carries nothing but the sentence. If the result looks crude, that is the correct result, and a crude mark that gets used beats a beautiful mark that stays in a drawer.

The charge, and the hour after it

The charge is a single deliberate act: you look at the glyph, you say or read the full aim aloud, and you close the book or the notebook. It is done once. Repeating it does not strengthen the sigil, and a person who charges every morning has built a morning devotional and mistaken it for a working.

The hour after the charge matters more than most beginners expect, because that is where the intention either becomes a plan or evaporates. Write down the specific next action before the hour is out, with a day and a time attached. Not 'work on income', but 'send the two outstanding invoices by Thursday at four'. If you cannot write it, the aim was not yet specific enough, and you should go back and rebuild rather than charge a vague mark.

The first fortnight, kept boring

The fortnight after the charge is where money workings usually die, and the cause is almost always a change of target. You set the aim around one thing, get a partial result, get excited, and enlarge the aim to cover everything. Within a week you are working on three fronts, none of which is the one you wrote down, and the sigil no longer corresponds to any behaviour you are performing.

Keep the aim fixed and the week dull. Same sentence, same number, same scheduled action, no additions, no new sigils, no enlarging. The temptation to add a second aim is strong precisely because the first one is working slowly, and adding feels like effort. Two aims in the first fortnight halves the attention available to each and gives you a result you cannot interpret, because you have no idea which mark was responsible for which change.

The ledger, opened on day one

Open the ledger on the same day you charge, before anything has happened, and enter the starting figure. This is the least interesting step in the whole process and the one that decides whether you will learn anything. A figure recorded after two weeks of activity has already been shaped by everything that happened in those two weeks, including the working, so it cannot serve as a baseline.

Keep three columns and no more: the number, the action you took, and a one-line note on the circumstances. The third column is the one that gets skipped and the one that matters most, because it is what lets you distinguish a week where you did the work from a week where the work was done for you by an unrelated event. A ledger with two columns records your optimism. A ledger with three records your memory.

Where beginners lose the thread

The three common losses are all foreseeable. The first is the second sigil, added in week three because the first one has not produced anything yet. The second is the reading of other people's results, at which point your own ledger quietly stops being the reference and other people's claims become the standard you are failing to meet. The third is the change of vocabulary, where the aim is reworded in nicer language and the new sentence no longer matches the behaviour you had built.

The defence in all three cases is the same: do not change the written material during the first month. If the aim turns out to be wrong, finish the month and then write a new one. A wrong aim that runs to completion produces information; an aim that is revised weekly produces a series of experiments with no accumulated result, and the sigil becomes a way of feeling busy about money rather than a way of doing something about it.

A number you can point at

At the end of the fortnight there should be a figure you can point at, even if the figure is zero. Zero is a legitimate and unusually informative result, because it separates the two hypotheses you started with. If the number moved, you have evidence that the chain from aim to action to transaction is live for you, and the next aim can be larger. If it did not move, you have learned something more valuable: that the scheduled action was not the thing that produces this particular number.

Write the outcome in a single sentence and resist interpretation. Not 'the sigil is not working' and not 'the sigil is working but slowly', but 'I sent the invoices on Thursday and the number changed by this much'. The sentence is dull on purpose. Dull sentences survive contact with a later, more disappointed version of yourself, and they make the second month's aim something you can build on rather than something you have to defend.

The ethics, briefly, without excuses

Two boundaries hold regardless of how the mechanism is understood. The first is that the aim must not require another person's participation without their knowledge, which rules out anything that depends on a specific person's feelings, attention, or decision. The second is that nothing in the practice may displace a financial obligation you already hold, since a sigil aimed at income competes directly with the income you have.

Both boundaries exist because the technique is unusually good at converting vague wanting into scheduled action, and that power does not care what the action is. A practitioner who aims at a specific person is using a reliable commitment device in the service of something the other person did not agree to, and the reliability of the technique is exactly what makes that a problem rather than a harmless eccentricity. Aim at your own behaviour and the ethical question largely dissolves.

What to write before the second one

Before building a second money sigil, write four lines. What the aim was, in the exact sentence you charged. What you did, in the specific actions you took. What the number did. And what you would change, naming the single element you would alter rather than the whole approach. That fourth line is the one that carries the method forward, and it is the one people skip because they would rather start the next aim than finish the first account.

Then make the second one bigger by exactly one increment. The first working was small on purpose, and the point of writing the four lines is that you now know which part to enlarge. Enlarging the amount, or the frequency of the action, or the number of people you contact, is fine. Enlarging all three at once gives you a month you cannot interpret, which is the same failure as never having run the first month at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should a first money sigil actually aim for?

Aim for a number you could plausibly influence within a fortnight through your own scheduled actions, not through a fortunate break. The test is whether you could name the specific things you would do differently this week to move it. If the answer is vague, the aim is too large. A small first aim is not a compromise; it is the only size that produces usable information about whether the method works for you.

Do I need to believe that the sigil causes money to appear?

No, and the practice was designed so that you do not. Everything load-bearing survives removing the supernatural claim: a compressed written instruction, a single charge, a scheduled behaviour, and a ledger you can check. If you later find the metaphysics convincing, nothing in the method has to be redone. If you never do, the method still runs, because it was never doing the work you were attributing to it.

What if the first month produces no change at all?

Record it as a result rather than a failure and let it stand. Zero tells you that the chain from aim to action to outcome is broken somewhere, which is more useful than a small positive you cannot distinguish from an unrelated coincidence. The usual culprit is an aim whose scheduled action is not the thing that produces the number, so the second working usually changes the action rather than the glyph.

How often should I charge the sigil?

Once. The charge is a commitment event, not a maintenance task, and repeating it converts the sigil into a devotional that produces reassurance instead of behaviour. If you find yourself charging it every morning because the day feels wrong, that is a sign the aim has stopped pointing at any action you take, and the fix is to rewrite the aim rather than to charge more often.

Can I build more than one money sigil?

Not in the first month. Two aims in the opening fortnight split the attention that made the first one work and leave you unable to say which mark produced which change. Finish the first month, write your four lines, and only then start a second. If you add one early, keep the original ledger running and treat the new mark as unmeasured until the first has had its full month.
