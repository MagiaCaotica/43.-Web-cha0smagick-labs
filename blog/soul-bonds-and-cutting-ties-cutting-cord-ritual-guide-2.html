Soul Bonds and Cutting Ties

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

The word bond does a lot of work in this material and most of that work is illegitimate. People describe relationships they no longer want as soul bonds, as karmic contracts, as cords that someone else tied. Some of those descriptions are doing real work on a real situation. Most are describing a habit with a supernatural wrapper, and the wrapper makes the habit harder to address, because a bond cannot be ended by a decision while a habit can. What follows separates the two, sets out what the classical sources actually describe, and then treats the cord rite as what it has always been usable as: a concrete object, a physical act, and a date on which the arrangement changes.

Most of what gets called a bond is a loop

The diagnostic question is simple and it cuts through the mystique: what happens if you stop doing the thing? If the answer is that a familiar distress appears, arrives quickly, and then subsides, you are looking at a habit with a supernatural label on it. The label does not change the mechanism. Withdrawal produces discomfort in both cases, and the discomfort is what people are describing when they say they cannot let go.

The distinction matters practically because a loop can be interrupted and a bond supposedly cannot. Behaviour loops yield to changes in cue, routine and consequence, which is unglamorous and reliable. Someone who believes their attachment is a karmic contract has been told the loop is not available to them, so they reach for a ritual instead of a decision, and the ritual cannot make the decision for them.

What the older sources describe

Cord cutting appears in genuine initiatory material, most usefully in the eighteenth-century kabbalistic Bahir ha-Levavot attributed to Eleazar ben Pitcharubut, and it carries through into later ceremonial writing. In those texts it is a rite of passage: a cord is tied between two people to represent an existing attachment, and the cutting marks the end of a stage. It is performed with something in hand, in front of witnesses or at least in front of yourself, and it is not reversible in the ordinary sense.

That is a genuinely initiatory shape, and it is worth distinguishing from a modern séance with a knife and an invocation. What the older material has is a concrete object performing a real function, an audience that makes it hard to pretend, and a direction of travel. Modern adaptations keep the object and often lose the audience, which is one reason the practice can feel hollow when done alone in a quiet room.

Concrete action on an abstract thing

The mechanism that makes cord rites more than symbolism is straightforward. An abstract attachment has no surface. A cord has one. You can hold it, measure it, choose where to put the blade, and feel the moment it parts, and a physical fact is easier to treat as settled than a decision you are still revising.

This is the same principle behind marking a contract, filing a notice, or moving out. The paperwork is not the substance of the ending, but it converts an ongoing negotiation into a completed event. Where a cord rite is weak is when it substitutes for the practical steps that also need to happen, because cutting a string does not change who you answer messages from.

Cutting, burning, and the difference

Two methods dominate. Cutting severs cleanly and is the ordinary form. Burning is used when the intention is destruction rather than separation, and the older sources treat it as more final and less reversible. Either is defensible; what matters is that you pick one deliberately, because a rite that mixes the two by indecision tends to feel incomplete afterward, and the feeling of incompleteness is what gets interpreted as a failed severance.

Whichever you choose, do it once, deliberately, at the point you intended. Repeated cutting of the same cord is the rite most associated with reinforcing what it was meant to end, which is the mechanism behind the warning you will find in most of this literature.

Why the do-it-twice warning exists

Many traditions in this area hold that a bond severed by force re-forms, and this is where the well-known instruction to do it properly or not at all comes from. Read as a claim about the invisible, it is untestable. Read as a description of human behaviour, it is entirely predictable and it holds well.

A severance that involves rage, or that happens in the middle of a fight, or that is performed in order to win an argument, is a statement about the relationship rather than an ending to it. The person on the other end receives the event as a message and responds to the message. What re-forms is not the bond; it is the pattern. Do the rite in a calm state, after you have already decided, and there is nothing in the act that invites a response.

Deciding before you cut

The rite belongs after the decision, not instead of it. Decide what you want, in advance, in writing: what specifically ends, what remains, and what you are doing about the practical arrangements. Then perform the ceremony as the record of a settled thing rather than as the way of settling it.

This ordering is what makes the practice safe to repeat and safe to stop. If the cutting is your decision, a year later you can tell the difference between a rite you performed and an attachment you are still actively maintaining. If the cutting was your decision procedure, you will find yourself returning to it whenever the attachment resurfaces, and each return is an act of renewal.

Encoding the severance as sigil work

The sigil adaptation keeps the object logic and drops the room. You write the severance as a single imperative statement, reduce it to a glyph, and the glyph becomes the cord. There is no physical cutting, and what replaces the blade is a single deliberate act of discarding or destroying the paper.

This is a legitimate adaptation and it has one real advantage over the traditional form: it can be done in private, without an audience, and it leaves no object behind to become a source of renewed attachment. Its disadvantage is equally real. Without an object and a witness, the act is less consequential as an event, so the surrounding conditions matter more. Set a date, close the notebook afterwards, and write down what you decided.

A sequence that runs once

Take something you can actually cut: cord, ribbon, or a strip of paper. Write both names on it, or your name and the arrangement you are ending. Say once, out loud, what is ending and what remains. Make the cut at the point you decided in advance. Then do the ordinary practical things, which are the part that determines whether anything changed.

Afterwards, do not check for signs. Signs will be sought and found, because the mind supplies them. Instead, check the observable facts on a fixed date: have you stopped initiating contact, have the practical arrangements changed, is there a specific behaviour that used to happen and now does not. Those three checks will tell you more than any amount of symbolic evidence, because they are the ones that cannot be argued with.

If the bond is not yours to end

Some of the most harmful framing in this material involves people believing their attachment is a contract someone else drew. Nobody else signed anything, and the idea is useful only to whoever benefits from your continued attention. Recognising the framing for what it is often produces immediate relief, because it returns the situation to ordinary human terms: one person wants contact, the other does not.

Where there is real risk of harm, no rite substitutes for distance, documentation, and, where relevant, formal process. The rite is for the part that is yours to decide, and it cannot protect you from someone else's behaviour. Treating a symbol as a safeguard against a specific person is how people end up waiting for a cut to do work that only distance can do.

What the rite does and does not settle

Honest accounting: a cord rite does not change another person's mind, does not remove a legal or financial entanglement, and does not repair a situation where the practical arrangements still permit the old pattern to resume. What it does is mark a decision that has already been made, at a time you chose, in a form you can point to afterwards.

That is a modest benefit and it is a real one. Most endings drag because nobody can point to the moment. A rite gives the moment a shape. If you are looking for something that releases you from consequences you do not want, this is not it, and it is worth knowing that before you begin rather than afterwards.

Closing the practice

A severance rite is not something to keep available. Once it is done and the practical steps are taken, the practice should end, and ending it deliberately is part of doing it well. Keep the note you wrote, or discard it, but do not leave the materials where they invite a second attempt.

The reason is that repeated endings require repeated reasons. If you are still cutting cords at regular intervals, something is being re-established each time, and the rite is functioning as a renewal mechanism rather than a closure. One rite, one decision, one set of practical changes, and then a different relationship to the whole question.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a soul bond in practice?

Usually it is a behavioural loop described in supernatural language. The useful test is what happens when you stop: if familiar distress appears and then fades, that is withdrawal, and withdrawal responds to changes in routine rather than to ritual.

Is cord cutting genuinely initiatory?

It appears in real initiatory material, notably the eighteenth-century Bahir ha-Levavot attributed to Eleazar ben Pitcharubut. What the older versions have that modern adaptations often drop is the concrete object and the witness, and those two features are doing most of the practical work.

Should I cut the cord or burn it?

Cutting is the ordinary form of separation; burning is used where destruction rather than severance is intended. The important part is choosing deliberately and doing it once. Repeated cutting tends to read as renewal rather than ending.

Why does the literature say do it right or do it twice?

Read behaviourally it is a warning against performing the rite in rage or mid-argument. A severance staged as a message gets responded to as a message, and what returns is the pattern rather than the bond. Perform it calm, after you have already decided.

Can a sigil replace the physical cord?

Yes, if the severance statement is reduced to a glyph and the glyph is destroyed in one deliberate act. The advantage is privacy and leaving no object behind. The disadvantage is that without an object and a witness the act carries less weight as an event, so the framing around it matters more.
