The Mind Science of Practice

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

The interesting question about this material is not whether intention changes outcomes. That question has been answered enough times to be boring. The interesting question is which specific claims survive a hostile reading, and what is left once the ones that do not are removed. This article takes the strongest version of the claim seriously, then applies the objections that have actually stuck, and then reports what is left. The result is smaller than most popular material and considerably more useful. You end up with a set of practices that work regardless of which explanation turns out to be correct, and a clear picture of the parts you should stop claiming. The people this helps most are not the committed sceptics, who never needed persuading, and not the convinced, who are not asking. It is the working practitioners whose practice has quietly become something they cannot honestly describe, usually because the claims grew faster than the record did. Everything below is offered in that spirit: less encouragement than the popular material, and considerably more that survives contact with a hostile question.

The claim in its strongest form

The strongest version is not that magic changes reality. It is narrower and much harder to dismiss: structured symbolic action reliably alters behaviour, attention and physiological state, and a practitioner who performs such action regularly and records the outcome gains a measurable advantage over one who does not. Every part of that sentence survives scrutiny, and none of it requires a supernatural mechanism.

The popular version is a different claim, much larger, and almost never the one supported by evidence. It says that the symbolic action produces an external effect unattached to human behaviour. When a practitioner defends a result by pointing to an external change, they have moved from the defensible claim to the undefendable one without noticing.

Keeping those two apart is most of the work. You can hold the narrow claim firmly and still do everything the broad claim asks you to do, and you lose nothing by dropping the metaphysics. What you lose is the ability to claim credit you did not earn, which was never really an advantage.

Two objections that hold up under pressure

The first objection is about selection. Human beings remember confirmations and lose the rest, and any practice that generates many more opportunities to notice a hit than a miss will look effective regardless of mechanism. This is not a subtle bias and it is not under your conscious control, which is why sincerity is not sufficient protection. The only reliable protection is a written record made before you know the outcome.

The second objection is about the comparison group. People who undertake any regular, structured practice aimed at a goal tend to improve on that goal, and they improve partly through the structure: the schedule, the reduced friction of doubt, and the salience of the goal at the moment a decision becomes available. Attributing that improvement to the symbol rather than the structure is the most common error in the field, and it is made by enthusiasts rather than sceptics.

Neither objection is answered by saying the practice is symbolic. Both are answered by a record, and a record is available to anyone willing to keep one.

Why one good week proves almost nothing

A single striking result is the most common reason people conclude a method works, and it is close to worthless as evidence. Any week contains many things that could be credited to a working: a decision that happened to go your way, a coincidence you noticed, an opportunity created by someone else entirely. With enough candidate explanations and only one observed outcome, the arithmetic never discriminates between them.

The number that helps is the base rate. If an outcome of the kind you are looking for happens reasonably often without any practice at all, then observing it once tells you close to nothing, and a hundred practitioners observing it once each tells you close to nothing in aggregate. This is unglamorous and it is the entire reason success rates matter.

A cheap self-test: before starting, write down how often the outcome you are seeking already happens to you. Most people discover the number is far higher than their intuition, and that discovery does more for honest practice than any argument about mechanism.

The attribution problem, applied to your own log

Even a well-kept log will mislead you, because the record captures what happened and not what caused it. A working that precedes a good result by a day looks identical in the record to a working that precedes an unrelated good result by a day, and there is no way to tell them apart without varying the practice.

The fix is the least popular instruction in this field: run a working you expect nothing from, with the same care, and keep it in the same log. Aimed-at and control workings written the same way give you the only comparison that means anything. Practitioners who do this reliably produce far more boring results and far more trustworthy ones.

A second fix is to record the days with no working at all, and to record what happened on those. The empty days are where the structure is doing its work unattended, and leaving them out of the record gives you a distorted picture of your own practice.

  1. Aimed-at workings, with the same care and the same logging format.
  2. Control workings aimed at nothing, recorded identically and never edited.
  3. Blank days logged explicitly, including what happened on them.
  4. Every result entered, including the ones that spoiled the sequence.

What a serious trial of this would require

If somebody wanted to establish whether the practice produces anything beyond behavioural commitment, the design is not difficult to state. You would need pre-registered outcomes, a fixed number of attempts, blind scoring where possible, a control group doing an equally committed non-symbolic activity, and an analysis that treats the nulls as data rather than as noise.

You would also need a large sample, because the effect sizes anyone would be looking for are small, and small effects with large noise are the classic way to manufacture a finding that does not replicate. Nobody has funded this, and the reason is obvious: the interesting question is not worth much to anyone who is not already invested in it.

The absence of a funded trial is not evidence for or against. It is a fact about attention and money. What it does mean is that the practitioner's own log is the only study most of them will ever run, which is another argument for making that log good rather than making the claims bigger.

Confounds that care does not remove

Some confounds are handled by care and some are not. Attention is handled: you can log how long you focused and whether the practice was completed. Effort is handled: you can log whether a day was hard. Expectation is partly handled: you can ask, at the end, whether you expected the outcome, and write the answer down before comparing it to your prediction.

What care does not remove is time. Your mood, your circumstances, your health and the state of your relationships all move over months, and any ninety-day window is sensitive to where those happen to sit. This is why short runs feel so convincing and so rarely survive contact with a second run.

It is also why the most honest practitioners tend toward long, dull records rather than dramatic short ones. A year of unremarkable data with occasional misses is a more useful instrument than a fortnight of vivid impressions, even though it feels far less interesting while you are living it.

The honest floor, and how to state it

There is a version of the claim that is unarguable: performing a structured symbolic action with a stated intent, followed by a written record, makes you more likely to act on that intent. Nothing about that sentence is mystical, and nothing in it requires you to hold any particular view about what happened. It is also, in most circumstances, enough to justify the practice.

The version that is arguable is that the practice produces an external effect. The evidence for that is thin, the theory is untested, and the burden sits with the person making the claim. Practitioners who state the narrow claim confidently and the broad claim tentatively are doing something rational, and almost nobody does it.

A workable rule: never attach a claim to a result unless the claim would still be true if your own behaviour were the only cause. That single test removes most of the overreach without requiring you to give up anything you actually rely on.

A practice that pays out either way

The useful consequence of the narrow claim is that a well-run practice is worth doing regardless of how the metaphysical question resolves. Keeping a record forces you to state targets precisely. Reducing a target to a single sentence exposes vague desires that you would otherwise carry indefinitely. Scheduling the work puts it in the same category as exercise, which is where it belongs.

The charge itself, whatever you think is happening during it, functions as a device for sustained attention on one question. That is a well-studied and reproducible psychological effect, and it is the component most likely to be doing the work in any case. Using it deliberately is not a compromise; it is the honest reading of the evidence.

The failure mode to watch for is inflation. When a practice starts being described with more certainty than it has earned, the people most damaged are usually the most committed, because they are the ones who have staked identity on the claim. Keeping the language flat is a form of self-protection.

How to describe your results to someone sceptical

Describe the behaviour, the schedule, the number of attempts and the number of results, and let the listener draw the conclusion. If you find yourself explaining why a particular result was not a coincidence, you are already arguing rather than reporting, and the person across from you has learned to discount everything after that point.

Reporting the nulls is the fastest way to be believed about the rest. A record with two hits and nineteen misses in twelve attempts is more credible than one with four hits and no mention of the other twenty, and it is also a better description of what is probably happening.

It helps to state up front what the practice is for, in terms of attention and commitment rather than mechanism. That framing invites the useful conversation about design and discourages the unproductive one about belief, which is a trade almost everyone in the field would take.

What to do with a null result

Log it, leave it in, and do not go looking for a reason to explain it away on the day it happens. A null is a data point with exactly the same status as a hit, and the temptation to interpret it immediately is the same bias that made you notice the hits in the first place.

After a run, the useful questions are about design rather than outcome. Was the target specific enough to act on? Did you perform the working or only intend to? How many attempts did you actually complete? Those questions have answers, and they change what you do next. Whether the spirit was pleased is not one of them.

A null is also the most useful thing that can happen to someone who has been over-claiming. It breaks the pattern early, when breaking it is cheap, rather than after years of building a structure on a result that was never there.

Where the remaining disagreement sits

After all the objections, there is still a real question and it is not the one most people argue about. It is whether symbolic action does anything at all when the behavioural explanation has been fully accounted for. The honest position is that nobody has demonstrated it, nobody has shown it is impossible, and the absence of a demonstration is uninformative when nobody has funded the study.

The productive move is to stop treating this as a dispute about truth and start treating it as a choice about how to allocate attention. You can spend your time arguing for a mechanism, or you can spend it improving a practice that already produces results under the narrow reading. The second is not a consolation prize.

Practitioners who make that choice tend to be calmer, keep better records, and last longer. None of that is a proof of anything, and it is also the most repeated observation in the field.

Frequently Asked Questions

If the mechanism is uncertain, why keep the practice at all?

Because the narrow claim is not uncertain. Doing something structured with a stated intent, and recording the outcome, makes you more likely to act on that intent. That effect does not depend on settling any metaphysical question, and it is the reason most people get anything out of this at all.

Does any of this settle whether practitioners get real results?

It settles what the results consist of, which is the part that was fuzzy. A result in this field is almost always a change in behaviour, attention, or circumstance, and it is usually a change you can describe in one sentence. If your result cannot be described that way, it is probably an interpretation rather than an observation.

How many attempts do I need before the record means anything?

Far more than feels reasonable, which is the problem. The number depends entirely on how often the outcome you are seeking already happens without any practice, and most people find that base rate surprisingly high. Measure it first, then set the number, and expect it to be larger than you hoped.

Where does the money actually go in a paid version of this material?

The claims are free and this whole page is free. What a paid product can supply is structure: a maintained log with automatic date stamping, control-working prompts, and export in a form you can analyse. The analysis is what separates a record from a diary, and it is the part nobody wants to do by hand.

Is there a version of this that is safe to practise alone?

Yes, and this is one of them. The risks in this field come from intensity, isolation and sleep disruption rather than from the claims themselves. A short daily practice, a written record, and at least one other person who knows what you are doing will address nearly all of it.
