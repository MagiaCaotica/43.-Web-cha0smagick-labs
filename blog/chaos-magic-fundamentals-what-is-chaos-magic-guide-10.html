Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

Most explanations of chaos magic describe what the method is. Very few describe what happens when someone actually uses it for two months, which is the part that decides whether you keep going. This is a report from that side of the fence: the workings that produced something, the workings that produced nothing, and the two or three habits that turned out to matter more than any technique involved. The chapters that follow cover the sequence I followed, the records I kept, the tools I tried, and the stretches where I stopped writing anything down at all. That last part turned out to be the most instructive. Nothing here requires you to believe anything in particular, and the conclusions I draw are deliberately narrower than the ones usually advertised.

The rules I set at the start

Four rules, written before the first charge. One working per week, no more, because weekly turned out to be about right. Every entry written the same day, in a paragraph, including the runs where I wanted to write nothing. Every entry to state the exact sentence used, so I could tell afterwards whether a miss was a bad outcome or a bad target. And every review date fixed in advance, with a stated rule for what counted.

The reason for the second rule is that the honest version of this account is less flattering than the popular one. The temptation is to write up the runs that worked, and the only reliable defence is a rule set before the first disappointment, when setting rules is easy.

What I expected and what actually turned up

I expected a handful of clear hits and some misses. What arrived was three results I count as real, a larger number of outcomes I cannot classify either way, and a substantial quantity of noise from ordinary life that I had attributed to the workings. The three that survived scrutiny were all cases where the target was a specific action with a date attached, and all three involved a decision I had been putting off.

The unclassifiable group is the interesting one. Most of it was work landing through ordinary effort in the same period, which is what usually happens and which is very hard to credit correctly. The noise is where the honest entries earn their place, because a record containing only successes teaches you nothing about your own reliability.

Where the misses clustered, and why

The misses shared a feature. Every one of them had an intent that was either too broad to fail or too tightly tied to somebody else's behaviour. Requests about my own state produced outcomes I could not classify. Requests about a specific person's response produced nothing, which is at least a clean result.

This is the same finding that shows up in every field log I have read, and it is worth stating plainly: the workings that produced checkable results were the workings whose targets were things I could actually do. Where the target was outside my control, the record shows nothing at all, and a record that shows nothing is a record I should have written differently.

The six-week gap, and what it did to the log

Six weeks in the middle of the year when nothing was logged. Work, illness, and the discovery that I did not actually want the practice badly enough to protect it against a busy month. The gap is the most useful part of the year for what it revealed, which is that the workings were not load-bearing in any sense, and that I had told myself they were part of a routine rather than a separate activity that could be dropped.

Coming back to it was harder than starting, which is the practical argument for keeping a habit small enough to survive a bad fortnight. The practice as I ran it in the first two months was about fifteen minutes a week. The version I had imagined was longer than that.

The habit doing the work rather than the glyph

Looking at the log, the effects cluster around the weeks where I did the unglamorous part, and not around the weeks where the ritual was more elaborate. Writing the sentence in the imperative, then acting on the specific thing named in it, is the part that correlated with results. The drawing, the charge, the timing, and the astrological consultation were all present in every week including the ones that produced nothing at all.

I do not draw a metaphysical conclusion from that. What I draw is a practical one. The components of the method differ enormously in how much they contribute, and the component that contributes most is the least interesting to perform.

What the run taught me about specification

The three successful outcomes all had a number and a date in the sentence. The ambiguous ones had neither. By the middle of the year I was writing sentences that could fail, which is a smaller change than it sounds, because a sentence that can fail has to name a thing rather than a condition.

This is the same lesson that shows up in every discipline that uses forecasting, and it arrives here with a slight twist. The point is not that a specific intention is more likely to work. The point is that a specific intention is the only one you will ever learn anything from.

  • Sentences with a number or a date produced the only outcomes I could classify
  • Ambiguous intents produced results I could not judge in either direction
  • Targets outside my own control produced a flat record across the whole year
  • The component that correlated with results was the least ceremonial part

The compulsion boundary, and how close it came

Twice during the year the practice drifted toward checking, which is the failure mode the abundance material warns about at length. Both times it followed a period of low results, which is exactly when the checking habit forms. What broke the drift in each case was deciding in advance that the review date was final, rather than reopening it on a day when a result had not arrived.

I had expected the risk to come from wanting the outcome. It comes instead from the discomfort of not knowing, and the two are not the same. Wanting an outcome is fine and is present in every working. Not being able to tolerate an open question is what turns a practice into a compulsion.

What the tools were actually for

I used a sigil generator for the reduction, which saved perhaps four minutes a week and introduced a screen into a private moment. That trade was roughly neutral, and I kept using it because four minutes a week is four minutes a week. The intention tracker was the tool that genuinely earned its place, because it was the only thing that made the review date impossible to forget.

A grimoire-style reference had no measurable value over the year, but the paper record did. The digital notes and the paper log disagreed at one point, and the paper one was the correct one, which is a small argument for keeping the primary record in a form that nobody can accidentally edit.

What I would tell somebody starting now

Start with the sentence and skip the rest. Write something with a number in it, act on it, and write down what happened. Do that for a month before you buy anything, before you learn a reduction method properly, and before you look at a planetary table. If you are still doing it after a month, and the log has at least one entry you cannot explain away, then the apparatus is worth building.

The reverse advice matters too. If you build the apparatus first you will enjoy the apparatus, and the log is the part that asks something of you. Almost everything in this field is more pleasant than the sentence-writing it is supposed to support.

What the year does not show

It does not show that glyphs cause anything, and the record gives no basis for that claim. It does not show that the practice is worthless, either, because a year of structured record keeping on a single question cannot answer a question that large. It shows what one person's results looked like with a decent process and an honest log, which is a smaller and more useful claim.

I also have no idea whether the three results would have arrived anyway. That is the limitation of every self-record, including the ones that conclude that the method works. The number of runs, the absence of a control, and the fact that the observer is also the participant are all reasons to hold the finding loosely.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happened in the six weeks I did not write anything up?

Nothing was logged, and that gap turned out to be the most informative part of the year. It showed that the workings were not load-bearing, that I had overstated how much the practice mattered to me, and that the version I was actually running took about fifteen minutes a week rather than the longer ritual I had been imagining. Coming back was harder than starting.

Can someone practise this without holding any belief at all?

Yes, and the results in this log are easier to read for a sceptic than for a believer, because a sceptic has no stake in reinterpreting a miss. The steps are the same either way: a sentence, an action, an entry, a review. What a sceptic must supply is not belief but a willingness to leave the entries alone long enough to be checked by somebody else.

How much of this depends on what I actually think is true?

Less than the enthusiast version and more than the dismissive one, which is the least useful answer and probably the accurate one. The belief enters at the point where you decide whether a run that went badly was bad luck or a bad target. A practitioner with strong expectations will read more into the same log than a sceptic will, and both will find support for their position in it.

Would a paid tracker make this practice better or just busier?

Better, and only in one narrow way. A tracker that fixes the review date and makes the pre-registered decision rule impossible to skip earns its money, because the review is the part people abandon and the part that makes the rest meaningful. A tracker that sends you notifications is a different product, and it works directly against the boundary this practice needs.

Is one working a week enough to see anything?

Enough to see your own patterns, which is a different thing from seeing whether a working causes an outcome. Fifty-two comparable entries will tell you that you write vague sentences when you are busy and specific ones when you are calm, and that the results cluster where your conduct changed. That is genuinely useful. It will not settle the mechanism question either way.
