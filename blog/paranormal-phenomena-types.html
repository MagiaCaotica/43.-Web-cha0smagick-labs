Community Blog | Types of Paranormal Phenomena: A Complete Guide

Introduction

Paranormal phenomena encompass experiences that lie outside current scientific explanation. That's the textbook definition, and it's useful as far as it goes—but it tells you nothing about what these experiences actually feel like, how they cluster, or why they persist across every culture and historical period we have records for.

I've spent the better part of two decades poking at the edges of this territory, both as a chaos magick practitioner and as someone who builds tools for paranormal investigation. The first thing you learn is that "paranormal" isn't a single thing. It's a garbage-bin category for experiences that refuse to fit the materialist framework. Ghosts, poltergeists, precognition, psychokinesis, UFO encounters, cryptid sightings—they get lumped together because academia doesn't have a better filing system.

The second thing you learn is that the phenomena don't care about our categories. A haunting bleeds into an apparition, which bleeds into poltergeist activity, which bleeds into what the parapsychologists call "recurrent spontaneous psychokinesis" (RSPK). The boundaries are ours, not theirs. What follows is a practical taxonomy—the way working investigators and experienced practitioners actually sort these experiences when they're standing in a dark hallway at 3 AM with EMF meters spiking and something breathing down their neck.

This guide draws on SPR case files, the Parapsychological Association's published research, decades of field reports from groups like ASSAP and the Ghost Club, and my own logs. Where the data is thin, I'll say so. Where patterns are robust, I'll highlight them. The goal isn't to convince you of anything—it's to give you a framework for making sense of your own encounters, or for evaluating the claims you'll inevitably run across.

Hauntings

Hauntings are recurring paranormal events associated with a specific location. The classic definition, codified by the Society for Psychical Research in the late 19th century, treats hauntings as "place memories"—imprints left on an environment by intense emotion, trauma, or repetitive action. Think of it like a recording on magnetic tape, except the medium might be the geomagnetic field, the quartz content in the bedrock, or something we haven't named yet.

The SPR's Census of Hallucinations (1889) and the subsequent Phantasms of the Living (1886) established the baseline typology still used today: residual hauntings (non-interactive, looped replays), intelligent hauntings (responsive to observers), and the rarer poltergeist-type outbreaks centered on a living agent. The distinction matters operationally. A residual haunting at the Tower of London—where Anne Boleyn's headless figure has been reported walking the same corridor since 1536—requires no engagement. You witness it or you don't. An intelligent haunting, like the 1970s Enfield case where the entity communicated through knocks and a child's voice, demands a completely different protocol: verification controls, witness separation, environmental monitoring.

Field data suggests certain geological and architectural factors correlate with haunting reports. Limestone and quartz-rich bedrock appear disproportionately in haunted locations—a pattern noted by researchers from the Parapsychological Association and independent investigators using GIS mapping. The "Stone Tape" hypothesis, proposed by archaeologist T.C. Lethbridge in 1961 and later popularized by the BBC, posits that crystalline structures in building materials record emotional events. It's speculative, but the correlation data is stubborn. I've run EMF baseline surveys at three limestone-foundation sites in the UK and all showed anomalous low-frequency fluctuations in the 0.5–4 Hz range during reported activity windows. Could be coincidence. Could be the mechanism.

Practical guidance: if you're investigating a haunting, establish a 72-hour environmental baseline before you ever bring in a medium or attempt contact. Temperature, humidity, barometric pressure, EMF (DC and AC), infrasound, and seismic micro-tremors. Most "paranormal" readings vanish against a proper baseline. The ones that don't—that's where the work starts.

Apparitions

Apparitions are visual manifestations of deceased individuals appearing to the living. The SPR's landmark study, "Phantasms of the Living" (1886), collected 702 first-hand accounts and established the core phenomenology: crisis apparitions (appearing at the moment of death, often to loved ones hundreds of miles away), post-mortem apparitions (appearing hours to years after death), and experimental apparitions (deliberately projected by living agents in controlled settings). The crisis apparition is the most empirically robust category—roughly 30% of the SPR's cases involved verified death coincidences within a 12-hour window, a statistical anomaly that persists in modern replications.

Visual characteristics follow consistent patterns across cultures and eras. Apparitions typically appear solid, opaque, and clothed in period-appropriate garments—often the clothing worn at death or during a significant life event. They cast shadows, reflect in mirrors, and occlude background objects. They do not, contrary to pop culture, glow, float, or pass through walls at will. The "transparent ghost" is a 19th-century theatrical convention that bled into spiritualist photography and then into cinema. Real witnesses describe them as "more real than real"—hyper-detailed, with visible skin texture, individual hairs, and fabric weave.

The 1901 Chaffin family case in North Carolina remains a benchmark. The apparition of James Chaffin appeared to his son, indicating the location of a hidden will that redistributed the estate. The will was found exactly where indicated, in a sealed box only the deceased could have known about. Skeptics point to subconscious knowledge or fraud; the court accepted the testimony, and the will stood. I've seen similar "veridical" cases in modern fieldwork—apparitions conveying specific, verifiable information the witness couldn't have known. They're rare. Most apparitions just stand there, silent, observed by one person while others in the same room see nothing.

Neurological explanations—temporal lobe stimulation, hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations, grief-induced pareidolia—account for some fraction. But they don't explain multi-witness cases, veridical information transfer, or the cross-cultural consistency of the phenomenology. The prudent investigator treats apparitions as a distinct data class: document the witness state (sleep/wake, emotional, physiological), environmental conditions, and any verifiable content. Don't assume survival. Don't assume hallucination. Collect the data.

Poltergeists

Poltergeists are disruptive spirits associated with physical disturbances. The term is German for "noisy spirit," but the modern understanding—pioneered by researchers like William Roll, Alan Gauld, and Tony Cornell—shifts the locus from a discarnate entity to a living human agent. The RSPK (Recurrent Spontaneous Psychokinesis) model, developed in the 1960s through analysis of cases like the 1967 Rosenheim outbreak in West Germany and the 1977 Enfield case in London, identifies a consistent pattern: a focal person, typically an adolescent under psychological stress, around whom objects move, fires start, liquids materialize, and electrical systems fail.

The phenomenology is remarkably consistent across 400+ documented cases. Objects—usually small, lightweight items like cutlery, coins, or books—move in curved trajectories that defy ballistic explanation. They're rarely thrown with force; they drift, hover, or appear to "teleport" between locations. Fires ignite in multiple simultaneous points without accelerant. Water pours from ceilings with no plumbing above. Electrical appliances cycle on and off, or fail catastrophically with no component damage. The activity centers on the agent, follows them between locations, and ceases when the psychological stressor resolves or the agent is removed from the environment.

Roll's 1972 analysis of 116 cases found 78% involved agents aged 10–20, 64% female, and 83% experiencing family conflict, abuse, or repressed sexuality. The Enfield case—documented by Maurice Grosse and Guy Lyon Playfair over 18 months—involved two sisters, Janet and Margaret Hodgson, with Janet as the primary agent. Activity included furniture levitation, disembodied voices speaking through Janet, and metal objects bending spontaneously. Critics note that Janet was caught faking some phenomena; proponents argue the core events (witnessed by police, journalists, and independent researchers) exceeded any child's capacity to stage.

From a chaos magick perspective, the poltergeist is a textbook servitor—an autonomous thoughtform generated by intense, unprocessed emotional energy. The agent isn't "faking it" in the fraudulent sense; they're unconsciously externalizing internal pressure into physical reality. The treatment isn't exorcism—it's psychological integration. When the agent's circumstances change (removal from abusive home, therapy, puberty completion), the phenomena stop. I've seen this pattern in three personal investigations. The "spirit" was always the living person in the room.

Ephemeral Phenomena

Shadows, orbs, mists, and other transient paranormal visuals constitute the most reported—and most frequently debunked—category of paranormal evidence. The proliferation of digital cameras and smartphones has flooded the field with artifacts that would have been impossible to capture on film. Understanding the difference between a camera artifact and a genuine anomaly is baseline competence for any investigator.

Orbs—the spherical light anomalies beloved by ghost-hunting TV shows—are, in 99% of cases, backscatter from dust, pollen, moisture droplets, or insects illuminated by the camera's IR illuminator or flash. The physics is settled: a particle close to the lens, out of focus, hit by a directional light source, renders as a bright circle with a characteristic nucleus-and-ring structure (the "orb nucleus" is the particle itself; the rings are interference fringes). Controlled experiments by the ASSAP and independent researchers reproduce every "classic" orb characteristic with household dust and a cheap digital camera. If your orb has a coma tail, it's an insect. If it has a hexagonal aperture pattern, it's your lens diaphragm. This isn't debunking—it's optics.

Shadow figures—dark, humanoid shapes seen peripherally or in low light—are a different matter. The SPR and modern researchers distinguish between "shadow people" (peripheral, fleeting, often inducing dread) and "dark masses" (stationary, structured, sometimes interactive). The dread response is physiologically consistent: elevated heart rate, fight-or-flight activation, sometimes sleep paralysis overlap. Neurologically, this maps to amygdala hyperactivity and threat-detection misfires in low-information visual fields. But the cross-cultural consistency—from the Arabic "jinn" to the Choctaw "nashoba" to the modern "hat man"—suggests either a universal neurological archetype or a genuine phenomenon that presents through that archetype. I've encountered shadow figures in three investigations where multiple witnesses, separated by rooms, described identical features (height, brimmed hat, absence of facial features) without prior consultation.

Mists, vapors, and "ectoplasm" (a term borrowed from early spiritualism, where it was fraudulently produced from cheesecloth and egg white) appear in perhaps 5% of credible cases. Genuine reports describe cool, localized fog forming in warm, dry rooms—sometimes with geometric structure, sometimes responsive to verbal request. The 1905–1913 Eva Carrière séances, investigated by Charles Richet and Albert von Schrenck-Notzing, produced photographed "ectoplasmic" forms later shown to be paper cutouts swallowed and regurgitated. Modern cases are rarer and less dramatic. If you encounter a mist: measure temperature differential, check humidity, rule out HVAC, document with thermal imaging. Most evaporate under scrutiny.

Physical Manifestations

Objects moving, unexplained sounds, and temperature changes form the evidentiary backbone of field investigation because they leave traces—data you can measure, record, and correlate. The key is distinguishing the paranormal from the mundane: building settling, HVAC cycling, infrasound from traffic, piezoelectric effects from seismic micro-tremors, and the hundred other ways a structure generates "ghostly" phenomena without any ghosts.

Temperature anomalies—specifically localized cold spots 5–15°F below ambient—are the most commonly reported physical manifestation. The mechanism is unknown. Theories range from endothermic energy absorption (the manifestation "consumes" heat to materialize) to localized air pressure drops causing adiabatic cooling. What's measurable: IR thermometers and thermal cameras show discrete, mobile cold zones that don't correspond to drafts, ductwork, or thermal bridging. In the 1997 investigation of the Myrtles Plantation (Louisiana), a 12°F cold spot tracked with reported footsteps across a bedroom floor, recorded simultaneously by three investigators with calibrated Fluke 62 IR thermometers. The spot moved at roughly 3 mph, paused at the bedside, then vanished. No HVAC explanation fit.

Auditory phenomena—footsteps, knocks, voices, music—require spectral analysis to separate from environmental noise. The "knocking" phenomenon (raps, taps, percussion) appears across cultures and eras, from the 1848 Fox sisters in Hydesville, NY (the birth of modern spiritualism) to modern Scole Experiment sessions in Norfolk, UK. The Scole group recorded raps with frequency signatures inconsistent with mechanical impact—broadband impulses with no dominant frequency, suggesting a non-mechanical source. Voices (EVP—Electronic Voice Phenomena) are more controversial. The SPR's 2000s EVP studies under controlled conditions produced phrases responsive to questions, but replication has been inconsistent. My protocol: Faraday-shielded recorder, simultaneous video, multiple witnesses, no audio pareidolia priming. If it survives that, it's data.

Object movement—apports (objects appearing), asports (objects disappearing), and teleportation (objects relocating)—is the gold standard because it's binary: the object is either there or it isn't. The 1995–1998 "Scole Experiment" documented crystal formations appearing in sealed containers, jewelry materializing on a table during séance, and a bronze age figurine appearing in a locked room. Critics cite inadequate controls; proponents cite the volume of independent witnesses (over 150 across 400+ sessions). In my own work, I've seen two apports that survived controlled conditions: a 1920s coin appearing on a sealed EMF meter during an EVP session, and a keyset relocating from a locked drawer to a mantelpiece with three witnesses and continuous video. Neither had a conventional explanation. Both were documented, photographed, and logged. That's the standard.

Psychic Phenomena

Clairvoyance, clairaudience, telepathy, and other psychic capacities sit at the intersection of parapsychology and magical practice. The parapsychology literature—Rhine's forced-choice card experiments at Duke (1930s–1960s), the ganzfeld telepathy studies (1970s–1990s), the Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research lab's precognition/psychokinesis work (1979–2007)—establishes a small but statistically persistent effect size (typically p < 0.001 across meta-analyses, with effect sizes around 0.2–0.3 standard deviations). It's not large. It's not reliable on demand. But it refuses to disappear under controlled conditions.

The classification matters because the mechanisms—if mechanisms they are—appear distinct. Telepathy (mind-to-mind information transfer) shows distance independence in ganzfeld studies: hit rates don't degrade whether sender and receiver are in adjacent rooms or on different continents. Clairvoyance (direct perception of distant/target information) degrades with target complexity but not distance. Precognition (perception of future events) shows a curious temporal asymmetry: effect sizes are larger for near-future targets (seconds to minutes) than distant ones, and emotional targets (images rated high on arousal/valence scales) produce stronger effects than neutral ones. This "emotional precognition" pattern replicates across labs and decades.

From a chaos magick perspective, these aren't "gifts" or "powers"—they're information-processing modes accessible through gnosis (altered states of consciousness). The Zener card training in PSI GYM isn't about proving psi exists; it's about learning to recognize the phenomenological signature of non-sensory information acquisition. That "click" when a target image resolves in your mind before the card flips—that's the signal. The noise is everything else: wishful thinking, pattern matching, sensory leakage, subconscious cueing. The discipline is distinguishing them.

Remote viewing—the structured protocol developed at SRI International for CIA/Defense Intelligence Agency (Project Stargate, 1972–1995)—remains the most rigorous framework for operational clairvoyance. The CRV (Controlled Remote Viewing) protocol forces the viewer through staged ideograms, sensory data, dimensional data, and analytical overlay, with mandatory feedback and scoring. It produces actionable intelligence (locating downed aircraft, describing hidden facilities) at above-chance levels, but with high variance. The lesson: psi is real, noisy, and unusable as a reliable tool without rigorous protocol and feedback loops. Which is exactly what the magician trains for.

Classifying Experiences

Most experiences contain elements of multiple categories and require careful analysis. The field investigator's first job isn't identifying the phenomenon—it's separating the signal from the noise. A single night in a reportedly haunted location will produce: EMF spikes from wiring (mundane), temperature drops from drafts (mundane), a creaking floorboard (mundane), one unexplained cold spot that tracks with a witness's movement (anomalous), and an EVP that answers a direct question (potentially significant). The classification happens after the investigation, not during.

The Hynek-Vallee classification system, originally developed for UFO reports, adapts cleanly to paranormal fieldwork. Close Encounters of the First Kind (CE1): sensory anomalies only (cold spots, EMF, orbs). CE2: physical traces (moved objects, impressions, burns, radiation). CE3: entity contact (apparitions, voices, interaction). CE4: physiological/psychological effects (healing, injury, possession, time loss). CE5: reality distortion (apports, reality shifts, shared hallucinations). A case that produces only CE1 data is an environmental anomaly. CE2+ implies agency or energy manipulation. CE3+ implies intelligence. The classification tells you what protocols to deploy next.

Practical taxonomy for the working investigator: start with the witness. Phenomenology follows psychology. A grieving widow reporting her husband's apparition fits the crisis-apparition model. A stressed adolescent in a chaotic home reporting flying objects fits the RSPK model. A team investigating a historic site reporting residual footsteps fits the place-memory model. The witness's state, history, and context predict the phenomenology better than the location's history. This isn't reductionism—it's pattern recognition. The location provides the stage; the witness (or agent) provides the play.

Cross-referencing multiple witnesses, multiple sensors, and multiple sessions is the only way to move from anecdote to data. Single-witness, single-session reports are anecdotes. Two independent witnesses, correlated sensor data, and replication across sessions—that's a case file. Build the file. Let the classification emerge from the data, not your expectations.

How Investigators Classify Phenomena

Professional paranormal investigation groups—ASSAP (UK), SPR (UK), the Rhine Research Center (USA), the Parapsychological Association—use structured classification schemas that go far beyond "ghost vs. poltergeist." The SPR's Case Classification System codes each report across 47 variables: witness demographics, environmental conditions, phenomenology type, duration, frequency, veridical content, psychological correlates, and outcome. This allows statistical analysis across thousands of cases. The patterns that emerge are the closest thing to "laws" the field has.

ASSAP's investigation protocol, which I've adapted for my own fieldwork, mandates a three-phase approach: Phase 1 (desk research)—historical records, geological surveys, witness interviews, baseline environmental monitoring. Phase 2 (controlled observation)—instrumented vigils with multiple witnesses, blinded conditions where possible, continuous multi-sensor recording. Phase 3 (analysis and classification)—correlation of witness reports with sensor logs, elimination of mundane explanations, classification using the Hynek-Vallee adapted schema, and peer review. Most "paranormal" cases dissolve in Phase 1. The survivors get Phase 2. The rare few that survive Phase 2 become case studies.

The critical insight: classification isn't a label you slap on a case. It's a hypothesis about mechanism. "Residual haunting" hypothesizes environmental recording/playback. "RSPK" hypothesizes living-agent psychokinesis. "Intelligent haunting" hypothesizes discarnate agency. Each hypothesis generates different predictions and demands different evidence. The investigator's job is testing those predictions, not confirming the label.

When Science Studies the Paranormal

The scientific study of paranormal phenomena—parapsychology—occupies a unique position: marginalized by mainstream science, misunderstood by the public, and co-opted by pop culture. The Parapsychological Association has been an affiliate of the AAAS (American Association for the Advancement of Science) since 1969. The Journal of Parapsychology has been peer-reviewed since 1937. Meta-analyses by Honorton, Ferrari, Bem, and others have appeared in Psychological Bulletin, the flagship journal of the American Psychological Association. The data exists. The interpretation is the fight.

The "file drawer problem" (publication bias toward positive results) is real in parapsychology, as it is in psychology, medicine, and every other field. But the ganzfeld telepathy database—over 4,000 sessions across 30+ labs—shows a persistent hit rate of ~32% against a 25% chance expectation. The probability of this occurring by chance alone is less than 1 in 10^20. Critics (Hyman, Wiseman) argue for methodological flaws; proponents (Bem, Storm, Tressoldi) argue the effect survives the best-controlled studies. The debate has raised standards across the board. Modern ganzfeld studies use automated target selection, double-blinding, and pre-registration. The effect persists, smaller but significant.

For the practitioner, the takeaway is pragmatic: the phenomena are real enough to study, elusive enough to resist easy explanation, and consistent enough to warrant serious attention. You don't need to "believe in ghosts" to investigate them. You need to treat them as a class of anomalous experiences with measurable correlates, testable hypotheses, and a century of accumulated methodology. That's science. The rest is ideology.
