Occult Culture Today: How Practice Is Actually Learned

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Practices that would once have arrived by post now arrive by autoplay. Nearly everyone reading this learned something about sigils, servitors or planetary hours from a short video watched at two in the morning, and very few of them did it through a teacher, a lodge, or a lineage anyone could trace. That is not a complaint. It is the situation, and pretending otherwise is how people end up giving bad advice to beginners. Ask how someone should start and the usual answer assumes a structure that no longer exists. Nobody is being contacted by a letter. There is no local branch you can walk into. The search box is the gate and the results page is the curriculum. What follows is a description of the terrain as it stands: where material actually surfaces, which learning routes reliably produce someone who can work rather than someone who collects books, and what the folk practices that never passed through England look like when you meet them. It is written for the person who has been told they are doing it wrong because their method is unsanctioned.

Where People Actually Find Occult Material Now

The single biggest change in the last twenty years is not that occult material got more available. It got stranger. A beginner in 1985 faced a wall of paper: a few translated grimoires, a handful of out-of-print Crowley, and a small number of people willing to say anything. A beginner today faces an infinite supply of video, most of it made by people with no training at all, and a real difficulty distinguishing a competent practitioner from someone who bought a font and a purple filter.

Search replaced correspondence as the first point of contact, and forum culture replaced the local circle as the second. Those forums did something the print era could not: they let a person with eleven years of practice be found by someone with eleven weeks of questions, in under a minute, at no cost. That is an enormous good. It is also where most of the false confidence in the community comes from, because a confident voice and a correct voice are visually identical in a text box.

The consequence for the beginner is specific. You will read the same claim from four people who have never tested it, and one person who has tested it and will not make a fuss about it. Volume tracks inversely with reliability. If you want signal, look for the person who reports what did not work, not the one with the most confident results.

The Many Ways a Working Practice Gets Learned

There are five recognisable routes, and they produce noticeably different practitioners. The first is book-led and slow: a tradition text, then a commentary, then practice, then a critique of both. It takes years and it produces people who can explain what they are doing. The second is video-led and fast, which is where most people start today. It produces excellent technique in small doses and almost no structure.

The third is community-led. A study group, a public ritual, a discord server with a real calendar. It produces accountability, which is the single strongest predictor of whether a beginner is still practicing a year later. The fourth is lineage-led, which today means an online teacher rather than an initiation. That route works and it fails in the same way: it can transmit skill quickly, and it can install a personality structure you will have to dismantle later.

The fifth is accidental. Somebody picks up a book about historical folk magic because they are interested in local history, and six months later they are doing a working. This is rarer than people assume, because it requires you to keep going without being told to. The people who are actually good at this, in every tradition, tend to have started by accident and stayed because the thing worked well enough to be worth the inconvenience.

Regional Traditions That Never Passed Through England

The dominant occult pedagogy in the English-speaking internet is a reconstruction. Victorian ceremonial magic was itself assembled from Renaissance grimoires, which were assembled from Arabic, Jewish and Latin sources, which were assembled from older material. Everything popular in the west is a copy of a copy of a copy, and the copies lost things in transit.

In Peru, Mexico, Brazil and the Caribbean there are working traditions that never entered that chain. They carry plant knowledge, house-level practice and family transmission that arrived by a completely separate route. Cascarilla work in Mexico, the séances and herbal practice across the Caribbean, the specific domestic forms of Brazilian folk magic: these are not early versions of the same thing. They are parallel systems with their own logic, often better documented locally than anything in the standard western curriculum.

Nobody is going to hand you permission to claim any of it, and the field has real internal politics about who gets to speak for whom. What you can do is read the practitioners rather than the commentators, notice that these traditions have never needed a symbolic logic to justify themselves, and take the practical parts. Cleaning, offering, household attention and local plant knowledge are not a preliminary exercise. They are the craft.

DIY Practice and the Punk Continuum

Since the late 1970s there has been a steady current of do-it-yourself occult practice running through punk and industrial scenes, and it has never been organised, credentialed or particularly apologetic. The continuity is real: the reason this current exists is the same reason the 1980s label existed, which is suspicion of anybody who wants you to wait for permission.

The practical character of that work is worth studying even if the aesthetic is not your scene. DIY practitioners tend to build their own tools, keep their own records, and treat borrowed authority with a raised eyebrow. They are also much more willing to say that a ritual failed, because there is no lineage standing behind them and no position to lose.

There is a cost. Without a teacher, a beginner can build a system that is internally consistent and externally nonsense, and there is no one to notice for years. The mitigation is not to join an order. It is to keep one outside reader whose only job is to say, in plain language, what your practice is supposed to accomplish. Most people never bother, and most of those people stop within two years.

Memes as Shared Symbolic Objects

The internet has produced something that looks trivial and is not: symbolic objects that nobody originates and everybody reinforces. The keke archetype is the cleanest example. It has no author, no lineage, no founding text. It emerged out of forum noise, mutated, and became a genuine shared symbol for a specific kind of intent.

The mechanism is straightforward social reinforcement. Each use by a new person signals what the symbol means, each use by an existing user confirms it, and the pair produces a conventional object out of nothing. That is the same process that produced most of the folklore in the first place, running at internet speed and with far less drift.

Whether a meme does anything is a separate question and it is not settled. What is settled is that it functions socially, which means the people using it are already invested, already signalling membership, already holding an intention in mind while holding the phone. If the practice is a component in producing a result, that component is doing something regardless of the symbol's origin. A practitioner who takes this seriously will still insist on writing the target as a sentence, because a sentence is what gets acted on later.

What Public Posting Changes and What It Costs

Being public about practice used to carry a specific risk: initiation meant exposure, and exposure meant leverage against you. That leverage is largely gone. A public practitioner today is visible, searchable, and harder to shame, and also easier to find by people who want something.

The main practical cost of posting is the feedback loop of performed certainty. Once your practice is content, you start editing it in real time. A disappointing result gets omitted. A good one gets a thread. Within a few months you have built a public record that makes you look more effective than your private notes support, and the gap between the two starts doing damage to your actual work.

The other cost is borrowed structure. A public practice acquires a calendar, a vocabulary and a set of approved results, all shaped by what the audience responds to. None of that is bad on its own. It is worth knowing that you built it, and that you can stop building it.

Checking Claims You Cannot Verify

Most occult claims cannot be checked, and pretending otherwise wastes months. A handful can, and those are worth the effort because the habit transfers. The three that transfer best are about your own record-keeping, your own attention and your own honesty about failures.

Pre-register the prediction. Write down what you expect, how you would recognise it, and by when, before the operation. Set a deadline. Then count every attempt, including the ones that produced nothing, including the ones you would rather forget, including the ones done badly enough that you can dismiss them. This is unromantic bookkeeping and it is the only mechanism anyone has found for separating a working practice from a lucky one.

The purpose is not to catch yourself being wrong about the universe. It is to find out which of your methods reliably produce results, so that you can do more of those and stop doing the rest. Most people who keep serious records discover that a small number of techniques carry nearly all of their effect, and that the technique they were most attached to does almost nothing.

A First Year That Survives Contact With Reality

A year that works has a shape. It is less eventful than the internet suggests and considerably more useful. Read method before lore, so that you can tell the difference between a system and a story. Pick one tradition, work it long enough to know whether you have an affinity for it, and treat everything else as reading rather than as competing truth.

Keep a daily practice short enough that a bad week does not end it. Fifteen minutes that survives a bad week beats two hours that do not, and this is the most reliable predictor of whether somebody is still practicing in year two. Keep a written record from day one, even when there is nothing to record yet, because the value is entirely in the accumulation.

Do not follow one figure indefinitely. A teacher is genuinely useful for a defined skill, and a single source held for ten years is the most common structural failure in this community, because nobody publishes a correction. Read method, read history, and keep one person outside your own lineage whose only function is to tell you what your practice is for.

  1. Read method before lore, and lore before history.
  2. Choose one tradition and stay inside it long enough to be assessed rather than started.
  3. Keep daily practice under twenty minutes for the first three months.
  4. Start a written log on day one, even with nothing to record.
  5. Keep one outside reader whose job is to say what your practice is for.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I follow a single teacher or study many sources?

Both, in a specific order. Learn one defined skill from one person, because scattered instruction is worse than a single imperfect source. Then read two or three traditions that disagree with each other, because disagreement is where you find out which parts were doctrine and which were method. Holding a single figure for a decade is the most common structural failure in this community, and the reason is practical rather than spiritual: teachers change, and if your whole practice is a translation of one person's preferences, it does not survive them.

Should I read books before I start practicing?

Read two, then start. Liber Null and Liber Kaos will give you the method, and they are short. Then read one solid history, not to believe it and not to argue with it, but because history is what keeps you honest about where a technique came from and what it was actually for. Everything else can wait until you have three months of practice behind you and can tell which claims you would have believed a year ago.

Is it acceptable to be public about my practice?

Entirely. Contemporary practice is largely public and obscurity no longer functions as protection, because anything worth hiding is already documented online. Being visible has one real cost: your practice starts getting edited for an audience, and a disappointing result quietly stops making it into the record. Keep the public version and the private notebook, and make sure only one of them is the one you actually learn from.

How do I tell which claims are real when I cannot verify them?

You mostly cannot, and you should plan for that rather than around it. What you can do is verify the three things that transfer: that you wrote the prediction before, that you counted every attempt including the failures, and that you recorded the result at the deadline rather than whenever the mood improved. Most people who keep serious records find that a couple of techniques carry nearly all of their effect, and that the one they were most attached to does very little.

What does a paid application actually add to a daily practice?

The learning is free and the method is free, and no application is going to hand you an ability you do not have. What a paid purchase actually buys is the unglamorous part: a structured record that keeps your daily practice and your written log in one place, timestamps so you cannot quietly rewrite a bad month, offline access, and reminders you cannot ignore. That is bookkeeping, not magic, and it is the part that determines whether you still have a practice in a year. Paying once suits a practice you expect to keep for a decade far better than anything that bills you every month.
