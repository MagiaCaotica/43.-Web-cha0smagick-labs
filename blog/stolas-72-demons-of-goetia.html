STOLAS

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 13 min read

Stolas occupies the forty-first position in the printed Ars Goetia, where he is described as a prince and credited with the instruction of astronomy. That single line is very nearly the whole of the source material, and almost everything written about him since is either an expansion of it or an invention layered on top. This guide works from the entry outward: what the text actually claims, where the rank listings disagree with one another, what a seal is for, and how a working built on the entry should be structured. It is written for a practitioner who already has a working practice and wants to add a goetic element to it without inheriting a pile of confident folklore. Nothing here requires you to treat the spirit as real, and nothing here asks you to perform anything you have not decided on yourself.

The entry as it stands in the printed text

The Ars Goetia presents each of its seventy-two entries in a fixed form. You get a name, a rank, a visible appearance, a set of offices or fields of knowledge, and a number of legions assigned to the spirit. Stolas is brief by the standards of the book. A prince, of the race of princes, he appears in the form of a great owl, and he is said to teach the arts of astronomy. He knows the herbs and the stones. That is close to the complete entry.

The compactness matters more than it first appears. Many practitioners quote long descriptions of other spirits and then present them as though the same density of source existed for all seventy-two. It did not. Some entries run to a full column, others run to two lines. Treating Stolas as a thin entry is not a criticism of the source. It is a description of it, and a working built on a short entry has less native material to work with than a working built on a long one.

Anyone who tells you the sources give you more here is either holding a different edition or holding an invention. The seventeenth-century arrangement is stable and widely reproduced, so there is no genuine scholarly disagreement about what the entry says. The disagreement is downstream, in the commentary, and it is worth knowing which side of it a source is on before you borrow from it.

Rank, and why the listings disagree

Stolas is called a prince in the printed text. He is also very often listed as a president, and a good deal of the material online simply contradicts itself. The three headings in the book are presidents, dukes and kings, and they are not a difficulty ladder in the way the popular reading implies. They are a structural feature of how the text was assembled, not a ranking of power.

The confusion is traceable. Later arrangements of the seventy-two, some of them reordered so the numbering runs differently, place him elsewhere. If you have been given a list, find out whether it is the seventeenth-century arrangement or a modern rearrangement before you build anything on the rank. It is a small thing, and it is still the kind of small thing that quietly invalidates a working.

  • What the text gives: prince, an owl form, astronomy, herbs, stones, and a stated number of legions
  • What later readers add: a wider curriculum, a temperament, and a biography nobody can source
  • What a working can actually use: a stated domain, a visible form, and a bounded set of claims

What teaching astronomy actually claims

Read plainly, the entry says Stolas instructs astronomy. It does not say he grants astronomical understanding as a gift, and it does not promise that anyone who performs a working will be able to read a star chart the next morning. The word carrying the claim is instruction, and instruction implies a teacher and a learner working over time. It implies a curriculum rather than an infusion.

That reading has an awkward consequence for how the entry is usually used. If the domain is teaching, a working built around him ought to involve learning something, and ought to carry some way of noticing whether the learning happened. Most practice in this area skips both halves. The ceremony occurs, something feels significant, and the entry is filed under a success. The alternative is duller and considerably more useful.

There is a historical reading worth taking seriously here. Astronomy in the seventeenth century comfortably included astrology, and it sat beside the mathematical disciplines as a practical branch of learning. A spirit credited with that curriculum is not a cosmic being in any modern sense. It is an office, and offices have purposes, which is a much better foundation for a working than grandeur.

The classical office behind the name

The name arrives in a lineage that was not invented for the grimoire. Stolas appears in earlier demonological material and in the medieval and early-modern lists the Ars Goetia draws upon, in the same register throughout: named powers, arranged in ranks, each with an assigned sphere. The purpose of such lists was never to describe wandering evils. It was to structure a body of knowledge about spirits so that the knowledge could be used.

The Aramaic and Hebrew demonological material feeding the grimoire works the same way, with officials, judges, executioners and tempters, each attached to a function. Read that way the spirits are administrative. They are the personnel of a system built to explain events and to direct conduct, and the technical vocabulary for calling them comes from the same procedural world as a court summons.

This framing does not make the material harmless, and it does not make it true. It makes it legible, which is what you need in order to work with it. A clerk's office can be described in twenty lines and still be a real office with real duties. The seventy-two entries behave the same way, and reading them as personnel rather than as monsters changes what a working is actually asking for.

Why the modern reading got louder

The popular image of these spirits came from somewhere, and it did not come from the grimoire. Nineteenth and twentieth century fiction, and rather more visual media after that, moved them into a register of menacing predators and personal agents. The shift is straightforward to document and worth knowing about, because a practitioner who has absorbed the popular version is working from a text that does not exist.

There is a commercial layer on top of that. The goetic material now circulates as a brand, with applications, video essays, ranking videos and commentary that treat the seventy-two as a product line. That framing tends to select the most dramatic detail from each entry and drop the rest, which produces a body of character descriptions that share a source but have no consistent shape.

None of this is a reason to leave the material alone. It is a reason to notice which version you are holding. If your working depends on the spirit being frightening, you are working with the fiction. If it depends on the stated domain, you are working with the text. Those are different projects, they produce different results, and they cannot be honestly reported as the same practice.

The seal, and what a seal is doing

Each entry in the book carries a seal, and a seal is a graphic device. A figure inside a circle, with a name, standing in for the spirit's identity in a single image. In the printed grimoire the seals are crude by any modern standard, drawn small and reproduced cheaply, and they were plainly never intended as art. They are identification marks.

The function is the same one a sigil serves in current practice. It compresses a named thing into a form that can be drawn, carried, inscribed and activated without writing the name out loud every time. That is not mysticism talking. It is what compression is. A name is a long string, and a seal is a short handle you can carry in one hand.

Where people go wrong is treating the seal as a portrait. It is not a picture of anything. It is a mark standing in for a name, and it inherits every property a handle should have, including the properties you would want: distinctive enough to recognise, portable enough to reproduce, and cheap enough that losing one does not matter.

A working, laid out in parts

If you want to work with this entry, the structure below is the one that survives contact with a real week. It borrows the shape of ordinary ceremonial practice and keeps the goetic element small, because the entry gives you very little to work with, and padding a thin source with borrowed material weakens whatever the thin part was doing.

The order is deliberate. Terms before action, so you are not improvising a contract in the middle of a rite. A bounded duration, so the working ends on its own rather than when you get bored of it. A record, so that afterwards you can tell whether anything happened or whether you simply had a pleasant evening. That last step is the one people drop, and it is the only one that tells you anything at all.

  1. State the terms in writing first: what you are asking for, how long you will work, and what you will not promise to do about the answer
  2. Set a start date and a duration, and put both somewhere you will see them on ordinary days rather than only on ritual days
  3. Do the work at a fixed hour, in the same place, with the same materials, for the length of the cycle
  4. Write the record on the day, including the days when nothing occurred and you felt nothing at all

The terms, settled before you begin

The most common failure in this material happens before the candle is lit. Someone arrives without a stated request, improvises one in the atmosphere of the rite, and later cannot tell whether the working was answered because the request was never specified in the first place. The atmosphere of the rite actively encourages this, because atmosphere is persuasive, and a request made in that state tends to be the one you most want to have made.

Write the request down in one sentence, in the imperative, with a measurable object. Not a state of mind. Not a general improvement in your life. A specific thing that could either happen or fail to happen inside the window you set. This is standard practice in chaos magic and it applies here without modification, because the goetic frame does not supply a better answer than the plain one does.

The second half of the terms is what you decline to promise. Most of the reported harm around this material comes from people who felt they had to reciprocate, as though a request carried an invisible invoice. Nothing in the text supports that. An office that instructs does not bill, and a request carrying an unspoken price will be answered by your own anxiety long before anything else gets a chance to answer it.

Where the reading overreaches

The overreach is easy to identify once you know what the text supports, because it clusters in three places. The first is the personality claim, where a spirit described in two lines is handed a temperament, a mood and a biography. The second is the power claim, where a stated domain quietly becomes a guarantee. The third is the hierarchy claim, where a prince is placed above a king in matters of consequence.

None of the three are available from the text, and all three are easy to find online. A source offering you a personality for Stolas is offering an invention, however plausible and however confidently it is delivered. The test is simple enough to apply in a reading room: can the claim be traced back to the entry, and if it cannot, is the source acknowledging that it is an addition rather than a source?

This is not pedantry dressed up as rigour. The reason to stay with the text is that invented material cannot be tested. You can check whether a working aimed at learning astronomy produces learning. You cannot check whether Stolas is irascible, because any outcome you report will be consistent with the claim. A framework that absorbs every result is not doing any work, whatever else it is doing.

The failure modes, in the order they show up

Four failure modes account for nearly all of the trouble people report from this material, and not one of them is supernatural. All of them are ordinary, all of them are preventable, and that is good news, because the prevention is cheap and does not require any belief at all.

They also arrive in a predictable order, which is most of the defence. The first two appear inside the opening week, while the novelty is still carrying the practice. The third arrives around the third week, when the work has become boring and the only thing keeping it going is the plan. The fourth arrives at the end, and it is the only one of the four that is genuinely serious.

  1. No stated request, which leaves the working with no measurable outcome and therefore no way to be wrong
  2. Enlarged expectations, where a bounded request is quietly swapped for a large one after the rite begins
  3. Drift, when the cycle stops being scheduled and becomes something done when convenient or when worried
  4. Escalation, where a routine piece of work becomes the centre of a life and sleep, work or relationships begin to suffer

What the record has to contain

A record is not a diary. Its job is to preserve the difference between what you expected, what happened, and what you did about it. Write the request first, in the same words you used in the terms. Then write what happened, in plain terms, including nothing. Then write what you did next, if anything. Three lines a day is enough, and consistency matters more than length.

The part that matters most is the nothing. A log that records only contact will show you a steady series of successes no matter what you were doing, because the occasions when nothing happened are precisely the ones you will not feel an urge to write down. Keeping them is what turns a record into evidence rather than a mood, and it is the single cheapest habit in the whole practice.

Review the record on the date you set, before you extend anything. Ask what would have had to occur for the request to count as met, whether it did, and what you would change if you ran it again. If the honest answer is that you cannot tell, the request was not specific enough, and the fix is a better request rather than a longer cycle.

The honest inheritance from this entry

Stolas is a useful entry precisely because he is a small one. There is not enough here to build a personality, and not enough to hide behind. What there is, is a stated domain in a field where the honest position is that very little is known, and a form of working that transfers to any learning goal you care to point it at.

Take the astronomy seriously and the entry becomes instructional rather than decorative. Learn the sky. Chart what is actually visible from where you live, at the hours you are claiming to work. Learn to identify what is up, and check your accuracy against a reference rather than against memory. If the working is doing anything at all, that is where it will show, and it will show over months rather than over a week.

The rest of the seventy-two behave the same way, and the discipline transfers cleanly to them. Read the entry, keep to the stated domain, bound the request, keep the record, and let the cycle end on the date you set rather than on the date you feel ready to stop. That sequence is unglamorous, and it is the only reason any of this is worth doing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Stolas a president or a prince?

The printed Ars Goetia calls him a prince, and that is the reading to work from. Several modern lists promote or demote him because they are working from rearranged editions, so check which arrangement your source follows before building anything on the rank. The rank matters less than the popular material suggests, since the operative content of the entry is the stated domain rather than the heading it sits under.

Can a working with this spirit actually teach me astronomy?

That is a testable question, which makes it the best kind. The entry credits him with instruction, and instruction implies a curriculum, so the design is to attempt the learning rather than to wait around for it to be transferred. Chart what is visible from your own location, keep a record of your accuracy, and check whether it improves over a few months.

What am I paying for when I buy a grimoire app instead of reading the print edition?

The methods are public and the print edition is not the only route to the text. What a purchased reference reliably gives you is search, cross-referencing between entries, and a form that survives being consulted at one in the morning. Those are genuine conveniences. They are not access to hidden material, and no product can honestly claim to be.

Is it safe to work with the goetic spirits?

The risks that get reported are behavioural rather than supernatural, and they are the ordinary risks of any self-directed commitment attempted without limits. Keep the cycle short, keep the request bounded, keep sleep and work intact, and stop the practice if it starts displacing them. Nothing in the material calls for self-isolation or for surrendering your decisions to a described entity.

Do I need to believe any of this for it to work?

No. The position the founders of the modern tradition took was that belief is optional and testing is mandatory, and that is a workable arrangement for anyone. What you need is a stated request, a fixed procedure, a deadline and an honestly kept record. With those four things you can tell afterwards whether the working helped, which is worth considerably more than conviction would have been.
