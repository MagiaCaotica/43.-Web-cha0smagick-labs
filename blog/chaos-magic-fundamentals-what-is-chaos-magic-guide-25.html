Chaos Magic Fundamentals

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

The first month of this practice takes about four hours of your time in total, costs nothing, and produces one glyph and one notebook. Everything else you have read about the subject, and a great deal of what you have probably already bought, is optional. This page is the route through: what the founding documents actually asked for, what to aim at, how to build and charge, what the first results will realistically look like, and the three specific ways beginners lose the thread. The most common mistake is treating this as a system to be learned before practice can begin. It is not a system. It is a method with two rules, and the rules only become clear once you have run a couple of cycles and watched your own results. Reading further delays the only step that produces information.

The first month, in order

Here is the whole sequence. In week one, pick one aim and write it as a single sentence with a date and something countable in it. In the second sitting, reduce that sentence to letters and cut the letters into a mark. In the third sitting, charge the mark for ninety seconds. Then spend the following fortnight doing the thing the sentence describes, at the same hour each day, and write the outcome on the fourteenth day before you decide anything about what it meant.

That is roughly four hours across a month, and it is a complete cycle. The order is not negotiable, because each step depends on the previous one existing in written form. The failure pattern is starting at step three, which feels like the interesting part, and it produces a charged mark with no aim attached. A charge without a written sentence is the single most common way a beginner ends up with a drawer of beautiful, meaningless marks.

What the founding documents asked for

Two documents from the mid-1970s are treated as the foundation, written by two people working independently. Both are short. Both spend their energy telling the reader to stop inheriting claims and start testing them, and neither asks you to believe anything about the nature of reality or the intentions of spirits. What they ask is that you write your aims down, run them, and record what happened.

The two requests that carry the method are specificity and honesty. Aims must be specific enough to fail, because a vague aim teaches you nothing. And results must be recorded without editing them in your favour, because a practice with no failure column has no way of knowing which parts are carrying the weight. Everything elaborate that has grown around this core is later addition, and it is not required to get the benefit.

The name and its affiliation

The name is a pun with a claim built into it. It plays on the ordinary phrase while also referencing a religious motif that the writers clearly expected readers to recognise, which was a deliberate statement of where the project came from. John Crowley's book, published the year after Carroll's pamphlet, makes the connection explicit and reprints the relevant text at the front.

Whether you find that compelling or embarrassing is a matter of taste, and neither reaction affects the method. It is worth knowing the reference exists so you can decide what you think of the framing, because a movement that puts a lineage reference in its own name has inherited an obligation to defend the reference. You are not obliged to inherit that obligation. The experiment does not require it.

Choosing a first aim you can check

The test for a first aim is that you will know on a fixed date whether it worked. That rules out almost everything interesting. If you cannot say what countable thing will be different in fourteen days, pick a smaller aim. Not smaller in importance, smaller in observability, which is a different thing and much easier to satisfy.

A good first aim names a behaviour you do repeatedly and currently avoid, and it is written in the imperative. The format is straightforward: a verb, a specific object, and a countable completion condition with a date. Writing it in the imperative is not stylistic. It removes the hedging language that makes an aim negotiable with yourself at four in the morning, and the sentence should be short enough that you can say it from memory.

The build, the charge, and the follow-through

The build is twenty minutes of ordinary craft. Cut the sentence down to the letters that carry it, then cut those letters into a single compact mark. Write the office on the same page. The mark should be simple enough to reproduce in ten seconds, because you will need to produce it quickly at the moment you actually want the reminder.

The charge is ninety seconds. Hold the mark, say the office sentence once aloud, and stop when the ninety seconds are up. Nothing is required here beyond the connection between mark and sentence. The follow-through is the fortnight, and it is where the entire effect lives. Every hour you spend refining the mark is an hour not spent doing the task the mark refers to, which is the trade this method is offering you.

What the first results will look like

Expect a partial success and a note explaining what happened instead. A first cycle that goes perfectly is rare, and if it happens you should be suspicious rather than pleased, because perfect results usually mean the aim was too easy to measure. The realistic outcome is that you did the thing some number of times, that you noticed the count, and that the noticing changed the count in subsequent weeks.

You will also probably find that the first fortnight is where the interest drops off. The charge is satisfying, the build was absorbing, and then there are fourteen days of doing the task with nothing to mark the occasion. That stretch is the method. If you stop there, you have learned that the ceremony is the pleasant part, which is a genuinely useful thing to learn and a common reason people conclude the method does not work.

The three ways beginners lose the thread

The first is buying. Not the book, which is cheap and fine, but the tools. A sigil generator, a set of candles, a deck, a pendulum, an app subscription. Each purchase feels like progress and none of it is, and the spending competes directly with the time that the follow-through requires. The method needs paper and a pen. If you have those, you have everything.

The second is elaborating, which is the same error in a different costume. A first aim should be a single sentence and a single mark. The urge to add correspondences, planetary timings, and a proper invocation arrives almost immediately, and every addition is a way of avoiding the fortnight. The third is abandoning at the first flat fortnight and starting a new aim, which resets the record before it has any content.

The notebook that decides everything

One page per cycle, four columns: the aim as written, the date, whether the countable condition was met, and one sentence on what you think happened. The fourth column is the one that will be worth reading in a year, because it is where you can see your own explanatory habits developing. Almost everyone discovers they are generous to their own failures and harsh on their own successes, and the notebook is the only place that becomes visible.

Keep the notebook even while you are not doing anything else with the practice. It costs nothing, and the value is cumulative. After a dozen cycles you will have something rarer than a system: an accurate record of which kinds of aims work for you and which do not, derived from your own data rather than from a general claim about how people work.

A month with nothing happening

This month is not a sign and it is not unusual. The aim either did not work or the record was not good enough to tell, and there is no third option. The correct response is to write the fourth column honestly, close the cycle, and start a second aim of a different kind. Two flat cycles in a row is worth investigating, usually by narrowing the aim further.

What to avoid is escalating. The pull after a flat month is to make the next one bigger, add more apparatus, and choose a more impressive target, on the reasoning that a bigger aim will produce a bigger signal. This inverts the logic. If the first attempt produced no signal, the instrument is not the problem, and increasing the amplitude of the test only makes it harder to read.

Emotional attachment and the working

Some aims produce strong feeling and some produce none, and the correlation is not what practitioners expect. Emotional intensity tends to appear with aims that are about identity, status, or other people, which are precisely the aims where outside factors are doing most of the work. Flat affects show up with quiet structural aims, which are often the ones that quietly work.

The practical consequence is that you should not select aims because they feel meaningful. A feeling of significance is a poor predictor of whether the fortnight will get done, and getting the fortnight done is the whole mechanism. Pick from the small list of things you have been reliably avoiding, regardless of how noble they sound, and let the significance follow the results rather than lead the selection.

The second aim, and why it should be smaller

The second cycle should be smaller than the first, not larger. This is counter-intuitive and it is the correct advice. The first cycle taught you how to run the process. The second should test whether you can run it again, which requires an aim you are confident you can complete, so that you establish a baseline of actually finishing cycles before you attempt anything hard.

When the second cycle completes, you have a two-row notebook. That is the minimum for any inference at all, and it is enough to notice the most important thing, which is whether you actually do the thing. Practitioners who escalate immediately spend a year with a notebook full of abandoned rows and no data. Practitioners who go small twice usually have a running practice by month four.

What to do before you buy anything

Before you spend a cent: write three aims in the sentence format, and pick the smallest one you can check. Do the build, do the charge, and do the fortnight with nothing but paper. Then read the four columns afterwards and write the fifth thing, which is whether you would do it again. If the answer is no because you lacked equipment, buy the equipment. If the answer is no because the fortnight was dull, that is the correct information, and no equipment will fix it.

This is the whole method, and it is smaller than you expected. The tradition is often presented as a large structure of ranks, correspondences, and initiations, and that structure exists and is interesting, but it is not what produces results. Two sentences and a notebook will do more for you in a year than any amount of apparatus, and the apparatus is available later if you want it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do I need to get started?

Paper and a pen, plus somewhere to sit. The method requires a written aim, a mark cut from letters, ninety seconds of attention, and a fortnight of doing the thing. Everything else sold in connection with this practice is optional, and buying it is the most common way beginners substitute activity for the part that works.

How long does it take to see results?

One cycle is a month: build, charge, then a fortnight of follow-through. Most first cycles produce a partial result, and a full picture requires four or five cycles. What you are looking for in the first year is not evidence that sigils affect the world. It is evidence about your own follow-through rates, which is genuinely useful information either way.

Do I have to believe in magic?

No. The method was written specifically to be usable by people who are uncertain, and it asks for a written aim and an honest record rather than for conviction. Many practitioners hold no metaphysical belief at all and use the technique as a commitment device. Others hold strong belief and get the same benefit. The variable that predicts results is whether the fortnight happens.

What if my aim does not work?

Write that in the fourth column and close the cycle. A failed aim is a normal outcome and it is the most informative kind, because it usually means the aim was either too large, too vague, or the work was not done. Start the second cycle smaller. Two failures in a row is the point at which narrowing is a better response than escalating.

Should I use a sigil generator?

Not for your first cycle. Generators produce correct results, and correctness is not the constraint. The constraint is that you have to build the mark yourself in order to hold it in mind later, and that the twenty minutes of building is time you could spend on the fortnight. Build by hand once, and use a generator afterwards if you find you want one.
