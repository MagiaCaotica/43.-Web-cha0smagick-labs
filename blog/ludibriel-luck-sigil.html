LUDIBRIEL

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

The first thing to get straight is that luck is the wrong word, and using the right word changes what you can expect. A ritual cannot bias a genuinely random outcome. If a coin is fair, the sequence of heads and tails it produces is unaffected by what you drew on a piece of paper this morning. That is not a disappointment; it is the load-bearing wall the rest of the practice is built on, because it removes the most common source of disappointment, the belief that the universe should have responded. What a luck practice can actually work on is the other side of the coin. Almost every situation people describe as lucky or unlucky was not a random outcome at all. It was a decision made under uncertainty by someone who was tired, distracted, or operating on a different schedule, and the decision went one way rather than the other for reasons that had nothing to do with merit. That is where attention, timing, and honest record keeping have purchase. The aim is not to attract fortune. It is to be more awake at the points where fortune is actually decided.

Random outcomes and marginal decisions

Sort any situation people call good or bad luck into one of two piles and the difference becomes obvious. The first pile contains outcomes generated by a process nobody can influence: a dice roll, a lottery draw, a card drawn blind from a shuffled deck. Nothing you do before the draw changes the probability, and any ritual that appears to have worked on that pile either changed the draw itself, which is a different activity, or it got the credit for something else.

The second pile contains decisions made under uncertainty. Which of four things to do next. Whether to send the message or wait. Which candidate to shortlist when two are equally qualified. Whether to take the meeting at four or push it to the morning. These outcomes are not random. They are determined by a person with limited attention, incomplete information, and a cost to weighing anything further. Change the attention, change the time, and the distribution of the choice genuinely moves.

The card deck, used as a probability lesson

The Rhine and Zener material from the 1930s is a contested part of the literature and the experimental record has not held up well under later scrutiny. What survives is not the claim that a particular card cannot be identified, but the deck itself, and its use as an instrument for making probability concrete. Five symbols, one chance in twenty by accident, and a hit rate that looks impressive until you work out how many attempts you made in total.

That arithmetic is the actual lesson, and it applies far beyond card guessing. Any practice where successes are counted and failures are forgotten will produce a high apparent success rate, because the denominator is built from the attempts that felt worth recording. A deck makes the arithmetic external. You can count the misses. Once you have counted them, you have something that could be checked by someone who does not believe you, and that is the point of using an object rather than a memory.

Days of the week as a marker, not a magnet

Assigning weekdays to planets is one of the oldest available scheduling systems, and it survives because it does one job well: it gives you a reason to move a decision to a different day. Monday belongs to the Moon, which carries fluctuation, revision, and things not yet fixed. Thursday belongs to Jupiter, which carries expansion and confidence. Friday belongs to Venus, which carries preference and finishability.

None of this pulls an outcome. What it does is make a boundary you can feel. The useful question at the start of a working is not which day is auspicious. It is which day of the week you are most likely to make the decision in question badly, because you are tired at the end of it, and which day you are most likely to make it well. Then move it. The planets are a vocabulary for that distinction, and the distinction is the working.

Weak signals, and learning to see them

The people who report durable improvement in this area almost never describe winning more often. They describe noticing more. The coincidence that would have been missed entirely three months ago gets registered, and once registered it gets acted on, and acting on it is what converts a coincidence into a result.

This is a trainable capacity and it is the real content of the practice. It consists of holding a weak signal in awareness long enough to check it, without immediately explaining it away in either direction. The person who has not trained this will either discount every coincidence or inflate every one of them. The trained version holds the observation flat and asks a cheap question about it. Flatness, held for long enough, is the skill. The belief comes later or not at all.

Writing a luck aim that is not a wish

The most common error in this material is writing an aim as a wish. I will win the competition. I will meet the right person. The universe will open a door. A wish names an outcome that is either outside your control or requires other people to behave in ways you cannot arrange, and it produces a working that can only ever be scored as a failure.

Write the aim as a change in your own behaviour, in the imperative, and keep it small enough that a month of honest counting could detect it. I will make the call before eleven instead of deciding in the morning. I will ask the second question in interviews. I will check the assumption I am most confident about, every time, before I act on it. Each of these is a decision point, and decision points are the only place the practice can reach.

A working, in order

Draft the sentence. Reduce it to a mark, if you use sigilisation, and if you do not, write the sentence in a form you will not see often enough to become automatic. Decide what the behaviour will be and when it will happen, at the level of the hour, because a decision scheduled for whenever the day is available will be decided by whoever is most tired.

Set the counting method before the first attempt, not after. A tick in a paper diary, dated, with a yes or no and nothing else. Then run it for a full month without a single adjustment to the aim. Every practice that fails in this area fails here rather than at the symbolic stage. The aim is amended on day nine because day four felt bad, the count is discarded, and the conclusion reached is that the method does not work, which was never tested.

Where luck work cannot reach

Be precise about the boundary, because vagueness here produces a record that supports any conclusion. If the outcome is generated by a process independent of your involvement, no amount of attention on your part changes it. If the outcome requires another named person to change their mind for no reason you can give them, you have entered a different ethical territory that is dealt with elsewhere and that this material has nothing useful to say about.

What is left is smaller than people expect and entirely workable. A set of recurring decisions that you currently make badly, at times that are reliably bad, for reasons you can partly name. A talent for noticing patterns you currently talk yourself out of. A set of small decisions you keep postponing because the cost of deciding feels higher than the cost of not deciding. The practice is aimed there, and the results are countable within weeks.

Dice, notebook, and the baseline count

For most of the pile that randomness has to be excluded from, a baseline is enough. Toss a coin or roll a die a fixed number of times and record the count. It takes fifteen minutes and it gives you the honest rate for your own system, which is almost always a little worse than you assumed, and which therefore tells you how large an effect you would need to notice.

That number is the most useful object in the whole practice, and it is the one people skip. Without it you have only impressions, and impressions are exactly what expectancy manipulates. With it you can say that a run of three successes at thirty per cent is unremarkable, which is a sentence that costs you nothing and that you will need to be able to say repeatedly.

Counting outcomes without cheating the count

The rules that keep a count honest are unglamorous and absolute. The unit is fixed in advance. Every qualifying attempt is counted, including the ones you would rather not have happened. A miss cannot be excluded for being ambiguous, since ambiguity is where the desire to exclude lives. And a streak is a streak, not a trend, and the record does not get restarted because the second week was poor.

The only acceptable way to change the count is to change the aim and start a new one, dated, with the abandoned one left in the notebook. Two aiming attempts, side by side, is a much better position than a single attempt followed by a rewrite of what was being measured, and it is the difference between a record and a story.

What a good month actually proves

A good month of ten qualifying attempts at a thirty per cent baseline is not proof of anything. It is roughly what chance produces regularly. The claim that it demonstrates the practice works is the most common error in the entire field, and it is the error that turns a weak technique into a strong delusion within a season.

What a good month does establish is that the practice can be run, and that the counting survived contact with a good result, which is the harder half. What it takes to move past a good month is repetition at a rate that chance would not produce comfortably, in a specific aim, with the misses in the notebook. Three months, one aim, no rewrites. Anything less is a mood, and moods are not evidence even when they are accurate descriptions of how you feel.

The ethics of claiming luck as yours

There is a social cost to this material that does not get discussed. If you tell people that a working improved your luck, and it did not, you have told them something false about the world, and you have trained yourself to notice the things that support it. The claim is not a lie in the strict sense if you hold it provisionally. It becomes one the moment you stop revising it.

The more concrete version of the problem is that luck is a zero-sum story told by whoever wins. If your framing requires other people's outcomes to be worse for your framing to work, the framing is broken, and it will produce exactly the entitlement that this material is supposed to be inoculating against. Keep the attention on your own decisions and your own counts, where the practice has actual purchase, and let the rest of the world keep its outcomes.

Abandoning the working, keeping the observation

A working that has produced no countable change after a defined period should be closed, and closing it does not mean the capability was acquired. The trained capacity, noticing weak signals, is separate from any particular aim and it is worth keeping if it was the real product. The symbolic layer can go.

Closing a working is a piece of administrative work that practitioners skip and regret. Write the end date in the notebook, summarise what was actually counted rather than what was felt, and note the one thing you would change. Then stop. A closed working with an honest summary is a data point you can use later. A working left open stays in the background, quietly discrediting every other practice you attempt, because the unresolved one is always the one that might have worked.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a sigil or ritual actually make a random outcome more likely?

Not if the outcome is genuinely random. No procedure of drawing, charging, or scheduling changes the probability of a fair draw. What the practice can affect is the decisions you make around the draw, and the decisions you make are where most of what gets called luck is actually produced. Any claim that the ritual itself reached into a random process is a claim that has nothing to offer, since it makes the ritual unnecessary.

What is the difference between luck work and a manifestation or wish ritual?

The difference is whether the outcome is something you do. A manifestation aims at an event in the world that requires other people and circumstances to cooperate. A luck working aims at a change in your own behaviour or attention that changes the distribution of choices you make. The second is measurable in weeks; the first usually is not measurable at all, and the difference in evidence is not a difference in sincerity.

How many attempts do I need before I know whether the method works?

Enough that chance becomes an unlikely explanation, which depends entirely on your baseline and how large the effect is. A modest shift in a frequent decision may need a few hundred counted attempts over months. Set the threshold in advance, because choosing it after seeing the results is the step that converts an experiment into a confirmation. A month is enough to know whether the practice is runnable and nowhere near enough to know whether it works.

Does timing really matter if the cause is not physical?

Timing matters as a change in decision quality, not as an influence on events. A tired person at the end of a long week makes different choices from the same person on a rested morning, and if you move the decision from one to the other you have changed the outcome distribution through a completely ordinary mechanism. That mechanism is testable, which is the reason to be interested.

What should I do if my aim is about another person?

Rewrite it, or drop it. An aim about whether a specific other person contacts you, chooses you, or changes their feelings is aimed at a decision you cannot make and would be trying to make for them, and no counting method will tell you anything useful about it. Aims that concern your own conduct in a situation involving other people, how you show up, what you ask, what you are willing to accept, are legitimate and testable.
