Hyper-Accelerated Sigils: What the Claim Survives in Practice

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 12 min read

Hyper-acceleration is a real term with a real source. It comes from Spare's late sigil material, where he describes producing a mark and charging it in a fraction of the time an ordinary working takes, and he means it as a gain rather than a trick. Most material that uses the term then slides from a claim about setup into a claim about outcome, and the slide is where the useful part gets lost. A short test of the idea separates three things that get run together: how fast you can build a sigil, how fast you can charge it, and how long you have to wait before you can honestly check whether it worked. The first two can be compressed almost to nothing. The third is the measurement, and it is the one that decides whether you learned anything.

Where the term comes from and what it originally claimed

Spare's sigil work runs from about 1909 to 1911, and the language changes across it. The early material is about notation, transcription, and the practical mechanics of reduction. The late material, circulated as the Sigils of Power sheets, adds a second register with words like transmutation, hyper-acceleration and the ninth key, and it is that second register the acceleration claim comes from.

The original claim is not that a faster sigil produces a bigger effect. It is that a shorter preparation releases the intention with less friction, because most of the delay in a conventional working is spent on preparation that has nothing to do with the intention: deciding what you want, choosing a symbol, redrawing because the first one was ugly, waiting for the right hour, talking yourself into it. Strip that out and the intention is released in a state closer to the one you had when you decided, which is the actual argument. Acceleration in this sense is a claim about the state of the person releasing, not about the speed of the universe responding.

That reading is narrower than the popular version and considerably more defensible. It also makes a specific prediction, which is the test in the next section.

The three intervals that get confused for one

Every sigil working has three intervals, and almost all the disagreement about acceleration is a disagreement about which one is being shortened.

**The build interval** is from the decision to the finished mark. In an unhurried practice it runs minutes to a week: you write the statement, sit with it, choose your approach, try twice, discard the first attempt. It is the interval most worth compressing, because most of that time is deliberation rather than magic, and Spare's objection to it is well founded.

**The charge interval** is from the finished mark to its release. Here the tradition is nearly unanimous and nearly silent on numbers, which usually means the honest answer is that it does not matter much. Fifteen minutes of focused attention on a small mark is not a small amount of concentration. The charge is rarely the bottleneck.

**The verification interval** is from the release to the moment you can honestly check. It is the one that cannot be compressed, and the reason is structural rather than mystical: an interval you are willing to shorten is an interval you can rationalise, and a check you could have shortened is a check whose result you can reinterpret afterwards. The interval is the measurement. Remove it and you are not going faster, you are reading your own mood sooner.

Material that claims a sigil manifested instantly is almost always describing the first two intervals and presenting them as the third. You can build in four minutes and charge in six. Whether that produced anything is still checked on a date, and the date is doing the work whether or not anyone mentions it.

The prediction the narrow reading makes, and whether it holds

If acceleration is about the state of the person releasing, then it predicts something specific: a fast-built sigil should be released in a state closer to the original decision than a slow-built one, and it should show that in how well the statement survived the reduction. A person who deliberated for a week and then wrote down a one-sentence outcome has done real work in that week. A person who builds in four minutes from a wish they formed four minutes ago has released a much vaguer intention, and the speed has not improved it.

This is easy to test and it mostly fails the popular claim. A short run of nine build times, recorded before the review date rather than after, shows the pattern clearly: the fast builds that produced checkable results were the ones written into a form that already existed, because the formulation had been done earlier without being called preparation. The fast builds that produced nothing were the ones where four minutes was the entire time the intention had existed.

So the useful content of the claim survives, but it relocates. What accelerates is not the sigil. It is the *formulation*, and only if you have already done it. Acceleration applied to an unformed intention is a way of getting an ambiguous intention into a compact shape faster, which is a genuine loss of information and not a gain.

Stacking: the technique that is actually in the sources

The most concrete accelerated practice in Spare's material is not a fast single sigil. It is building several sigils for one intention, in one sitting, in a sequence, sometimes with the pieces combined at the end. He did this systematically, and in the traditional framing each key or layer is doing a different job in one composite act rather than four separate attempts.

Done well, stacking is a single concentrated act on a single statement, which is exactly what the narrow reading of acceleration describes. You decide once, reduce once per layer, charge the composite once, and you have avoided the eight separate sittings that four independent workings would have cost. The efficiency is real and the concentration is real.

The cost is that stacking is the least reviewable practice in the tradition. Four workings on one intention give you four chances to be wrong and a strong incentive to count only the layer that worked, which you will not be able to identify afterwards. One working on one intention gives you one result, and the one result is interpretable. If you want to know whether a given technique works on you, single layers are the only version that produces that knowledge.

There is also an interference problem that nobody in the accelerated material mentions. Several marks for the same intention, charged in one sitting, in the same emotional state, are close to duplicates from the point of view of the thing you are trying to influence, and the usual result of duplication in a working is dilution or noise rather than emphasis. If you are stacking to increase force, you may be increasing the chance that nothing in particular is the message.

A demonstration run, with the numbers left in

Three intentions, three sigils each, built in a single sitting per intention. Sittings spaced two weeks apart so the review dates do not collide. Build times recorded. Nothing here is evidence for anything; it is a worked example of the bookkeeping, and the bookkeeping is the transferable part.

**Intention one, an admin task.** One form, sent to a body that had ignored two previous requests. The ordinary baseline: 25 minutes of formulation, 14 minutes to build, 10 to charge. The accelerated version, three days later: 4 minutes to build and 8 to charge, after the formulation had been done during the previous sitting. Result: response in 9 days, which is within the range that a reminder email would have produced anyway. Recorded as indistinguishable from the ordinary route.

**Intention two, a question asked.** A collaborator who had gone quiet. Accelerated: 3 minutes, 7 to charge, and a change worth noting, which is that the formulation was written as a yes-or-no question with a date, rather than the drifting open-ended version the first attempt had used. Result: reply in 5 days. The sigil is not the plausible mechanism. The sharper question is.

**Intention three, the one that looked best.** A question about a payment, built with the intention of a specific sum. Three-layer stack, 11 minutes total. Result: nothing at 14 or 30 days, and on inspection the statement had been written as a wish for an amount rather than as a request to a named party, which is the two-word difference the worksheet exists to catch. The acceleration did not fail. The formulation had been skipped and the speed concealed the skip.

Across the run, the accelerated build was faster every time and the outcomes were unremarkable except where the formulation had been improved. The acceleration is real and it is not the interesting variable.

Five ways accelerated work goes wrong

**Formulation compressed into building.** The dominant failure. An intention gets four minutes instead of four days, the reduction faithfully encodes a wish that was never examined, and the working is uninterpretable because there was no decision to check against. This looks like a failure of the technique and is a failure of the worksheet.

**The verification interval quietly removed.** The working is built and charged, and the check happens whenever it feels like checking. Because the interval is the measurement, this converts an experiment into a mood, and the practice starts confirming whatever you want. If you accelerate anything, do not accelerate this.

**Stacking to dilute the failure rate.** Multiple sigils for one intention is genuinely useful as a single composite act and genuinely useless as an attempt to run the experiment several times. Four attempts at one question with the same wording in the same state is one attempt with four chances to reinterpret.

**Speed as a proxy for intensity.** There is a felt sense in compression, and intensity is easy to mistake for accuracy. The fastest builds in the run above were also the emptiest. Intensity and information are different quantities, and the sigil rewards information.

**Accumulation at speed.** A faster build makes a much larger number of workings per month, and a practice of many unreviewed workings is worse than a practice of few. If acceleration increases your output, increase your review rate at least as much, or the acceleration is just a faster route to a bigger drawer.

What the tradition is actually optimising, in one line

Strip the acceleration language and the narrow claim is: decide clearly, then act without the delay that deliberation adds, then check on a date you wrote down earlier. The first half is a discipline of formulation, the second is a discipline of commitment, the third is a discipline of honesty, and only the second is what anyone means by speed.

That version is entirely uncontroversial, entirely achievable this afternoon, and gives up nothing that the dramatic version actually delivers. The dramatic version promises that the interval between charge and result can be compressed, and the only way to test that promise is to keep the interval, which is a strange thing to offer and an honest one.

The ethics, which acceleration does not change

Compressing your own preparation has no ethical dimension. Compressing the interval before a check does, because it removes the only protection you had against marking your own homework. The two are often sold together as one technique and they are not one technique.

On targets, the usual limits hold at any speed. A working on another person's decision, without their consent and unknown to them, is a working on a person rather than on a situation, and the compactness of the mark does not change what it is pointed at. The single useful test remains whether you would describe the working to the person it affects, and speed is irrelevant to the answer.

On money, the accelerated version of a financial sigil is the most common place for a bad formulation to hide, because haste and vagueness are the same sensation. A statement written in four minutes about a sum is a wish. A statement written with a named party, a figure, an invoice number and a date is a request, and only the second one can be reviewed. The acceleration is not what makes financial workings go wrong. The skipped worksheet does, and the acceleration is the reason nobody notices.

What to build if you want the speed honestly

Formulate first, in a separate sitting, and write the sentence you will reduce. If the sentence is not there, no amount of speed helps and the fastest you can go is zero.

Build in one pass. Do not redraw because the first attempt was not graceful. The aesthetic is not the mechanism and an unfinished mark charged late is a worse working than a plain one charged on time.

Charge once, for as long as you can actually hold attention, which for most people is under twenty minutes. Beyond that you are going through motions, and a short honest charge is better than a long performative one.

Release it, and write the review date and the acceptable conclusion before you release it, not after. This is the step that makes the working knowable, and it takes thirty seconds.

Keep the build time in the log. Not because the number is meaningful but because watching it drop is a good way to notice when it drops because you started skipping the formulation.

Three objections worth answering properly

**If speed does not matter, why did Spare insist on it?** Because for a person doing daily work, the friction of a long preparation is the thing that stops them doing it at all. His acceleration is a practice-design argument aimed at the practitioner rather than a physics claim, and read that way it is a good one. What it does not do is license compressing the check.

**Some people do report fast results.** They do, and there are at least three explanations that are not contradictory: the working was about a thing that was going to happen anyway and the timing was the person's own action, the preparation before the sigil did the work and the sigil was the marker, and some small number of cases involve the result being the kind of thing people are good at getting when they decide. None of those is refuted by a long interval, because in all three the interval is not doing the work. The interval is doing the work in the cases you would want to learn from.

**Isn't demanding a review date just rationalisation?** A review date is the only commitment you can make in advance that constrains the later interpretation, and the reason later interpretation is the problem is that it is where hope does its work. Spare, who is not a sentimental writer, was insistent on the check, and the reason he could be is that he had watched enthusiasm fill the space where a record should have been.

Frequently Asked Questions

How fast can a sigil actually manifest?

The build can be compressed to a few minutes and the charge to a few more, and the honest answer to how fast the result arrives is that it depends entirely on what you built a working about, not on how quickly you built it. Results that look instant are most often results about things already in motion that the preparation moved you to act on. Keep the verification interval whatever the preparation speed, because that interval is the only thing making the result knowable rather than merely felt.

What is Spare's hyper-acceleration technique?

It is the claim that a sigil prepared and released in a very short time holds the intention better than one prepared over days, because most of the delay in a slow preparation is deliberation that dilutes the original decision. Read that way it is a practice-design argument about the state of the person releasing, and it makes a checkable prediction: the accelerated working should be released in a state closer to the original decision. It says nothing about the universe responding faster, and the popular version that claims the latter is an extension nobody in the source material makes.

Is it better to build several sigils for the same intention?

For one composite working in a single sitting, yes, and this is genuinely in the sources as a layered technique where each key does a different job. For several independent attempts at the same question, no, because they are not independent: same wording, same state, same day, and four results you will be tempted to count selectively. One working with one review date produces knowledge. Four workings on one intention produce a story, and the story will be assembled after the fact from whichever layer moved.

Should I keep the sigil visible while I wait?

Keep it or destroy it, but decide before you charge rather than after, and do not check on it daily. Its physical presence is not supposed to be doing anything, and treating a visible mark as a progress indicator is a way of manufacturing a daily signal that means nothing. If you want a daily practice during the interval, a scored run of something genuinely unpredictable serves you better, because it at least produces a real number on the day.

Why did my fast sigil do nothing when slower ones worked?

The usual cause is that the speed went into the build and the formulation got skipped, so what you encoded was a wish rather than a decision. The tell is that you cannot state the observable in one sentence, or that you cannot name the date you will check on. A second cause is stacking: several near-identical marks in one sitting dilute rather than emphasise. A third is that the outcome was never in your control, in which case no speed would have helped and the working was a request about somebody else's decision.
