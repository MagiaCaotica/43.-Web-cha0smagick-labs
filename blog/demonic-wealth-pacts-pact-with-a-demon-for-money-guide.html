Demonic Wealth Pacts

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

A search for a pact with a demon for money usually starts from a simple hope: that wealth can be negotiated for, the way a favour might be arranged with someone powerful. The sources do not say that. What they describe is access, luck, discovery and the clearing of obstacles, which are considerably smaller promises than the ones the modern internet attaches to them. This guide starts from the grimoire text rather than from the marketing, and follows the money claim back to where it actually comes from. You will see which spirits the older material connects to riches, why ceremonial tradition put wealth pacts under planetary spirits instead of infernal ones, and what a pact is described as asking of the person who signs it. The hazard worth naming first is not supernatural. Framing your own ambition as an external bargain can hand your impulses to a fiction, and a fiction with a price attached is harder to walk away from than a decision you made yourself on an ordinary Tuesday.

What a money pact is actually asking you to sign

A pact in the technical sense is a negotiated exchange. You offer something, a spirit is described as offering something in return, and both sides are bound by terms that were stated in advance. That structure is what separates a pact from a prayer, and it is also what makes it hazardous, because a contract with a defined obligation is harder to walk away from than a wish.

The trouble is that almost nobody who searches for a money pact is looking for a contract. They are looking for a shortcut, and the ritual vocabulary is being used to make the shortcut feel like a transaction rather than a fantasy. Once the framing is accepted, the difficult part becomes explaining to yourself why you are not yet meeting the terms.

Written out plainly, a defensible pact says four things: what you are giving, what you are asking for, by when you will report back, and what you will do if nothing arrives. A version that omits the fourth item is not a pact. It is a donation with a supernatural receipt.

Where the goetic wealth material came from

The Ars Goetia names seventy-two spirits in three ranks, and it was first printed in the seventeenth century out of older Jewish and Christian demonological material. The text itself is late. Its sources are not, and the picture it presents is administrative rather than monstrous: a spirit of a particular rank governs a particular domain and answers to a superior.

The association of individual spirits with riches does exist in the older layers, chiefly through figures such as Clauneck, a duke described as granting wealth and the arts, and Mammon, a king tied to coin. What the historical record does not contain is a modern finding: a systematic fusion of goetic spirits with wealth ritual as a genre. Ceremonial magic put money work under planetary spirits.

So the practice most people encounter under the name of a demon money pact is largely a twentieth and twenty-first-century internet synthesis, assembled from a demonology that had nothing to do with money and an astrology that had everything to do with it. That does not make it worthless. It does mean that when a claim is traced back, it stops earlier than the marketing implies.

  • The Ars Goetia is a seventeenth-century printed grimoire drawing on Jewish and Christian demonology.
  • Clauneck is a duke associated with riches, arts and sciences in the older material.
  • Mammon is a king associated with coin, and his modern cult is largely a twentieth-century growth.
  • Planetary spirits, not infernal ones, carried wealth work in the ceremonial tradition.

The two spirits the sources name, and what they claim

Clauneck and Mammon are the pair that recur. Clauneck is described as a duke who can grant riches, arts and sciences, and who is said to discover hidden things and provide a flow of money already in circulation. Mammon is described as a king of the west associated with coin, and the ambition offered in his name is generally milder: discovery, favour, the removal of a blockage.

Neither description mentions creating money where none existed. Both point instead at circulation, access and visibility, which are the terms an honest reading should use. A spirit of discovery is a different proposition from a spirit of creation, and the gap between them is where most disappointment in this material lives.

  1. Clauneck: riches, arts and sciences, discovery of hidden things, money already moving.
  2. Mammon: coin, favour, the lifting of an obstacle, the appearance of opportunity.

Why no source promises money out of nothing

No historical tradition describes a pact that produces wealth ex nihilo. Read the grimoire generously and you get access, luck, discovery and the removal of obstacles. Read it narrowly and you get a description of a favourable wind. Neither reading supports a promise of a specific sum, and any text that guarantees one is modern invention wearing older clothes.

This matters for more than accuracy. A promise of a specific sum creates a deadline and a shortfall, and a shortfall experienced as a broken contract is far more disturbing than a wish that quietly did nothing. The sources are kinder than the promotional versions, because they never commit you to a number.

The hazard that is psychological, not supernatural

The documented risk in this material is not demonic possession. It is the way a bargain can externalise your own wanting. Once an impulse is described as something you obtained from outside, it stops feeling like a choice, and choices are the only thing you can revise. People enter unplanned debt with great enthusiasm when the spending has been assigned to a spirit's instruction rather than to their own preference.

The second documented risk is social. A pact kept in secret, especially one involving money or sacrifice, tends to shrink the life around it. The practice becomes the relationship. Anyone who asks what you are doing is a distraction from the commitment, and the commitment is the only thing left.

Neither of these is exotic. They are the ordinary risks of any binding personal obsession, given a supernatural vocabulary and a set of terms. Naming them costs nothing and takes most of the danger out of the practice.

What a defensible pact looks like on paper

Write it as a document rather than an invocation. Four headings are enough, and the fourth is the one that changes behaviour. If you cannot state what you will do when nothing happens, you have written a wish and mislabelled it.

Keep the page. A pact you cannot show anyone is a private obligation, and private obligations are where the failure modes live. Showing it to one other person costs nothing and removes most of the pressure that produces escalation.

  1. What I give: a named amount or a named habit, stated as a figure rather than a feeling.
  2. What I ask for: access, discovery or the removal of one specific obstacle.
  3. When I report back: a date, not a season.
  4. What I do if nothing arrives: the correction I have already agreed to in advance.

Choosing a spirit by stated domain

Choose by what the text claims, not by reputation. Reputation in this corpus was manufactured in the last twenty years by search results, and the reputations are frequently attached to the wrong rank. The Ars Goetia gives every spirit a rank and a domain, and those two fields are the whole of the historical claim.

If your problem is that you cannot see an opportunity, a discovery framing is a better fit than a wealth framing. If your problem is that money moves badly once it arrives, a circulation framing fits. If your problem is that you want a specific number, no spirit in the text is a fit and the honest response is to stop looking.

How to tell a warning sign from a fair price

A price rises in one direction only. If the amount you give increases over time, if the giving moves from money to something you cannot replace, or if the spirit begins to specify what you should give rather than what you have offered, the arrangement has changed category. It is no longer a bargain and it should be ended.

The traditional forms were modest and legible: a tithe, a percentage, a fixed offering of the same kind each time. The point of a fixed offering is that it can be afforded from ordinary income. When a practice cannot be funded from ordinary income, that is the finding, not a reason to dig deeper.

  • The amount given grows between cycles, or the timing becomes urgent.
  • The request moves from money to blood, health, relationships or silence.
  • You begin hiding the practice from people who would ask sensible questions about it.
  • Stopping begins to feel like a penalty rather than a decision.

Writing the record that keeps the pact honest

Keep a short dated log. One line per cycle: what you gave, what you asked for, what changed in the world, and what you did next. Four columns, no interpretation. The value of the log is not that it proves anything. It is that it converts memory, which is generous to itself, into a record, which is not.

Review at the date you set, not at the end of the cycle when you feel committed. The question at review is narrow: did the agreed exchange happen, in both directions, on the terms written down. If the answer is no twice running, the correct response is to close the file rather than to renegotiate harder.

Where the offering tradition actually comes from

Traditional forms were a tithe, a percentage, or a fixed item given the same way at each cycle. None of them was open-ended, and the reason is a matter of design rather than moral. A bounded offering can be completed, and a completed obligation is what makes the arrangement feel finished rather than permanent.

An unbounded offering is a different instrument entirely. It asks for a proportion of something you have not yet acquired, which means the debt grows as your situation improves, and the arrangement becomes most expensive at exactly the moment it appears to be working. That inversion is the clearest sign that the terms have slipped away from anything the older sources describe.

The practical test is affordability under a bad month, not under a good one. If the offering survives a month when income drops, the terms are sustainable. If it only survives months that are already going well, the practice has attached itself to your circumstances in a way no historical source would recognise.

  • A fixed sum or a fixed habit, the same at every cycle, affordable in a bad month.
  • A percentage of income, which caps naturally when income falls.
  • An unbounded share of future gains, which inverts cost against reward and should be refused.

The difference between access and creation

Access means a door that was already there, and the sources describe access, luck, discovery and the removal of obstacles. Creation means a new resource appearing where none existed, and no spirit in the older material is described in those terms. The distinction is the whole of the honest claim, and it is one sentence long.

In practice the confusion is what generates the most alarming experiences. Someone asks for creation, receives something that looks like access, and concludes either that the arrangement failed or that it succeeded in a way they did not authorise. Both conclusions come from the same muddle. Ask for access and the outcome is readable; ask for creation and it is not.

Restating the request is a legitimate part of any cycle, and doing it does not break the arrangement. What breaks it is quietly expanding the terms after the fact, which converts a closed question into an open one, and an open question never returns a verdict.

Questions to ask before you sign anything

Four questions, asked in order, and an arrangement that cannot answer all four cleanly is one to decline. Where in the source does this claim appear. What exactly is being given, as a figure. What is the date at which this stops. Who can see the page.

The last question catches most arrangements, and it catches them on purely practical grounds, with no spirit involved at any point. A page you would not show anyone is a private commitment, and private commitments are the documented site of the escalation and isolation problems in this material. If showing it is unthinkable, the terms are wrong rather than your judgement.

Add a fifth if the request involves your work, your housing or your health. Those three attract the most elaborate framing in this genre, and they are the three where a real cost is most likely to be mistaken for a fair price.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a demonic wealth pact dangerous?

The documented risk is psychological and social rather than supernatural. A pact framed as an external bargain can externalise your own impulses, which is how people arrive at unplanned debt with a clear conscience, and a secret financial obligation tends to shrink the life around it. Write the terms down, name a review date, and let one other person see the page.

Which spirit is associated with wealth?

Clauneck, a duke, is described as granting riches along with arts and sciences, and as discovering hidden things. Mammon, a king, is tied to coin, favour and the removal of an obstacle. Read the domains in the Ars Goetia rather than the modern reputation, because the reputation is recent and the domain is four hundred years old.

What does a pact actually ask of me?

A genuine pact names what you give, what you ask for, when you report back and what you will do if nothing arrives. Traditional forms were a tithe, a percentage or a fixed offering that ordinary income could cover. Any arrangement where the demanded payment escalates has stopped being a price and become a warning.

Can a spirit really hand me money?

No source supports that reading. The older material describes access, luck, discovery and the removal of obstacles, which are all plausible descriptions of a favourable wind and none of which is a guarantee of a sum. A specific number is the one promise that cannot be traced to any historical tradition, and it is the one most often made.

Is there software that makes tracking this less tedious?

The method is free and the sources are public. What a paid purchase tends to buy is the bookkeeping: dated logs across many cycles, the terms of each pact kept in one place, and reminders that a review date is due. If your problem is that you keep forgetting what you agreed to, that bookkeeping is worth paying for. If your problem is that you want a guarantee, nothing will sell you one.
