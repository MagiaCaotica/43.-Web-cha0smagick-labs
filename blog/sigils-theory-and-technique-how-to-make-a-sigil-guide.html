Sigils: Theory and Technique

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

A sigil is a compressed instruction. That is the whole idea, and everything else about sigils is a consequence of it. You take a statement of intent, remove the redundancy, reduce what remains to a small set of marks, and charge the result. The compression is not decoration and the glyph is not a picture of your intention. It is an encoding of it, closer to a checksum than to a logo, and the skill lies in doing the compression honestly rather than decorating the result. This is a first route in for someone who has heard the word and not yet made one, and it covers what a sigil is, where the method comes from, how to charge one, and what to do afterwards.

What a sigil does that a sentence does not

A sentence says everything about your intention, including the hedges, the qualifications and the part you added to sound reasonable. A sigil contains only the load-bearing part, because you had to decide which part that was. The act of reduction is therefore an act of deciding, and it is much harder to do honestly than most people expect. A wish that survives being compressed into four marks is a different and better-specified wish than the sentence it came from.

There is a second function, which is that a glyph is small enough to carry and hide. You can put one on a piece of paper, in a locket, in a tattoo, in a phone case, in a signature. It does not announce itself. That is part of why the form has survived, and it is also part of why sigils get associated with secrecy in a way that the method does not actually require.

Reduction, and why it is not decoration

Reduction means removing everything that does not change what you are asking for. In I am going to try to become a more confident person in public, the words I am, going to try and a are doing no work. What is left, somewhere behind being confident in public, is the actual instruction. The reduction process is a series of decisions about what is load bearing, and each one can be argued with, which is the point.

What people do instead is skip the reduction and go straight to a pretty shape, usually one generated by an app or copied from a diagram, and then charge it. This produces a working with no content. The glyph will be attractive and it will be nobody's intention. The test is simple: look at the finished mark and say what it means. If you cannot, the reduction did not happen, however pleasant the result looks.

Where the method comes from

The clearest early statement of the technique is in Austin Osman Spare's Book of Gates, published in 1904, which reduces a statement to a glyph and then treats the glyph as the carrier of the intent. Spare was working independently and slightly before most of the ceremonial magic that followed, and his account is compact. Everything that came later in the twentieth century, across several different traditions, is recognisably the same operation with different notations.

The visual language that surrounds it is older and less consistent. Circles for unity, lines for direction, crosses at the centre, the square and the triangle: these appear throughout grimoire material from several centuries and several cultures, and their meanings differ from source to source. Treating any one grimoire's correspondence as canonical is a mistake, because the correspondences are not consistent enough to be canonical. The reduction is the method. The symbol vocabulary is decoration with a history.

Two stages, and why keeping them apart matters

Most of the traditional instructions make this separation, and the reason is practical rather than mystical. The encoding stage is analysis and the activation stage is commitment. Doing them in one motion means the desire to get it right is still running while you are trying to act, and the result is a glyph you keep revising in your head for a week and never charge.

The check step is the one people skip, and it is the one that catches the most. Write down the intent. Produce the glyph. Come back an hour later, look at both, and confirm they correspond. If they do not, the reduction was wrong, and you have found out in an hour rather than after a month of a working aimed at the wrong thing.

  1. Encoding: write the intent, reduce it, produce the glyph, and stop.
  2. Check: read the glyph back and confirm it means the thing you meant.
  3. Activation: charge it in one short deliberate sitting.
  4. Dismissal: a small deliberate act that ends the working.
  5. Forgetting: put it somewhere you will not see it again.

Drawing badly and drawing well

You do not need to be able to draw. A sigil is a diagram, not a picture, and diagrams reward clarity rather than skill. Straight lines, circles and a few crossing strokes are enough. What matters is that the mark is deliberate, consistent and legible to you when you look at it in six months.

Generated sigils are a reasonable option and there is nothing wrong with using one. The failure mode is using a generator on a wish you have not properly stated, which gives you a random mark with a random intent attached and no way to check the correspondence later. The good version generates from a statement you have already reduced yourself, in which case the tool is doing the drawing while you do the thinking, which is the correct division of labour.

A first sigil, made slowly

Write the intent as a single positive statement in the present tense. Positive because a negated instruction is difficult to encode, since there is no mark for not. Present tense because the encoding is easier and because it is arguably more honest about what you are attempting. Then cross out every word that does not change what you are asking for.

Reduce what remains by taking first letters, or first and last letters, or by pulling the skeleton out of each word. All three are traditional and none is more correct than the others. Then simplify the letters into strokes, and add a shape if the reduction has left you with something unreadable. Do not add the shape first. The order matters, because adding a shape before reducing is how you get an attractive glyph with no instruction inside it.

What a derivative is for

A derivative is a copy of a sigil made in advance of a working rather than for it. It is used when you want a sigil that works for a recurring situation rather than a one-off, such as a standard approach to a difficult conversation, a standard way of beginning any working, or a standard marking for a place you need to keep an eye on.

The value is that it can be charged repeatedly without re-deriving anything, which is a real saving when the same aim recurs weekly. The cost is that it is fixed, and a fixed sigil cannot respond to a situation that turns out to need something slightly different. Derivatives work best for aims that genuinely repeat and worst for aims that are similar but not identical, which is the harder distinction to make in advance.

Charging, in enough detail to repeat

Choose a fixed length in advance. Forty seconds, ninety seconds, three minutes, a set number of breaths. Fixed matters more than the specific value, because a duration decided beforehand gives you something to count and a duration negotiated in the moment does not. Dim the lights or not, play music or not, it makes less difference than people expect and matters more that you chose it in advance.

During the charge, hold the meaning rather than the shape. The glyph is a handle for the instruction and your attention should be on the instruction, since that is what you are encoding and the mark is only standing in for it. Where you are looking, whether your eyes are open, whether you say the intent aloud, all of these are conventions. None of them is the mechanism and treating any one of them as the mechanism is how a working becomes a set of arbitrary requirements.

Forgetting, which everyone skips

A dismissal is a small deliberate act that ends the working, and putting the sigil somewhere you will not see it is the standard way to do one. It is not superstition in the sense of being a false belief, it is basic hygiene. A charge left open on an object you handle daily is a standing invitation to keep attending to it, and most people find that their attention does exactly that whether or not they intended it.

The practical method is dull and effective. Seal it in an envelope, or put it in a drawer you do not open, or photograph it and delete the object. The point is only that the working has an end, written into your behaviour, rather than an end you hope happens on its own. A great many unfinished workings are simply charges that were never closed, and the person is not in a state you would call haunted, they are just tired.

When a sigil produces nothing

Nothing visible happened. This is the most common outcome and it is not evidence of anything, in either direction. A working is an action with an uncertain payoff, and most actions with uncertain payoffs produce nothing visible on a timescale of days. The mistake is to read the null as a verdict on the sigil rather than as one sample.

The useful response is to check the specification before touching the method. Was the intent specific enough that success or failure could have been recognised? Was the duration long enough to matter? Was there anything else different that week? Was the aim something within your own influence at all? A sigil for a change in the world is a different proposition from a sigil for a change in your own attention, and the second one is the one you can evaluate.

A month of practice

The month is structured so that the last week depends on the first three, and that is the point. The record is what turns a month of sigils into something you have learned from, and reading twenty-eight lines in order at the end is more informative than any single working could have been. Most people discover at that point that their aims cluster, that some lengths of time suit them better than others, and that roughly a third of what they made was not worth making.

That last finding is the one worth taking seriously. A month of small workings is a cheap way to find out where your actual attention is, and it does so through your own record rather than through anyone's opinion. The people who are still making sigils years later usually got there by cutting most of them, not by adding more.

  1. Week one, one sigil a day, small aims, no charge longer than a minute.
  2. Week two, the same, keeping a one-line note of each intent.
  3. Week three, add the check step and the written dismissal, and drop one sigil that produced nothing.
  4. Week four, build one derivative for an aim that genuinely recurs.
  5. At the end, read the twenty-eight lines and decide which three you would repeat.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I charge a sigil?

Pick a fixed duration before you start, hold the meaning rather than the shape, and end it with a deliberate act that puts the object away. Forty seconds to three minutes is a sensible range and the specific number matters less than having decided it in advance. What matters more is that the charge has a beginning and an end in your behaviour, since a charge left open is an open loop that will occupy more attention than you intended.

Do I need to be able to draw?

No. A sigil is a diagram rather than a picture, so straight lines, circles and a few crossing strokes are entirely adequate. If you would rather not draw at all, a generator is fine provided you have already reduced the intent yourself, since the tool is good at drawing and poor at deciding. Clarity of the mark matters, because you will need to read it back in six months and recognise what it was for.

What is a derivative?

A copy of a sigil made in advance and used more than once, usually for an aim that genuinely repeats. It saves redoing the reduction each time and it is the right form for a standard beginning to a working, a standard approach to a recurring conversation, or a standard marking for a place. The limitation is that it is fixed, so it suits aims that repeat exactly rather than aims that are merely similar.

Is there anything a purchased version of a sigil tool does better?

The drawing and nothing else, unless it also keeps a record. Generating a glyph from a statement you have already reduced yourself is a real convenience and costs very little, since the thinking is the part that matters. A tool that stores each sigil with its date, its intent and the outcome you recorded is worth more, because that is the bookkeeping that makes a practice compound instead of accumulating. Anything that generates a sigil from an unreduced wish is doing the part you need to do yourself.

How many sigils should one person make?

Fewer than you expect, and the constraint is not effort but attention. Twenty-eight small aims in a month is plenty, because the point of the exercise is to find out which aims your own attention actually goes to, and that question is answered faster than people think. People who accumulate hundreds tend to be avoiding the review step, since a large collection makes it easy to believe the practice is working and hard to check. The record matters more than the count.
