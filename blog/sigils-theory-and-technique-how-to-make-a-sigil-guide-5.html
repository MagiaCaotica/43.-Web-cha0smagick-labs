Sigils: Theory and Technique

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

A sigil is a compressed glyph, and the compression is the whole technique. You start with a written statement of intent, reduce it to a minimal figure, and then charge that figure with the intention you originally wrote down. The first stage is a letter puzzle and can be learned in an afternoon. The second stage is the part people describe in spiritual language, and it is much closer to a discipline of attention than to anything else. This article covers the reduction in order, the recurring geometry, why the two stages should not be fused, and the honest limits on what any of it accomplishes, including a clear account of what an automated generator handles for you and what it cannot.

Where the letter reduction comes from

The technique is documented in Austin Osman Spare's A Book of Gates, published in 1904, which shows him reducing written statements into glyphs by hand. Spare predates every esoteric group that uses the method, and the derivation is documented rather than inferred, which is unusual in this field and worth noting when claims about who invented sigil work come up.

Later practitioners added structure rather than invention. The circle that encloses the figure, the line through its centre, the division of the reduced letters into quadrants, and the removal of duplicate shapes all come from the grimoire tradition's general visual language. Different sources disagree about what the circle means. Everyone agrees it is there.

The geometry that keeps recurring

Across sources with no common ancestry, the same elements appear. A circle for unity or containment. A line for the element of intent. A central cross or division into four quadrants. That the pattern is stable suggests it derives from a shared graphic convention rather than from copying between modern practitioners, though the two are hard to separate at this distance.

The meanings attached to these elements vary more than the elements themselves. A circle is unity in one source and boundary in another. If you find a system assigning precise numerological significance to each stroke, be aware that the assignments are the least consistent part of the tradition and the easiest to generate retrospectively. The shapes are traditional. The glosses are modern.

Encoding and activation are different jobs

The two-stage method, reduce the statement first and charge the glyph afterwards, is the structural idea that makes sigil work workable. It lets you build the artefact on a calm day with nothing at stake, then charge it deliberately, and it lets you revise the wording before any intention has been invested. Fusing the stages produces rushed sigils with vague statements attached, which is the most common failure among beginners.

There is also a practical reason to separate them. Encoding requires care and attention to shape. Charging requires a particular state and a willingness to sit with the result. Trying to do both at once means the aesthetic compromises win, because nobody draws well while trying to hold a raised state. Draw badly, charge well, and let the ugliness stop mattering.

Writing the statement before drawing anything

Write the intent as one sentence with a subject and an outcome, and keep the original paper. The sentence is the sigil's specification, and it is also the only record you will have of what the figure was supposed to do. Six months later the glyph means nothing without it, because a reduced figure carries no legible content for anyone who did not make it.

Watch the wording for requests rather than descriptions. Wanting a job is a description of a gap. Being the kind of person who has work to do is closer to a description of a capability, and it sigilises more cleanly because it is shorter and less possessive. Most weak sigils come from statements that want something rather than from statements that were poorly reduced.

Vowel removal, and what it does

The first reduction step removes the vowels, on the usual reasoning that vowels carry no consonant articulation and therefore contribute nothing to a shape. This is the step everyone does, and it is also the step with the least justification behind it. Whatever the rationale, the practical effect is that the figure is made only of consonants, which is what you were going to do anyway.

Removing vowels also shortens most statements by roughly a third, which is a real benefit since long sigils are hard to hold and hard to draw. Keep a consistent rule and apply it every time. Practitioners who keep the vowels sometimes report better results, which is unresolvable from the outside, and switching conventions per sigil is what makes a collection unreadable.

Shape assignment, and why the alphabet is yours

Remaining letters get assigned shapes. Some systems use a fixed table where a is a circle and m is a set of peaks. Others assign shapes on the fly by judging the letter's skeleton, so a looks round and k looks angular. Both work. The fixed table produces consistent results across a collection. The judged version produces figures that resemble the words they came from.

The judged version is easier to learn and produces results you find more satisfying, because you can see the statement inside the shape. The fixed table is easier to check, since anyone can reverse it. Neither is correct. Pick one, stay with it for a season, and do not spend the months you have available debating the choice with people who have already chosen.

Crossing out repeats, and the order you do it in

Duplicate shapes are removed because a repeated letter does not add meaning to a compressed figure. Removing the first occurrence and keeping the last is the usual convention. Keeping the first is also used. The choice changes the figure slightly and carries no significance that anyone has demonstrated.

Where it does matter is consistency and legibility. Remove duplicates before you draw rather than after, so you know how many distinct shapes you are working with. Four or five is a comfortable target for a short statement. Ten or more produces a figure you will not recognise on sight, which defeats the purpose of having a glyph at all.

What a generator can and cannot do

A digital sigil generator is an algorithmic compression of a statement of intent into a minimal glyph, which is a straightforward application of the same reduction rules. For producing consistent geometry, for batch work, and for comparing your own hand-drawn results against a reference, they are useful and fast. The reduction they perform is exactly the reduction described above.

What they cannot do is the part that is not reduction. Generators do not produce a statement, and the statement is where the intent, the wording, and the reviewable specification live. People who use one and skip the sentence end up with attractive glyphs for intentions they have not actually formulated, which is a well-worn route to a collection of pretty objects and no results.

Charging without begging

Charging means focusing on the outcome as though it has already happened, which is different from wanting it, wanting it, or asking for it. The distinction is subtle in words and enormous in practice. Wanting generates effort and effort generates the sense of not having it, and the working stalls in a loop of striving.

The techniques people use to get there are all ways of changing state rather than ways of feeling more. Breath discipline, a raised state from earlier in the day, exhaustion, repetitive sound, or simply reading the statement and imagining the scene after. Ten minutes is a common figure and a generous one. If you have spent forty minutes straining, something has gone wrong earlier in the procedure, almost always the statement.

Where the sigil lives afterwards

Common practice is to destroy or bury the charged figure, on the reasoning that the charge is complete and the artefact has served. There is no strong argument that destruction is necessary. There is a strong practical argument against keeping everything: a drawer of several hundred sigils becomes a drawer you avoid looking in, and the record you would want to review disappears with the paper.

The middle path is to keep a numbered list. One line per sigil, with the date, the statement in full, and the date you will check it. That list is more valuable than any individual glyph, and it is the thing that lets you learn anything at all from practice. Discard the figures, keep the log, and the practice becomes measurable.

Testing a sigil honestly

Fix a check date before you charge anything, write the outcome you will accept, and write down what partial success would look like. Then report what happened without editing. This is the whole discipline and it is the step that makes sigil work different from prayer, because it produces a record that can disagree with you.

Null results are common and they are information. A sigil for something you do not care about, charged badly, checked badly, produces nothing, and that is the expected distribution. The reason this is worth doing anyway is that after twenty or thirty logged sigils you can finally look at your own hit rate and your own preparation, and you will know whether the technique suits you or whether you have been generating reassuring noise for months.

A one-week routine

Pick five small requests you would not mind failing at. For each: write the statement, reduce it, draw it, charge it, log the date and the outcome you will accept. Five sigils in a week is more than enough to learn the reduction and enough to see whether anything happens. Keep the statements short and specific, and resist adding a second technique in the same week, since you will not know which one did anything.

At the end of the week, read the log rather than the sigils. Note how many produced the outcome you described, how many produced something adjacent, and how many produced nothing. That count is the first real measurement of your own practice, and it takes an hour and costs nothing, which is a better return than almost anything else available at this stage.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is letter reduction in sigil magic?

It is the step of compressing a written statement into a minimal glyph by removing vowels, assigning shapes to the remaining consonants, and dropping duplicate shapes. Documentation of the method sits in Austin Osman Spare's 1904 work. Its practical purpose is to produce a figure compact enough to hold in mind, not to encode a meaning hidden in the letters.

Do sigils work if they are generated digitally?

The reduction a generator performs is the same reduction you would do by hand, so the compression step is equivalent. What a generator cannot supply is the written statement, which is where the intent actually lives. People who use generators without writing a sentence tend to accumulate attractive glyphs for intentions they never formulated.

How long should charging a sigil take?

Ten minutes is the common figure and a generous one. The state matters more than the duration, and straining for forty minutes is a sign that the statement was weak or the technique is wrong, not that you need more time. Methods that change your state, such as breath discipline or exhaustion, work better than methods that try to will the outcome into existence.

Can one sigil be reused for a different intention?

No, because the glyph is derived from the statement and carries no other content. Reusing it asks a handle to do work it was never shaped for. If you find yourself wanting to repoint a sigil, that is a sign the statement was too vague to begin with, and the better move is to write a new one from a sharper sentence rather than to overload an existing figure.

Is a sigil different from a servitor?

A sigil is a compressed glyph and by itself carries no task. A servitor is a small construct given a specific function, usually by charging a sigil with that function and then retiring it after a set period. Many practices pair the two, but the distinction is worth keeping: the sigil is the encoding, the servitor is the assignment, and only the first is really a technique in its own right.
