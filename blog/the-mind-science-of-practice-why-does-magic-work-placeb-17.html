The Mind Science of Practice

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Almost every conversation about whether practice changes anything welds four separate findings into one claim, and the weld is where the argument becomes impossible to finish. Three of the four are about the practitioner. One of them is about a pack of cards. The one about your body is small, repeatable, and yours to measure. The one about expectancy is real, modest, and about reporting rather than about the world. The one about the deck is arithmetic with no psychology in it at all. And the one about how perception is organised is a model that will accommodate whatever you already believe, which is exactly why it does no work in the argument. Run in the right order, the four tell a coherent story about a person doing something to themselves and nothing about anything reaching back. Run in the usual order, beginning with the framework, they appear to describe a mechanism that touches the world. The order matters more than the content here, because each finding changes what the next one is allowed to mean. Below is that order, set out as a sequence you could hand to a stranger and have them follow without knowing anything about the subject.

Four claims that are usually welded into one

The weld is easy to spot once you look for it. Someone will say that ritual works because the brain builds models, that the brain builds models because the body settles, that the body settles because the deck was read correctly, and that the deck confirms the whole arrangement. Four steps, four kinds of claim, one sentence, and the sentence is nonsense because the third step is arithmetic and the fourth is a coincidence being asked to do structural work.

Separating them is not pedantry. Each has a different evidential status, a different size, a different subject, and a different way of being wrong. A body that settles after a fixed sequence is a small physiological fact about one person. A card turned face up is an event with a known rate and no interpretation attached. Keeping the four apart is what lets you say something true about all four at once.

Which of them is about your body

One. A structured sequence of known shape produces a change in breathing and heart rhythm within minutes, and the change is large enough to feel without equipment. That is a fact about a person doing something to themselves, it is repeatable, and it is the best-evidenced item in the entire field of questions about this.

It is also the smallest claim in the set, which is why it is the one that gets dropped. Almost nobody wants the finding that a fixed breathing pattern changes your autonomic state, because it does not reach outward. But it is the one that survives scrutiny without qualification, and if you build a practice on it you are building on the only part of the four that nobody has successfully disputed.

Which of them is about a deck of cards

One, and it is not about you at all. Five symbols, one turned face up, a guess, the card returned to a shuffled pack. The rate that follows from the design is one in five, and it follows from the design regardless of who is guessing or how hard. Nothing in that calculation involves attention, intention, or a person.

The value of this item is that it is a floor. If somebody claims to be doing better than chance at guessing, one in five is the number their result has to beat before the beating means anything, and the arithmetic of how many guesses it takes to establish a beat is unforgiving at small samples. Used properly, the deck is not evidence for anything. It is the measuring stick that tells you the size of the interval you are trying to move.

The baseline, taken before anything happens

Before you test expectancy, take your own baseline, because expectancy claims are only interpretable relative to where you started. A wrist sensor, the same wrist, the same hour, morning and evening, for a fortnight, with a note on how you slept. That is the baseline and it is unglamorous.

Most people who skip this step end up comparing a good week to a bad one and attributing the difference to their practice. The fortnight of matched readings costs nothing and it converts a vague sense of improvement into a number with a direction, which may be a real direction or may be nothing, and you will not know which until you have it.

The deck, and the number that cannot move

Now run the deck, a hundred calls, and tally before you start. The point is not to score high. The point is to find out what your own attention is doing at the mechanical level, because a hundred calls is where the interesting part turns up: almost everyone becomes steadier at the counting and sloppier at the attention, and the two trends run in opposite directions.

That observation is worth more than the tally. It is a genuine piece of evidence about the limits of your own practice, obtained for the cost of an afternoon, and it bears directly on the largest class of claims in the genre. Whatever else you are training with a sigil, you are also training the thing that gets worse when the tally is long.

Expectancy, measured against your own baseline

Here is the item that complicates the tidy account. In trials where participants are told directly that what they are receiving contains nothing active, reported outcomes still shift. Not on every measure, not for everyone, and not by much, but the effect survives being called out in advance, which is the fact that a belief-only story cannot accommodate.

Read carefully, the finding is not that people lied to themselves. It is that a settled context changes reporting. Ritual supplies a settled context: a known shape, a known duration, a known beginning. Whatever the mechanism, it operates on the report, and the report is a real thing that then feeds back into what you do next. The subject of this finding is still the practitioner.

The order, and why changing it changes the reading

Baseline, then deck, then expectancy, then model. The deck comes second because its result is fixed and it calibrates your sense of what chance looks like when counted. Expectancy comes third because it only means something against the baseline you took first. The model comes last because it has no measurements of its own and will fit whatever the previous three produced.

Reorder these and the readings change without any of the data changing. Put the model first and the bodily finding becomes a prediction the model made. Put the deck last and a run of ordinary scores becomes a thing that happened after a change in the framework, which is how people end up believing a deck confirmed a theory. The order is doing argumentative work, and the order is a choice rather than a fact.

Where the welding shows up in ordinary conversation

It shows up in the phrases people use. Something touched me. The cards showed me. My body knew before I did. Each of those collapses two or three of the four into a single statement with a subject that has been quietly changed, and the change of subject is what makes the conversation unfalsifiable. A statement about your own body can be checked against a sensor. A statement about what touched you cannot be checked against anything.

You do not need to police your speech. You do need to notice, when you catch yourself using one of those constructions, that the sentence has quietly promoted a finding about your own physiology into a claim about an outside agency. That promotion is the entire disagreement, and it usually happens in a clause.

A sequence you could hand to somebody else

One aim you can check. Two weeks of matched sensor readings, morning and evening, with sleep notes. A hundred deck calls with the tally written down beforehand. One fixed-sequence sitting of ten minutes, twice a day, with a note on what you noticed. A record page with four columns, the last of which is what you would have done anyway. Nothing else. No framework reading until the fourteen days are finished.

That is the whole protocol and it fits in an evening to set up. Its virtue is that it can be run by someone who has no interest in the question at all, and its result is a set of numbers and notes that does not require you to believe anything about the deck or the model. Run it on a boring fortnight, when you have nothing else going on, and the readings will tell you more than any argument will.

The one result that would change the picture

A repeat of the deck result above the chance line, at a sample size where the interval is narrow, produced by someone with no stake in the outcome and a protocol fixed in advance. That would be a different situation from everything else on this list, and it is worth being clear about why rather than waving at it.

Even then, the most it would establish is that guessing above chance is possible, which is a claim about a deck and not about ritual, a body, or a world. The genre's leap goes straight from a deck result to an external intelligence, and that leap is not licensed by the deck. Keeping the four apart is what stops a genuine future finding from being wasted on the wrong argument.

Keeping the four apart after you have measured them

After a fortnight you will have four sets of data that do not agree and should not agree, because they are about different subjects. One is about your body and has a direction. One is a floor that did not move. One is about your reporting and is small. One is a model with no numbers attached. Write them as four separate paragraphs with four separate subjects, and resist the sentence that joins them.

That is the whole discipline. Not a conclusion, not a position, not a defence of the practice. Four paragraphs, four subjects, and an honest note on each. It is a much smaller outcome than the genre promises and a much more useful one, because in six months you will be able to say exactly what your practice did, which is a sentence almost nobody in this material can currently say.

The line the four findings draw

The line runs between the practitioner and everything else. Three of the four findings are on your side of it, and the strongest of those is a measurable change in your own physiology produced by a structure you chose. The fourth is arithmetic. Not one of the four, and not any combination of them, tells you that anything outside you responded.

Drawing the line is not scepticism and it is not a rejection of practice. It is a description of where the evidence is, offered by someone whose practice depends on knowing which parts of it are load-bearing. A body that settles is enough to build a life on. It is not enough to build a cosmology on, and the genre's persistent trouble is that it keeps trying.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why separate a finding about your body from a finding about a deck?

Because they have different subjects, different evidential status, and different ways of being wrong, and combining them lets each one borrow credibility from the others. A change in your own breathing is a repeatable physiological fact with a known size. A card guess is an event with a fixed rate and no interpretation in it. Written as separate paragraphs they are both usable. Written as one sentence they are a claim that cannot be evaluated by anything.

What does the expectancy finding actually establish?

That reported outcomes can shift even when participants are told directly that what they are receiving is inert. That complicates a story in which belief is the necessary ingredient, since the participants were not believing a mechanism was active. The subject remains the reporting, and reporting feeds back into behaviour, so the finding is about a practitioner rather than about the world. It is real, it is small, and it is not evidence that anything responded.

Why take a baseline before testing anything?

Because expectancy claims are only interpretable relative to a starting point, and most people skip the starting point and then compare a good fortnight to a bad one. Fourteen days of matched readings, same device, same wrist, same hour, with a sleep note, gives you a number with a direction. That direction may be real or it may be nothing, and without the baseline you have no way of telling which is which.

Does a good deck result prove anything about ritual?

No, and this is worth holding onto even if a strong result ever appears. A sustained above-chance result at a narrow interval would establish that guessing above chance is possible, which is a claim about a pack of cards and a person's attention. It would not establish anything about ritual, about the body, or about an external intelligence, because those are separate subjects that a deck result does not speak to. Keeping them separate is what stops a real finding being spent on the wrong argument.

Is there a version of this that does not feel like damage to the practice?

Yes, and it is a matter of what you were building. If the practice was a way of getting your breathing to settle and your evenings to have a shape, three of these four findings are directly in support of it and you have gained a way of knowing whether it is working. What you have lost is the option of making claims about the outside world, and those claims were not load-bearing for anything you were doing at ten minutes a day.

Put this into practice

Most of the claims on this page are about attention, expectancy and measurement, and the three are easier to test on yourself than to argue about.
