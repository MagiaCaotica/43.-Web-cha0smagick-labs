Sigils: Theory and Technique

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 9 min read

Almost every serious working in this tradition funnels through a glyph, and almost every disagreement between practitioners turns out to be a disagreement about what the glyph is doing. One camp treats a sigil as a compressed instruction that the mind executes on autopilot. Another treats it as a symbol that concentrates attention until the operator's state changes. A third holds that the mark has no independent power at all and functions purely as a mnemonic device for organised intention. All three positions can be held at once, and the technique is easier to learn once you stop picking a side. This page sets out the mechanics: how a statement of intent is reduced to a form, what the visual conventions in the grimoire literature actually encode, why encoding and charging are kept as separate operations, and how a generated glyph relates to a drawn one. The tables below are meant to be used while you work rather than admired afterwards.

What a Sigil Is Doing Mechanically

A sigil takes a sentence you would otherwise have to hold in working memory and puts it into a form that costs almost nothing to hold. That is the whole trick, and it is a real one. Verbalising a target before each repetition of a long working is cognitively expensive; you either forget the wording, drift the wording, or spend attention on the wording instead of the work. A glyph moves the specification out of the loop. What remains in the loop is the single thing you actually meant.

The consequence is that a sigil improves throughput and consistency rather than adding energy. It does not make the intention stronger. It makes the intention more repeatable, which is a different and more useful property. Someone who writes their target as forty loose words and repeats it gets a different target each time. Someone who reduces it to a stable glyph gets the same target every time, and only then can they ask whether the target produces anything.

This is why the encoding step matters more than the drawing step. Encoding is where the ambiguity gets removed. If you cannot state your target in a single sentence without a second clause, you do not yet have a target. Reducing that tangled sentence to a form will not fix the tangle; it will hide it.

Letter Reduction and Its Documented Source

The reduction method has a traceable origin. Austin Osman Spare's A Book of Gates, printed in 1904, presents a procedure for reducing a statement of intention down to a glyph by taking the initial letters, and then treating the first and last letters of the whole phrase as boundaries. That is the ancestor of the letter-drop technique most practitioners use now, and the reason for a rule that surprises beginners: you do not simply take first letters from start to finish.

The mechanics as usually taught run something like this. Write the target as a single declarative sentence. Mark the first and last letter of the entire phrase, then the first and last letter of each internal word. That produces a string which is then reduced further by dropping vowels, collapsing repeated pairs, and folding characters that share a common axis. The last surviving character stands in the centre and closes the figure.

Two cautions sit alongside the mechanics. First, several early printed versions of the reduction tables disagree with one another, and there is no single authoritative table. Different tables produce different glyphs from the same sentence. Pick one source, write it down, and use it consistently; consistency across your own practice matters far more than matching anyone else. Second, the reduction is not magic. It is a compression scheme. Knowing that does not reduce its usefulness, it only stops you from attributing the compression step itself to the sigil.

Reading the Visual Conventions

The symbolic vocabulary of sigils is small and more or less stable across the grimoire literature, even where the glosses disagree. A circle, usually at the top or enclosing the whole figure, stands for unity, totality, or the self. A single descending line stands for the element of intent, and is treated as the operator's will entering the diagram. A cross or a central intersection marks the point where the intention lands. A horizontal bar alone tends to read as a boundary, an edge, or a refusal.

Elements get added by convention. Four quartering lines around the centre stand for the four classical elements, and get invoked when the working is elemental rather than planetary. Numbers written into the interior are used for planetary or angelic hours. A hook or downward curve at the top tends to be read as a descent of influence, and an upward stroke as an ascent of intent, although this is one of the more contested readings in the literature.

The honest position on these glosses is that they function as a shared vocabulary. Two practitioners using the same convention for a boundary are more likely to mean the same operation than two using different conventions, and that coordination is worth something in a practice that often happens alone at odd hours. The conventions are not a code with a single correct decryption. Treat them as a compression scheme with a cultural history, and record which gloss you used whenever you write the gloss down.

The Two-Stage Separation

Every serious method keeps encoding and charging apart, and the separation is structural rather than incidental. First you reduce the statement and produce the glyph. Then, at a different time, you charge the glyph. Collapsing the two stages is the most common practical error among people who have been doing this for a few months, and it has a specific reason: once you have just written the glyph, you are at peak attention on it, and it is tempting to do both things in one sitting.

The problem with the combined session is that the emotional charge of composition contaminates the encoding. You write the glyph while still holding the full unsqueezed statement in your head, so the glyph inherits the vagueness of the statement. Separating the stages forces a gap in which the statement has to survive without the glyph, and that gap is where you discover whether the statement was actually clear. Plenty of glyphs get written during a composition session and abandoned a day later because the target turns out to be a wish rather than an instruction.

A workable default is a day between the two stages at minimum, longer if the target is emotionally charged. Write the glyph, close the notebook, and come back. The second session does not need to be long. It needs to be boring enough that you are executing rather than composing.

Drawn Glyphs and Generated Ones

A generated sigil and a hand-drawn sigil differ in ways that are easy to overstate in either direction. The generator is deterministic: the same sentence and the same reduction table produce the same glyph every time, which is exactly what you want for a target you intend to keep working. The hand-drawn version is a record of a specific moment, including the state you were in while you drew it, and it is not reproducible from the sentence alone unless you also reproduce the hand.

The practical difference shows up after months. If you plan to work a target over a long period, reproducibility is worth more than the trace of the original session, and generated glyphs have the edge. If a working is short and its importance lies in having been made by you in a particular state, the drawn version carries information the generated one discards, because it encodes the hand as well as the sentence.

There is a middle route that gets both. Generate the glyph, then redraw it by hand without looking at the screen. You get a reproducible target and a hand-marked record, and the redrawing takes under a minute. If the two differ in more than a few strokes, that is worth noticing, though it is not evidence of anything on its own. Most generators also let you adjust circle count, line direction, and whether to include a border, and those three controls cover almost all the variation that traditional sources actually specify.

A Table of Failure Modes

The table lists what goes wrong, how the failure presents, and the cheapest repair.

  1. Target stated as a mood. The glyg encodes a state you might experience, so nothing is actionable. Repair: restate as something you could count or observe within a fixed window.
  2. Both stages run in one sitting. The glyph carries the charge of composition and is hard to read the next day. Repair: separate them, and if it has happened, re-encode from the sentence rather than reusing the glyph.
  3. Reduction table changed mid-practice. Glyphs stop matching and the notebook becomes unreadable. Repair: write the table's source and version on the first page and never change it for a target you are still working.
  4. Glyph redrawn freehand every session. The target drifts. Repair: keep one master copy and trace it rather than redrawing from memory.
  5. Charging treated as a performance. Nothing is recorded, nothing is counted, and the working cannot be evaluated. Repair: log the date, duration, and the predicted observable before you start.

Where the Belief Comes In, Honestly

The interesting question is not whether belief is involved, since some amount of expectancy is present in almost any deliberate activity. It is what belief is doing mechanically. Three roles are separable and worth separating in your notes. Expectancy changes perception, so you will notice the thing you were looking for more often. Attention is directed by the glyph, so your time is spent on the target rather than distributed across whatever was nearest. Commitment is expressed by acting, so the working costs you something in effort and inconvenience.

The first role is the one most exposed to the usual errors. Anyone who has looked for a result in a fixed window with no pre-registered prediction has a poor signal-to-noise ratio, and improving expectancy will not fix that. The second and third roles are the ones with the least to do with magic in the narrow sense. A glyph that makes you spend twenty focused minutes on a single question is doing something regardless of what you believe about it.

The most useful habit is to write the prediction down before the charging, with a date, and to log every attempt. Not to catch yourself believing, but to catch yourself being unmeasurable. Everything else about the sigil is negotiable; that habit is not.

A Compact Reference Card

Reduce the statement. Mark first and last letter of the phrase and of each internal word. Drop vowels, collapse doubles, fold on shared axes. Place the surviving centre character as the cross at the middle. Draw the circle, then the intent line, then whatever the convention you use requires. Close the notebook. Come back later and charge what you made, not what you intended.

The four things to record for every sigil: the exact sentence, the table and source you reduced with, the date of encoding, and the date and observable for charging. Those four lines are what turn a pile of drawings into a working record, and they take ten seconds to write at the moment you already understand the working. Six months later you will not remember.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the reduction method matter, or can I draw the glyph any way I like?

The shape of the glyph is much less important than consistency. Reduction gives you a repeatable procedure, which is what you need if you plan to work a target for months. Drawing freely works too, provided the mark stays stable across sessions. What fails is a fresh shape each time, because then the mark is no longer the target and each attempt is really a new working. Pick one procedure and stay with it.

How long should I wait between writing the glyph and charging it?

A day is the minimum that still does real work, and a week is better for anything emotionally loaded. The gap is not superstition about the mark settling; it is the only interval in which you can find out whether the sentence survived without the picture. If you come back after the gap and cannot restate the target in plain words, the encoding was not finished.

Is a sigil from an app weaker than one I draw by hand?

Neither is weaker in any way that can be demonstrated. The generated mark is reproducible and identical every time, which is an advantage for long workings. A hand-drawn mark records the state you were in while drawing it, which is an advantage for short workings where the making itself carried weight. The redraw-the-generated-glyph route gives you both, and there is no tradition that treats it as a lesser form.

What do I do when a sigil does not produce the stated result?

Check three things in order before adding anything to the working. First, whether the original statement named something observable, because a mood cannot fail or succeed. Second, whether the same glyph was used every time, since a drifting mark means the attempts were not comparable. Third, whether you logged a prediction before starting. If all three hold, the working was sound and the target was simply not the one that moved; pick a more specific target rather than a stronger charge.

Can I put a sigil on an object and forget about it?

Yes, and a great many people do. Treat the object as the persistent anchor for a target you have already charged, not as an independent charge. If the object is going to sit in a pocket, the mark needs to be legible at a glance or small enough not to be intrusive, and the target statement should be written down somewhere you will not lose it, because the object will outlive your memory of why it was made.
