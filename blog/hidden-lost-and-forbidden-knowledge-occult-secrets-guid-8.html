Hidden, Lost and Forbidden Knowledge

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 8 min read

A year of reading in this field produces a very specific kind of disappointment. The texts advertised as suppressed turn out to be in print, the texts advertised as ancient turn out to have a copyright page from the last century, and the genuinely old material is mostly boring administrative fragments that nobody has turned into a compelling practice. This is an account of that year rather than a list of treasures, because the list is available everywhere and almost none of it tells you what happened next.

What a Reading Log Is Actually For

The useful artefact of a year spent reading in this field is not a collection of favourites. It is a record of which books changed what you did, which ones changed nothing, and what you were doing at the time. Those three columns turn out to be very hard to write honestly and extremely easy to write retrospectively in a self-flattering way, which is exactly the property a practice log needs.

The second job of a reading log is to keep you from restarting. People in this field re-read introductions instead of practising, and they do it because an introduction feels like progress and produces no results to argue with. A log that records only finished books and stated intentions makes that loop visible, because the gap between the two columns is the thing you were not doing.

The Enoch Problem Is a Transmission Problem

The Book of Enoch is the clearest case of a text being old and excluded at the same time. Fragments survive among the Dead Sea Scrolls, and several church fathers cite it as scripture, which means the exclusion was a decision made by a specific council for reasons that are documented and arguable rather than mysterious. It is one of the oldest surviving pieces of Jewish apocalyptic literature in any form.

What the popular presentation leaves out is the reason it does not function as a practice manual. Enoch is a heavenly travel narrative, a book of instructions for bringing divine figures to earth, written in a form that assumes its reader already holds a considerable theological framework. There is no protocol in it. What there is, if you want it, is a set of names and a cosmology. Reading it as a working method is a projection; reading it as a source document for later methods is a legitimate use.

A Cheap Book Beat an Expensive One

The most-used entry point into modern practice is a short book from 1977 that costs almost nothing, and the reason is not that it is the best text available. It is that it contains instructions. Liber Null and psi is built as a sequence of short experiments a reader performs rather than a system of doctrine to be absorbed, and that structure is the reason it survives contact with people who have ten minutes rather than ten years.

The expensive books fail for a predictable reason. A long book about a tradition requires you to hold a lot of uninterpreted material in mind while you work out what to do, and most people abandon it in chapter four. A short book that ends with something you did yesterday is a better teacher. Nobody recommends Liber Null enthusiastically. It is simply the only thing most practitioners have actually used.

The Attribution That Does Not Survive Contact

One book in this field is usually recommended first and should be recommended last, if at all. The Kybalion appeared in 1908 with no author named and a claim of transmission from a sixteenth-century occult order. The claim does not hold up: the manuscript trail leads nowhere, the transcription details do not match the framing, and the movement it claims to belong to was not organised in the way described.

That does not make the text worthless, and this is where most criticism of it is too quick. The seven principles are a usable vocabulary for talking about mental influence, polarisation and correspondence, and a lot of writers who never mention the attribution use the ideas. The recommendation is to read it as an early twentieth century synthesis of Hermeticist themes rather than as a recovered document, and to stop treating its provenance as an argument for its claims.

Plurality Is the Point, Not a Defect

The most expensive mistake in self-directed study is the hunt for one book that settles it. People spend a year looking for the volume that explains everything, and the reason it is not found is structural rather than unlucky. The material is an accumulation of distinct lineages with incompatible commitments, and no single volume can reconcile them without misrepresenting at least one.

A workable substitute is a small set covering different functions rather than different opinions. One short practical book, one readable history, one introduction to a single divinatory system, and one source text read for pleasure rather than for use. Four books, four jobs. That is enough to start, and it is enough to keep going for a year without the search spiral that derails most self-taught readers.

Fiction Is Not a Grimoire

The Necronomicon appears constantly in lists of forbidden books and has never existed outside fiction. It was invented for a series of horror stories, its title is a coinage rather than a translation, and its content is a set of invented deities, invented books and invented history arranged to be unsettling. Nothing in it is a source for anything.

This is worth stating plainly because the conflation is commercial. A fictional artefact with a resonant title can be sold, illustrated and described as authentic, and the descriptive apparatus around it is often real scholarship about a text that is not there. Reading the scholarship is genuinely informative. Reading it as evidence that the object exists is a category error that costs money.

The Failure List

Four texts consumed an unreasonable amount of time and produced nothing. A modern reissue of an eighteenth-century collection that turned out to be a later compilation presented as older. A popular series whose central technique is impossible to perform as described. A translation with errors severe enough to invert instructions. And a handbook whose author openly disowned the practices in it by the time of the second edition.

The disowning case is the most instructive, because it is normal. Practitioners revise. What matters is whether they say so, and whether the revision reached the editions being read. A book whose author has publicly abandoned its central claims is a historical document, and reading it as a manual is reading a superseded document. Nobody warned you about this because the failure is not in the book.

Reading a Source Text Properly

Source texts have a specific shape and reading them as instruction manuals is the second most common error after the single-book hunt. They are long, repetitive, uneven in quality, and frequently stitched from earlier material by a compiler. They were not written to be read in an evening, and the parts that feel like instructions are usually the parts a later editor added to make the text usable.

A workable approach treats a source text as a corpus. Read a section, write down what the author claims, and note what kind of claim it is - historical, cosmological, or operational. Then read a different section and see whether the same claim recurs. Repetition is evidence of importance within the text's own world. Discrepancy is evidence about the compiler, and is often more interesting than the material itself.

Four Questions Before You Start a Text

Before committing time, ask four questions. What is this book's job: practice, history, doctrine, or entertainment. Who is it for, stated plainly rather than implied. What would I have to already understand. And what would count as evidence that it was wrong. The fourth question is the one that filters the most material, and it is the one nobody asks because it is uncomfortable to apply to something a stranger recommended enthusiastically.

If a book cannot answer the fourth question, it may still be worth reading for the first three purposes. Entertainment is a legitimate use of an evening. It stops being legitimate when a book is sold as the thing that changes your life and then makes no claim that could come out false.

A Year of Reading, Condensed

Of roughly thirty texts started, eleven finished, four changed a practice, and one was still being read a year later. The one that survived is a short practical book. The four that changed a practice were a history, a source text read slowly, and two introductions to systems I had previously judged pointless. Everything that promised a complete system contributed nothing.

The condensed version is unglamorous and it is the whole result. Read less, finish more, keep the log, distrust the single volume that claims to explain everything, and check whether the author still stands behind the book. That is a year of work expressed as four instructions, and applying them would have saved roughly eight months of the twelve.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I tell whether a grimoire is authentic?

You usually cannot, and the inability is a normal feature of the field rather than a failing in your method. Very few grimoires carry a clear chain of transmission, and many were assembled by compilers who obscured their sources deliberately. The workable substitute is a provenance check: find out who printed it, when, from what, and whether anyone has ever disputed that. Where all four are untraceable, treat the text as an artefact of its own period rather than as a recovered document.

Is the Kybalion genuine Hermeticism?

Its claims to provenance do not survive checking, and it should be read as an early twentieth century compilation of Hermeticist themes rather than as a recovered sixteenth century document. That assessment does not make it useless. The seven principles gave a generation of writers a compact vocabulary for mental influence, polarisation and correspondence, and most of them use those ideas without ever citing it. Read the content, discount the provenance claim, and move on.

Which book should someone start with?

A short practical one, a readable history, and a proper introduction to a single system you will actually use. That combination is enough for a year and it covers the three things a beginner is short of: something to do, some context, and one tool worth learning properly. No single volume covers the field, and the search for one is a reliable way to spend a year reading introductions and practising nothing.

Why do the same books get recommended to everyone?

Because the recommendation lists were built by people who had a small number of formative texts and described that set as the field. The repetition is partly genuine signal, since a book that has survived fifty years of readers is worth something, and partly an artefact of how recommendations propagate. Treat a widely recommended title as a starting point rather than as a verdict on what exists, and check whether the recommender read anything outside their own tradition.

Is it worth reading the real source texts at all?

Worth it, but only after you have something you are doing. Source texts are long, repetitive and often compiled rather than authored, and reading one without a practice gives you a great deal of material and nothing to apply it to. Read them for vocabulary and for the names and cosmologies that later systems took as given, and be suspicious of any modern writer who lifts a passage out of its surrounding chapter and presents it as a step by step method.
