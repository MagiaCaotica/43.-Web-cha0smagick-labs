The Seventy-Two Spirits of Goetia, Read Properly

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 6 min read

The Goetia is the best-structured entity catalogue in Western grimoire literature, and it is almost always read in the worst possible way. Seventy-two spirits, three ranks, an office and a set of powers for each, compiled from Jewish and Christian demonological material that reaches Europe through the Pseudepigrapha Book of Enoch, the seventeenth-century Conjuratio Ben-Maimon and the Lemuria. In the original scheme these were administrative and disciplinary officials, described in terms of office and jurisdiction, not torturers of the innocent. Everything that made them frightening in the popular imagination is a twentieth and twenty-first century addition. What follows is the catalogue read as what it is, which is a tool index with a filing system.

The Three Ranks

Nine Presidents, thirty-six Dukes, thirty Kings. The ranks are a functional hierarchy rather than a threat scale, and the grouping into families of two, three or four matters more than the rank titles for practical work.

A President is described as a general. A Duke governs. A King rules. Those descriptions come from the administrative vocabulary of a world where spirits were modelled on the civil service of a large empire, which is exactly what the sources are doing.

The rank tells you how much effort the classical authors thought the operation required. It is a hint about historical practice, not a statement about power, and treating it as the latter is the most common misreading in the whole field.

Where the Material Comes From

The material descends from Jewish and Christian demonological sources, particularly the Pseudepigrapha Book of Enoch, and arrives in the grimoire lineage through the Lemuria and the Conjuratio Ben-Maimon of the seventeenth century. Understanding that chain explains almost every oddity in the text.

The Book of Enoch is apocalyptic literature, not demonology. The figures it names are not devils in a later sense; they are the angels who, in that text, did not abandon their appointed order and were consequently consigned to darkness. Everything downstream inherits that framing, including the assumption that these beings are ex-angels with former duties.

Which means the whole catalogue is best understood as a tradition of authority without jurisdiction. An ex-angel who is not doing the job has authority and no assignment. That is a much more usable model than a demon, and it explains why the offices are specific and the threat is not.

Read the Office, Not the Reputation

Each entry gives a rank, a visible form, an office and specific powers. The office is the functional content, and it is the part the popular version drops. An entry saying a spirit grants riches or teaches a craft is telling you what the operation is for.

Mammon is the spirit of wealth, which is a straightforward entry. Clauneck is a duke said to grant riches, along with other goods. Paimon grants dignities and confirmations of favour, and is described as being especially dangerous to the magician's enemies, which is a line worth reading as a description of the office rather than a warning.

The general rule that prevents most mistakes: open the source, find the stated office, and confirm it matches the domain you need. If it does not, choose a different name. A mismatch is not a message about your readiness. It is a filing error.

The Nine Presidents

Bael, Agares, Vassago, Samigina, Marbas, Valefor, Aamon, Belphegor, Saleos. That is the complete list of Presidents, and having it in order removes most of the ambiguity that comes from lists published out of sequence.

Their described roles range widely. Marbas is given mastery of arts and sciences and a specific eye for discoverers of hidden things. Vassago teaches the arts of philosophy and the sciences, and is noted as solitary and not appearing unless invoked. Samigina appears as a count, presides over games and matters of chance, and is described as visible in the form of a great count.

Notice how few of these entries resemble the popular image. No cruelty, no torture, no menace. The catalogue is a filing system, and reading it as a horror prop produces both errors and pointless fear.

The Brand Is Recent

Goetia as a media category, a set of video essays and app listings and commentary channels, is a twenty-first century phenomenon layered over the older text. The layering is not fraudulent, it is just layered, and the interesting question is what each layer added.

The older layer gave structure: ranks, offices, families, forms. The commercial layer gave imagery: monstrous forms rendered as character art, ranks recast as a difficulty scale, offices reduced to a one-line summary for a thumbnail.

What got lost in the compression is exactly the useful part. The office is what lets you select a spirit, and a summary too short to contain an office cannot be used for selection. This is the same problem the popular framing creates for grimoires in general: brevity for marketing, unusable for work.

Using the Catalogue Without Harm

Step two is the whole discipline. Reputation-based selection is how people end up working with a name whose stated domain has nothing to do with their problem, and then interpreting the useless result as a message.

Step three takes a few minutes and prevents the most common procedural error in this field, which is working with an entity you have never researched. There is no evidence of external danger here. The danger is procedural, and reading removes it entirely.

  1. Write the domain first, as a sentence with no spirit in it.
  2. Search by office rather than by reputation, and open at least one source you can actually cite.
  3. Read the rank, the form and any conditions the entry states. Do not skip to the powers.
  4. Build the operation as a sigil with a stated frame, and record the frame in writing.
  5. Close with food, water and daylight, and write the entry the same day.

Families and Pairings

Beyond rank, the seventy-two are grouped into families of two, three or four, and the grouping is often the more useful unit because it shows which entries were designed to operate together.

Some families share a theme, some share a form, and a few appear to have been assembled for administrative tidiness rather than any deeper relation. None of that requires a theory to use. Selecting a family and working through it in order is a perfectly good structure, and it is far more practical than picking names on reputation.

Where the Office Is Silent

Plenty of entries state a domain without stating a method, and the honest response to those is to accept the limitation rather than to fill the gap with a story. A spirit listed as teaching or ruling gives you a direction, not a procedure.

This is where the modern commentary does the most damage, because a summary written for a thumbnail will confidently supply a procedure the source never mentioned. Cross-reference two sources and prefer the one that sounds less dramatic. The more specific and less exciting the claim, the closer it usually sits to the text.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are the Goetia spirits demons in any real sense?

Historically they are entries in a demonological catalogue, with no evidence of agency outside the page. The category word arrives through a specific textual chain, and the figures at the head of it are ex-angels from apocalyptic literature rather than devils in the later popular sense. Practically, the catalogue is a rich symbolic vocabulary with a filing system, and it is genuinely more useful read that way than as a horror list.

Which spirit is associated with money?

Mammon is the spirit of wealth and is the most direct answer. Clauneck is a duke said to grant riches along with other goods, and Paimon grants dignities and confirmations of favour, which is a different and vaguer kind of benefit. Always read the stated office in the source text rather than relying on a summary, because the summaries are where the accuracy problems live. Once you have read the office, choosing between these three is a matter of which description matches the operation you actually want.

Which nine are the Presidents?

Bael, Agares, Vassago, Samigina, Marbas, Valefor, Aamon, Belphegor and Saleos. Marbas covers arts and sciences, Samigina presides over games and matters of chance, and Vassago is described as solitary and unwilling to appear unless properly invoked. Knowing the roles is more useful than memorising the names, and the roles are where the selection work is done.

Is the catalogue dangerous to engage with?

Not through any external agency, and the realistic risks are ordinary. The one worth naming is working with a name you have not researched, which leaves you without its stated office and any conditions the source mentions. The second is escalating intensity, which produces fixation, narrowing social contact and disrupted sleep. Short daily practice with a written record avoids both, and so does reading the entry before you begin.

Is it worth paying for a Goetia app or book?

The older text has been in print for three centuries, so you are not buying information, and the useful part of the catalogue, the offices and the ranks, is what gets cut when it is compressed for a screen. What is genuinely hard to do well is cross-referencing a large catalogue against your own records of what you attempted and what happened, and that is the part worth paying for if anything is. I would own a tool that indexes a catalogue once rather than rent access to a list I could read for free, because the subscription buys me a summary I could not use anyway.
