Dreams, Astral Projection and Tulpas

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 27, 2026 ⬢ 10 min read

Most dream practice gets assembled in the wrong order. People buy a technique for leaving the body, practise it for a month, get nothing, and conclude the whole field is nonsense. The order is the problem, not the technique. Of everything in this domain, one item has a measurable population base behind it and reliable induction methods: lucid dreaming. Out-of-body experiences are reported by a small minority, cluster near sleep onset and in illness and meditation, and do not come on demand. Remote viewing did not replicate reliably, although training genuinely does improve the visual description of a target. The tulpa does not come from Western esotericism at all; it arrives from Tibetan Buddhist practice, where it is a tool for visualisation and for the yoga of deities, not the manufacture of a separate being. A sequence that starts with the one part that works and refuses to treat the other three as the same kind of claim is a different exercise from the one usually offered. This is that sequence, in the order it actually runs, with the point at which it stalls and the two things the record shows when you stop adding to it.

The sequence, and why recall comes first

Most sequences in this area open with the dramatic part, and that guarantees you will judge the whole practice by the hardest thing to do. The order that holds together starts somewhere duller: can you remember a dream at all when you wake. That is a question with a measurable answer, and if the answer is no, then everything after it is built on an unverified floor. Memory of dreams improves quickly with recording, and the improvement is usually the largest single change in the first fortnight. It is also the only part of the sequence you can confirm within eight hours of the fact happening.

So the first stage is not a technique for lucid dreaming. It is a practice of capture, and its output is three lines a night. The point of the ordering is that a later stage depends on a substrate you have to build first, and building it costs a fortnight of boredom that most people will not spend if the opening item promises transcendence.

A night-time practice that fits a real week

A practice that needs forty minutes nightly will not survive a working month. The version that fits is deliberately small: a paper by the bed, a pen that works without light, and three lines written the moment you are awake enough to hold a pen. The pen matters more than it sounds, because a torch or a phone screen costs you the first minutes of recall, which is where the load is. What you write is not interpretation and not meaning. It is the location, a single image, and whether you were reading or dreaming.

The third line is the load-bearing one. Distinguishing whether you were reading and dreaming is a skill, it is trainable, and it is the same skill the later stages depend on. People with years of ordinary dream recall are usually faster at it than people who have read extensively about lucid dreaming, and that is a direct argument for putting recall first rather than third.

The three checks at the moment of waking

Three questions, asked while you are still in the transition, are the difference between a record and a guess. Was the world strange? Could I read text, and did it stay put? Did I have a body, and did I feel its weight and position? These are not mystical criteria. They are perceptual, they have been used as diagnostic markers in laboratory settings, and they can be checked while you are still half asleep without needing a method.

They also give you a discipline about interpretation. A vivid dream that fails all three checks is a vivid dream. A vague one that passes them is a candidate. Building the record around the checks rather than around how much the dream moved you is the single change that most improves what you learn from four weeks of practice.

Writing three lines before anything else

Three lines: where I was, one image, and the verdict on those checks. Nothing else. This sounds severe and it is, because every extra field is a field that gets filled in with reconstruction later. A dream diary with a column for how you felt about the dream will, within a month, be a diary of your mood with some dreams in it. A record with no interpretation column can be read honestly six months afterwards, which is the only time it is worth reading.

The three lines also make the practice finishable at eight lines a week, which is the number that decides whether it survives. When the format is small enough to do while half awake, people do it. When it asks for fifteen minutes of narration, it quietly stops, and the sequence loses its substrate before stage two ever begins.

The induction, and the window the morning gives

Only after two weeks of clean capture do the induction methods earn their place, and the timing matters more than most of them. REM periods lengthen toward morning, so a practice that targets the last hour of sleep sits in the part of the night where lucid experience is most likely to be had and most likely to be remembered. Waking yourself at a fixed early hour, holding a single simple thought for twenty minutes, and then returning to sleep is unglamorous and has a better track record than the elaborate alarms-on-a-string constructions.

The thought you hold has to be short and has to be yours. A long complicated intention collapses under the drowsy conditions of the induction; a single phrase survives. This is worth knowing because the most common reason an induction fails is not that the method is wrong but that the thought it required was three sentences long and got dropped the moment attention thinned.

What a first lucid dream looks like from the inside

Almost nobody recognises the first one as it happens. The usual report is that the dream continued normally, something small felt odd, and the realisation arrived afterwards, sometimes with a sense of having missed an obvious cue. A recurring version is reading text that will not hold still, or light behaving slightly wrong, or looking down and finding the hands wrong. The presence of that oddity is the marker, and it is why the three checks from the earlier stage are worth training.

Second lucid dreams are the ones people remember, because they are the first ones where the realisation arrives in time to do something. The useful description of the experience is not that you escaped anything. It is that a layer you had been treating as the only one turned out to be a layer, and that this was noticed in a state in which it had never been noticed before. That is the whole content, and it is a modest, repeatable finding rather than an event.

Why projection is a later step

Out-of-body experience sits in a different evidentiary category, and treating it as the next rung of the same ladder is the mistake that makes the practice collapse. It is reported by a small percentage of people, it clusters near the onset of sleep and in illness and in long contemplative practice, and it does not reliably arrive on request. Nobody has an induction technique for it. What people have is falling asleep, and the experiences that sometimes occur at that seam.

So the sequence puts it after the parts that are verified, and frames it as something to record rather than something to achieve. If it happens, the record helps: where, what the transition felt like, how long before you were certain. If it does not happen, nothing downstream fails, because nothing downstream was ever built on it. This ordering is the difference between a practice with a long tail and a practice with a cliff.

The bilocation framing, and what it claims

Bilocation describes the experience of being in two places at once, and the framing is usually sold as a technique: you concentrate on being elsewhere and you go. As a description it is unobjectionable and matches a class of reported experience. As a mechanism it asserts something the evidence does not support, because the state described is precisely the state in which self-report is least reliable, and there is no independent measurement of where a person was.

The honest version of the framing is that bilocation names a family of subjective states and does not tell you what produces them. That is a smaller claim and it is defensible. It also removes the pressure to verify, which is what makes the framing dangerous: a practitioner who has staked a worldview on the claim will find ways of experiencing that confirm it, and the ways are easy to come by when you are half asleep and looking.

Where the sequence stalls

It stalls at the first stage, in most cases, and the reason is not laziness in the usual sense. It is that recording three lines a night produces no sensation, and sensations are what people join practices for. The capture phase is where the payoff is absent and the input is highest, and most people read that as evidence the method is not working when it is evidence that it is working.

The second stall is at the transition from capture to induction, and it has a clear signature: the person who has beautiful dreams and a full diary and no lucid experiences at all. What is missing is usually the check habit, not the dream. The third is the person who has lucid experiences and then wants projection, and cannot get it, and concludes the whole structure was fraud. The sequence exists precisely so that these three stalls are predictable rather than interpretable as failure.

Four weeks, and what each one is for

Week one is capture only, and the goal is three lines a night without an interpretation column. Week two is the same, and the goal is enough entries that you can see a pattern in the writing rather than in the dreams. Week three introduces the morning induction and the three checks as a formal part of waking. Week four is where the first lucid dreams cluster for most people, and the job that week is only to log them, not to extend them.

The reason for making the phases explicit is that without them the practice collapses into a single undifferentiated effort that either feels like it is working or does not, and there is no way to tell which part is the problem. Four weeks is also short enough to finish. The characteristic abandonment point for anyone going further is week six, and it is worth building a rest week into the design rather than discovering the wall at it.

The two things the record shows

When you stop adding to the record and read it, two things tend to be visible. The first is that dream recall is now a habit rather than a skill, and the entries cluster in themes you would not have predicted from the outside, which is a finding about the record-keeping and only weakly a finding about dreams. The second is that the lucid experiences cluster in a small number of locations and situations, usually the same room, the same hour, or the same problem.

Neither of those is evidence that the practice controls dreams. Both of them are evidence that the practice gives you a stable observer, and a stable observer is the only thing any of this field has ever actually required. Anyone who keeps the record can see them. Anyone who stops keeping it is guessing, and cannot tell the difference between a quiet month and a month they were not paying attention.

Stopping before it becomes an obsession

The practice carries a specific risk that the promotional material never mentions: the three-line record, kept faithfully, gradually becomes the most reliable source of self-knowledge available, and that is corrosive. Dream journals have a documented history of producing hypochondria, because they supply an endless supply of material for interpreting whatever is already feared, and the material is never contradictory.

The safeguard is to keep the record for a fixed period and then read it once, in full, as a document about your weeks rather than as a series of omens. The second safeguard is to refuse interpretation entirely during the capture phase, since interpretation is what converts a diary into a worry list. And the third is to keep the entry count small enough that you stop when your life stops, rather than pushing on because the format is cheap.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is lucid dreaming actually real, or is it just a trick of waking up?

It is a well-documented phenomenon with a measurable population base, and induction techniques with repeatable success rates have been demonstrated in laboratory conditions. The interesting question is not whether it happens, which it does, but what it consists of, which is thinner than the popular account suggests: a noticed layer rather than an escape from one.

Can I learn to have out-of-body experiences on demand?

No reliable method has been demonstrated, and the experiences that do occur cluster near sleep onset, in illness, and in extended contemplative practice rather than under instruction. Treat any technique offered for this as something to record rather than something to achieve, and keep the parts of your practice that are verified independent of it.

How long should I keep a dream diary before doing anything else?

Two weeks of clean capture, with no interpretation column, is the minimum that makes a record readable. The reason is that the diary's first job is to establish that you can remember dreams at all, and until that is settled, the quality of the record cannot be distinguished from the reliability of the memory.

What is the difference between a tulpa and a servitor?

The tulpa comes from Tibetan Buddhist practice, where it is a tool for visualisation and for the yoga of deities rather than a separate being you manufacture. A servitor comes from nineteenth and twentieth century ceremonial literature and is framed as a displaced part of the self given a task. The origins are different and the vocabularies should not be merged.

My dreams stopped being interesting after I started recording them. Is that normal?

Yes, and it is a good sign rather than a bad one. Once the record exists, vividness stops being the interesting variable, because content is what you can measure and texture was never evidence of anything. The shift in what feels interesting is usually the moment the practice becomes usable rather than escapable.
