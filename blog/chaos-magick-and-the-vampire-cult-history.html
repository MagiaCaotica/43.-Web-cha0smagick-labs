Chaos Magick and the Vampire Cult: Where the Image Came From

By Frater Alek0s ⬢ September 26, 2026 ⬢ 7 min read

The image is everywhere. The pale practitioner in black, the pentagram laptop, the trench coat, the line about having been this way longer than you have. It reads like a lineage and it is not one. It is a convergence that took about forty years, happened mostly in Britain, involved at least four groups who did not know about each other, and was assembled after the fact by people who found the pieces fit. The history is more interesting than the image and it explains several things about the practice that the image hides.

Vampirism as an identity, long before it was a costume

The vampire in the west has been a disease, a curse and a condition for a very long time, and for most of that time the person carrying it had no choice in the matter and no community to go to. What changed in the twentieth century is that it became a self-description. The 1970s gothic scene, the post-punk interest in outsider identities, and later the internet made it possible to be a vampire as a preference rather than as a fate, and that shift is the origin of everything the modern image depends on.

Crucially this identity is not satanic. It is an identity of liminality, of being awake at the wrong hours, of a body that runs on a different schedule. That description fits a great many people who have never done anything occult at all, which is precisely why it travels so well and why it fuses so easily with any practice that promises a different kind of wakefulness. When it does fuse, the result is usually a costume worn over a real appetite, and the appetite is the part worth keeping.

The punk occult, which is where the word chaos was actually used first

Between roughly 1970 and 1980 a group called the Temple ov Psychick Youth fused ritual, sex, altered states, music and a wholesale refusal of the mainstream into a single practice, and it did so deliberately and publicly. Genesis P-Orridge and Cosey Fanni Tutti were at the centre, and the name William Bennett uses, the(OTo), was an acronym for a post-industrial group he founded in New York whose material drew on both this scene and the ceremonial tradition.

The important point for the history of chaos magick is chronological. The word chaos was in wide use among British punk and industrial musicians and their audience years before chaos magick was noticed by the same people, and the punk usage meant something specific: chaos as a stance toward the culture, the refusal of a social order, sometimes the subversion of its rituals. Carroll's usage came from an older sense, closer to Empedocles, meaning the entire domain of what is not governed by a declared order. Same word, two projects, and the popular image merges them because both attract the same people.

What Carroll deliberately left out

Carroll came from a formal initiatory tradition and he left it, and the reasons he gave were structural. An initiatory structure reproduces the gatekeeping the material was supposed to remove, and a lineage you inherit is a claim you cannot check. He published Liber Null in 1972 with no sexual material, no atmosphere, and no community, and the tone of every subsequent branch of the tradition descends from those three omissions. The punk scene had the opposite profile: intense, communal, and full of atmosphere.

This is the pivot of the whole story. Two practices formed in the same decade in the same country, drawing on overlapping sources, and one of them chose to be reproducible and the other chose to be intense. The reproducible one is what became the international thing with a hundred-page manual at its centre. The intense one had a shorter and louder life, and it ended in litigation rather than in a canon. Neither of them is the vampire image, but the vampire image is much closer to the second than to the first.

The parts that went wrong, which are documented

TOPY collapsed in 1980 in a court case concerning child abuse, and the case is part of the record rather than something to be argued around. Separately, the case brought against the Ordo Templi Orientis in Britain between 1986 and 1988 concerned allegations of ritual abuse, and it was followed by the Honest History Project, which compiled a substantial amount of primary material. Whatever one concludes about the rites, the pattern that emerges is about design: practices involving other people with no consent structure inside them eventually find someone who did not agree.

The reason this belongs in an article about a vampire image is that both scenes were young, urban, media-literate and heavily invested in being a chosen identity, which is the exact combination the pattern requires. The failure was not a secret rite. It was the absence of a written specification, which is the same absence that the whole of chaos magick's later design work was aimed at filling. The tradition that went quiet has a specification sheet. The scenes that burned had a vibe.

What the two traditions genuinely share

The real overlap is functional, and that is the part that survives. Both are identity technologies for people who feel misfit, and both do the same two jobs: they take a private strangeness that has no name and give it a name, and they convert it into a practice with a structure. The vampire frame is a diagnosis and the magic is the treatment plan. Neither of those is frivolous and neither requires any supernatural claim to be useful.

What they do not share is a lineage, and this is the error the image makes most often. There is no documented chain from a ceremonial order through a punk scene into a chaos magick convention, and there is no practice taught by one and passed to the other. The relatedness is a shared appeal to people with a particular kind of insomnia, which is a real thing and a human thing, and it is not a transmission of anything.

The internet finished the merger

The 1990s and 2000s did what the earlier scenes could not. Forum culture, image boards and eventually video made a thirty-year-old oral history searchable in a way it had never been, and the pieces were found next to each other. The pentagram laptop, which is the single most recognisable piece of the modern image, has a specific origin in stage imagery rather than in any tradition, and it is a costume before it is a symbol. What the internet did was make the costume portable and then let it accumulate a meaning it was never built to carry.

There is a real cost to this. A practice that arrives as an aesthetic is very hard to learn from, because the aesthetic contains no method. Most of the confusion in the popular material comes from people who have acquired the visual language of a lineage without the instructions of a practice, and the instructions are the part that does the work. If you took the imagery out of the history entirely you would lose nothing operational and gain the ability to learn something.

Five ways people get the history wrong

The first is retrospective continuity, reading the modern image backwards into 1970s practice and finding a tradition that was never there. The second is the costume failure, which is the most common outcome and is worth naming plainly: the practice becomes an outfit, the outfit becomes the practice, and something that was a way of paying attention turns into a way of being seen. The third is identity foreclosure, which is the serious version of the second, and it happens when a practice is taken to be the whole of a person rather than a thing that person does.

The fourth is borrowing the mythology wholesale. A vampire is not a demon, has no grimoire entry, and carries obligations in folklore that have nothing to do with anything, so the vampire-behemoth-einzelne-freunde material is a different creature tradition entirely. The fifth is claiming a lineage you do not have, which is the most common thing in the popular scene and the one the historical record most clearly does not support.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a vampire tradition in chaos magick?

No. There is no documented chain from any ceremonial order through the punk scene into chaos magick, and no practice taught by one and passed to the other. What exists is a shared appeal: both are ways of naming a private strangeness and giving it a structure, which is a real overlap without being a transmission.

What is the Temple ov Psychick Youth?

A group founded in 1969 by Genesis P-Orridge and Cosey Fanni Tutti that fused ritual, sex, altered states and music in an explicit refusal of the mainstream, distributing its ideas through zines, tapes and later the internet. It collapsed in 1980 in a court case concerning child abuse. It matters here because the word chaos was in common use in that scene before chaos magick was noticed by the same people.

Why does chaos magick not have the sexual or ceremonial elements people expect?

Because the founder deliberately left them out. He had broken with the one organisation he belonged to and gave structural reasons for building something with no initiatory gate and no lineage, on the grounds that both reproduce the exclusivity the material was meant to remove. The absence is an editorial decision rather than a tested conclusion, and the reason has less to do with the rites than with the design.

Is the pentagram laptop a real occult symbol?

No, it is stage imagery that acquired a meaning by circulation. It has no grimoire entry, no historical use in any tradition, and it entered the scene through performance rather than practice. It is a good example of how a visual shorthand can be transmitted far more efficiently than a method, and how the two then get confused.

What should I take from the punk scene?

The refusal of a social order, which is a legitimate stance and the thing the word chaos originally meant there, and the practice of treating a technique as worth building a scene around. Not the secrecy, which is what made the material unfalsifiable and what the litigation exposed, and not the borrowing of occult material as atmosphere without any method behind it.
