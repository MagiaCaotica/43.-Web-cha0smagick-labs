What an ephemeris is (and why it decides the app)

Every astrology app is a thin interface over an ephemeris: a table of planetary positions for a given moment. The two that matter are the Swiss Ephemeris, developed by Astrodienst and used by most professional software, and NASA's JPL DE numerical integrations, exposed publicly through Horizons. The differences are measured in arcseconds for the modern planets and can matter at the boundary of a sign or a house cusp. An app that computes positions with a simplified formula will be right most of the time and wrong exactly when the chart is interesting — at a cusp, a retrograde station, or a Moon ingress.

This is the first question to ask of any app: does it name its ephemeris? "Accurate astrology" means a specific dataset with a known precision.

Tropical or sidereal: the first fork in the road

Western tropical astrology measures from the equinox and keeps the signs fixed to the seasons. Sidereal astrology measures against the fixed stars and applies an ayanamsa (the Lahiri ayanamsa is the most common) which currently shifts the zodiac by roughly 24 degrees. The same birth moment can produce a different Sun sign under each system. Neither is wrong: they answer different questions. The problem is an app that will not say which one it uses, or that mixes a sidereal ayanamsa with tropical house tables.

Houses: Placidus, Koch, whole sign and the argument nobody wins

House systems divide the sky between the ascendant and the midheaven. Placidus is the default in most Western software, Koch is a variation popular in some schools, and whole-sign houses — one sign per house from the ascendant — is the older Hellenistic method and the standard in much traditional and Vedic practice. At high latitudes Placidus can break down, which is why serious apps offer a fallback. If an app only offers one system and does not say which, that is a reason to look elsewhere.

Time zones, daylight saving and the error that ruins charts

The most common cause of a wrong chart is the clock, not the ephemeris. A birth time recorded during a wartime double-summer-time, a country that changed its UTC offset, or a hospital that rounded to the nearest half hour will produce a wrong ascendant and wrong house cusps. Good apps convert a local time to UTC using a historical time-zone database, and they let you enter the offset manually when the database disagrees with the birth certificate. An app that only accepts "current" offsets is unusable for anyone born before 1970.

What the 12 apps in this comparison do

We scored the apps on four things that can be checked from the listing and the app itself: whether the ephemeris is named, whether the ayanamsa and house system are selectable, whether the time-zone conversion is historical, and whether the birth data leaves the device. A birth date, time and place is enough to identify a person; an app that uploads it has a data-protection obligation, and an app that keeps it offline does not.

What astrology can and cannot claim

The 1985 Carlson double-blind test, published in Nature, asked astrologers to match natal charts to psychological profiles and found no effect above chance. That result is why astrology is classified as pseudoscience in the academic literature. This does not make the practice worthless: people use charts to reflect and to talk about themselves, and that use is real. It does mean an app should not be sold as a prediction engine, and that the honest framing is symbolic, not causal.

The Barnum effect — the tendency to accept vague descriptions as personally accurate — explains most of the "accuracy" users report. A good app can work with that by being specific and falsifiable: exact degrees, exact aspects, so the user can check rather than nod.
